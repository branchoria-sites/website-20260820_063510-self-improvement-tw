## Introduction

Self-monitoring is one of the better-supported ideas in the self-improvement toolkit, but the evidence points to a more specific lesson than simply “track your goals”. Monitoring tends to work better when progress leaves a visible trace: a written log, recorded measurement, step count or other record that can be reviewed. Effects have also been larger when progress is reported to somebody else rather than kept entirely private.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_evidence_quality_ef04cc_visible_progress_tra_29ec5e-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Visible Tracking illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_evidence_quality_ef04cc_visible_progress_tra_29ec5e-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_evidence_quality_ef04cc_visible_progress_tra_29ec5e-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

The strongest broad evidence comes from a meta-analysis of 138 randomised studies involving 19,951 participants. Interventions designed to increase progress monitoring produced a moderate improvement in goal attainment, with an average effect of *d* = 0.40. Crucially, the benefit was larger when monitoring information was physically recorded and when outcomes were reported or made public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span>

That makes visible tracking a useful example of why implementation matters in evidence-based self-improvement. The intervention is not merely “pay attention to your goal”. A record can make discrepancies difficult to forget, while reporting can add accountability. Neither mechanism, however, means that more tracking is always better.

## What randomised studies found about self-monitoring

Benjamin Harkin and colleagues' 2016 *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis provides unusually broad experimental evidence because it restricted its main analysis to studies in which participants were randomly allocated to an intervention promoting progress monitoring or to a control condition. The 138 studies covered nearly 20,000 participants and a variety of goals, although health-related goals were particularly prominent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span>

The interventions had a very large effect on how frequently people monitored progress (*d* = 1.98, 95% confidence interval 1.71 to 2.24), but the effect on actually attaining goals was substantially smaller: *d* = 0.40 (95% CI 0.32 to 0.48). Changes in monitoring frequency statistically mediated the improvement in goal attainment. In plain terms, interventions successfully got people to check their progress much more often, and this increased monitoring was associated with a meaningful but considerably more modest improvement in achieving the goal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. A tracking system can be extremely successful at generating entries, check-ins or measurements without producing an equally dramatic change in the outcome that ultimately matters. The evidence supports monitoring as an aid to goal pursuit, not as a guarantee of success.

The meta-analysis also produced a particularly useful implementation finding. Monitoring in private had an estimated effect of *d* = 0.19 on goal attainment, whereas monitoring in public was associated with *d* = 0.55 and reporting monitoring information with *d* = 0.47. Physically recording the information was associated with an effect of *d* = 0.43, compared with *d* = 0.29 when information was not recorded. When goal attainment itself was measured objectively, the corresponding recorded-versus-unrecorded estimates were *d* = 0.57 versus *d* = 0.23.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://1library.net/document/rz3x7o7z-does-monitoring-progress-promote-attainment-analysis-experimental-evidence.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: 1library.net">[Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">1library.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence...</span></span></span>

These are moderator findings across sets of studies, rather than clean estimates from one enormous experiment independently randomising people to “visible” and “invisible” tracking. They should therefore be interpreted more cautiously than the overall randomised comparison between monitoring interventions and controls. The authors themselves called for direct experiments comparing reported with unreported monitoring to establish the mechanisms more firmly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul%20harkin%20raw%20final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Why a visible record may be more useful than a mental check

The basic self-regulation logic is straightforward. Monitoring supplies information about the difference between the present state and a desired state. If the target is 8,000 steps and the display says 4,300 late in the afternoon, the discrepancy can prompt an adjustment. Without sufficiently frequent [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}), people may simply fail to notice that behaviour is drifting away from the target. The Harkin meta-analysis supports this feedback-loop account because interventions that changed monitoring frequency subsequently improved goal attainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span>

Recording adds something beyond noticing the number in the moment. Harkin and colleagues proposed that a physical record may strengthen the encoding and later retrieval of progress information. A diary, spreadsheet, calendar mark or stored digital reading also gives the person something that can be examined across time. It becomes harder for yesterday's shortfall—or improvement—to disappear from memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul%20harkin%20raw%20final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

A record can therefore turn an impression such as “I have been exercising quite regularly” into a sequence that can be inspected. The useful feature is not necessarily paper rather than pixels: the relevant distinction is between transient awareness and information that is actually recorded and available for review. Indeed, [physical-activity]({{ 'physical-capacity/' | relative_url }}) monitors provide a particularly concrete modern version of this feedback loop.

A 2022 *BMJ* systematic review of 121 randomised controlled trials involving 16,743 participants found that physical-activity-monitor interventions that provided feedback increased overall physical activity by a standardised mean difference of 0.42, equivalent in the authors' analysis to roughly 1,235 additional steps per day. The certainty of evidence differed by outcome, and the authors cautioned that publication bias might mean the physical-activity effect was overestimated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bmj.com/content/376/bmj-2021-068047" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bmj.com">[BMJ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bmj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">bmj 2021 068047</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">bmj 2021 068047</span></span></span>

A separate review of step-count monitoring found 70 randomised trials and reported increases of about 1,126 steps per day at four months or less and 464 at one year in its preferred change-from-baseline analysis. The estimated effect was much less certain at two years, illustrating a recurring problem with tracking interventions: an immediately visible number can change behaviour without establishing that the same benefit will persist indefinitely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33036635/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## Public reporting adds accountability, but the mechanism is not settled

Making progress visible to somebody else changes the intervention again. A private spreadsheet primarily supplies information to the person pursuing the goal. A weekly report to a coach, group or other observer adds a social audience.

Harkin and colleagues proposed several possible reasons why public or reported monitoring might strengthen goal pursuit: greater public commitment, accountability, concern about how one's performance is presented to others, and—in experiments—the possibility of demand effects, where participants alter behaviour because they infer what researchers expect. Their meta-analysis established the moderator pattern, but it did not establish which of these explanations causes the stronger effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul%20harkin%20raw%20final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This distinction guards against turning “public tracking works better” into another oversized self-help rule. Posting every goal on social media is not what the evidence demonstrates. Reporting can be as simple as submitting step counts, recording symptoms for review, or having measurements reviewed within a structured programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.collegesidekick.com/study-docs/28285561" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: collegesidekick.com">[College Sidekick]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">collegesidekick.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">study docs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">study docs</span></span></span>

More recent evidence reinforces the broader idea that feedback surrounding self-monitoring can matter while also showing how uncertain the optimal design remains. A 2024 systematic review examined 19 randomised studies with 3,261 participants comparing forms of feedback around self-monitoring of diet, physical activity or weight. For physical activity, feedback produced a small additional benefit over self-monitoring without feedback (*d* = 0.29, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.43). But results for other outcomes and feedback formats were mixed, and 10 of the 19 studies were judged at high overall risk of bias. The reviewers concluded that there was not yet enough evidence to say which forms of feedback or presentation are best.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-023-01555-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on doi.org.</span></span></span>

The practical distinction is therefore between **making progress inspectable** and assuming that any extra social or technological layer will improve it. The first has broad experimental support; the best way to engineer the second remains much less certain.

## Track the measure that corresponds to the goal

Visibility is useful only if the visible number represents something worth changing. One of the more practical findings in the Harkin review was that monitoring behaviour and monitoring outcomes did not produce interchangeable effects.

Across the relevant comparisons, monitoring behaviour had a reliable effect on behavioural performance (*d* = 0.45) but no reliable effect on outcomes (*d* = 0.14). Monitoring outcomes showed the reverse pattern: a medium-to-large effect on outcomes (*d* = 0.62), but no reliable effect on behaviour (*d* = 0.17).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://1library.net/document/rz3x7o7z-does-monitoring-progress-promote-attainment-analysis-experimental-evidence.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: 1library.net">[Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">1library.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence...</span></span></span>

This gives visible tracking an important design rule. If the objective is to establish a behaviour, record the behaviour: sessions completed, pages written, minutes practised or another direct measure. If the objective is an outcome, an outcome measure may be informative, although outcomes can often be influenced by factors beyond the person's immediate control.

The American Psychological Association's summary of the research gives the health example succinctly: monitoring food intake is more directly aligned with changing diet, whereas monitoring body weight is aligned with a weight outcome.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals?AreaStudy=COMM&amp;Program=CSTC&amp;abtestactive=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apa.org">[American Psychological Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apa.org.</span></span></span> The same logic generalises beyond health. Counting hours at a desk is a poor progress indicator if the real goal is completed work; recording books purchased says little about reading; and maintaining a long habit streak can become misleading if the underlying behaviour has become perfunctory.

The point is not to find the most impressive metric. It is to make the relevant progress—or lack of it—visible enough to guide the [next action]({{ 'next-action/' | relative_url }}).

## Where tracking becomes excessive or misleading

The evidence does not support the stronger claim that adding ever more dashboards, devices and measurements will keep improving results. Self-monitoring is an intervention component, and more elaborate monitoring can fail to outperform a simpler system.

The IDEA randomised clinical trial provides a useful counterexample to technological enthusiasm. It enrolled 471 young adults in a behavioural weight-loss programme. After six months, one group used a study website to self-monitor diet and physical activity, while another received a wearable device and web interface. At 24 months, estimated weight loss was **5.9 kg in the standard group but only 3.5 kg in the wearable-technology group**, a statistically significant difference favouring the simpler standard intervention. Both groups improved physical activity, fitness and diet, without significant between-group differences on those measures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2553448" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jamanetwork.com">[JAMA Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jamanetwork.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jamanetwork.com.</span></span></span>

That trial does not show that wearables generally make people worse at achieving goals; large reviews of physical-activity monitors find benefits on average.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bmj.com/content/376/bmj-2021-068047" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bmj.com">[BMJ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bmj.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">bmj 2021 068047</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">bmj 2021 068047</span></span></span> It shows something narrower and more useful: a more sophisticated visible-tracking device is not automatically an improvement over an existing self-monitoring system.

There is also a human cost to consider. People sometimes avoid progress information precisely because they expect it to be unpleasant, demotivating or to demand an unwanted response. Research on avoidance of goal-progress information has identified perceived effort, anticipated negative emotions, doubts about the value or accuracy of information and concern about what the information will require them to do as reasons people stop checking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5297323/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> A systematic review of 67 empirical studies on health-related self-tracking similarly identified discontinuation and rejection as significant themes and highlighted adverse psychosocial consequences as an area needing more research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8493454/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The risks also depend on what is being tracked and by whom. A meta-analysis of self-weighing found no overall association with affect, body-related attitudes or disordered eating, although it found a small negative association with broader psychological functioning and evidence that effects differed according to participant and study characteristics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4917920/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> That is another reason not to universalise a tracking prescription from [average effects]({{ 'average-effects/' | relative_url }}).

## The strongest practical version of the claim

The evidence supports a restrained version of “what gets measured gets managed”. Deliberately increasing progress monitoring improves goal attainment on average, and the largest broad randomised evidence base suggests that monitoring works better when information is recorded and when progress is reported or made public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span>

For self-improvement, that translates into a fairly simple implementation pattern:

* **Choose a measure closely matched to the goal.** Track the behaviour when changing the behaviour is the objective, rather than an attractive but weakly related proxy.
* **Leave a record.** A written tick, logged session, stored measurement or automatically recorded step count creates information that can be revisited rather than merely noticed and forgotten.
* **Make the record easy to compare with a target.** Visibility is most useful when it reveals whether action is on course and makes adjustment possible.
* **Use reporting selectively.** A coach, training partner, group or other appropriate observer can add accountability, but the evidence does not require broadcasting goals widely.
* **Keep the system lighter than the behaviour it serves.** A tracking ritual that consumes excessive time, produces distress or becomes the goal in its own right has stopped functioning as [useful feedback]({{ 'useful-feedback/' | relative_url }}).

Visible progress tracking is therefore best understood as **feedback infrastructure**, not motivation theatre. Its value lies in repeatedly confronting intention with evidence: what was actually done, what changed, and how far that leaves the person from the target. Randomised research gives that principle credible support. It also puts a sensible ceiling on the claim: recording and reporting can improve the odds of follow-through, but a streak, graph, wearable or public commitment is only useful insofar as it helps produce the behaviour or outcome the goal was meant to achieve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul%20harkin%20raw%20final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>
