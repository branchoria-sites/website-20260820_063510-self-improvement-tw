## Introduction

Another person seeing your progress can improve follow-through because it changes monitoring from a purely private act into one with social consequences. Instead of simply noticing whether you did what you intended, you know that the result will be visible to somebody else. Experimental evidence suggests that this distinction matters.

The strongest broad evidence comes from a meta-analysis of 138 experimental studies involving 19,951 participants. Interventions that increased progress monitoring improved goal attainment overall, but the effects were larger when monitoring results were **reported to another person or made public** than when they remained private. Importantly, reporting to just one other person was enough to show this pattern; the evidence does not imply that broadcasting progress publicly is necessary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? ## From private monitoring to socially consequential monitoring

Progress monitoring already performs an important self-regulatory function. It tells you where your behaviour or outcome currently stands relative to where you want it to be. In the 2016 meta-analysis led by Benjamin Harkin and Thomas Webb, interventions designed to increase monitoring produced a very large increase in monitoring frequency, with an average effect size of *d* = 1.98. More importantly for actual goal pursuit, they produced a smaller but meaningful improvement in goal attainment, *d* = 0.40. Changes in monitoring frequency statistically mediated the improvement in attainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Social visibility appears to strengthen this basic [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) loop. The researchers distinguished three forms of monitoring: progress monitored publicly; progress monitored privately and then reported to at least one other person; and progress monitored privately without being reported. Their example of the middle category was deliberately ordinary: a person could weigh themselves privately and then telephone a research assistant with the result.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That distinction matters because it separates **being accountable to somebody** from **performing a goal in front of an audience**. A private spreadsheet sent to a coach, a weekly step count given to a friend or a completed-session total reported to a colleague can all make progress socially visible without making it broadly public.

Why might that alter behaviour? Harkin and colleagues proposed several possible explanations, including public commitment, personal accountability and concerns about how one's performance will appear to others. The common feature is that the information no longer exists solely inside the person's private self-assessment. Someone else can now observe whether actions and intentions match.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This interpretation fits the broader idea of **supportive accountability** developed by David Mohr and colleagues. Their model defines accountability around an expectation that a person may have to account for actions or inaction. It argues that human support is particularly useful when expectations are clear and process-focused and when the person receiving progress information is regarded as trustworthy, benevolent and credible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jmir.org/2011/1/E30/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jmir.org">[JMIR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jmir.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Medical Internet Research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Medical Internet Research - Supportive Accountability: A Model for Providing Human Support to Enhance Adherence to eHealth...</span></span></span>

The qualification is important. The social element is not simply “pressure”. Visibility can create a reason to keep tracking and acting, while the other person's role can make that information consequential. The supportive-accountability model explicitly cautions against framing monitoring as threatening surveillance; it instead emphasises agreed expectations and a relationship that provides benefits to the person pursuing the goal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jmir.org/2011/1/E30/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jmir.org">[JMIR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jmir.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Medical Internet Research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Medical Internet Research - Supportive Accountability: A Model for Providing Human Support to Enhance Adherence to eHealth...</span></span></span>

## What the progress-monitoring meta-analysis found

The most revealing result for social accountability lies in the meta-analysis's moderator analysis. When progress was monitored privately and **not reported**, the estimated effect on goal attainment was *d* = 0.19. When monitoring was conducted privately but **reported to at least one other person**, the effect was *d* = 0.47. Public monitoring produced an estimated *d* = 0.55.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Those figures should not be treated as though the researchers ran one giant experiment randomly assigning nearly 20,000 people to private, reported and public tracking. They did not. These are subgroup estimates across different experiments whose interventions had different characteristics. The underlying studies were also highly heterogeneous, and the authors noted that better-quality studies generally tended to produce smaller effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

So the evidence supports a careful conclusion: **progress-monitoring interventions tended to work better when the resulting information became visible to someone else**. It does not establish that visibility itself accounts for the entire difference between the subgroup effect sizes.

That caution is particularly important for the public-monitoring result. Only three studies, representing roughly 214 participants in the goal-attainment analysis, fell into the public category. By contrast, the private-but-reported estimate drew on 95 studies and more than 12,000 participants. The evidence base is therefore much stronger for the ordinary act of reporting progress than for conspicuously pursuing a goal in front of a group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The authors themselves called for experiments directly comparing reported and unreported monitoring so researchers could identify whether mechanisms such as accountability, public commitment or presentational concerns actually explain the advantage. In other words, the meta-analysis identifies a strong and useful pattern, but it does not settle exactly why social visibility works.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Another result reinforces the general idea that progress becomes more consequential when it leaves the realm of fleeting private thought. Physically recording monitoring information was associated with a larger goal-attainment effect (*d* = 0.43) than not recording it (*d* = 0.29). The authors suggested that records may make progress easier to remember and harder to ignore or reinterpret.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Taken together, these findings suggest a progression from weak to stronger feedback: merely intending to improve provides little information; monitoring creates feedback; recording preserves that feedback; and reporting it gives the feedback a social audience.

## A direct test of someone else seeing the record

More recent experimental research offers a particularly clean example of this principle. A 2024 study in *Obesity* randomly assigned 322 adults taking part in a 12-week behavioural weight-loss programme to conditions that differed in whether their coaches could access their digital self-monitoring data. Everyone was asked to track weight, [physical activity]({{ 'physical-capacity/' | relative_url }}) and food intake. In the “Coach Share ON” condition, coaches could regularly view information from participants' wireless scales, activity sensors and food records through an online portal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39358838/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coach access to digital self-monitoring data: an experimental test of short-term effects in behavioral weight-loss treatment - PubM...</span></span></span>

This is useful evidence because the crucial difference was not whether participants possessed tracking technology: both groups did. The manipulation concerned whether the person providing support could actually **see what had been tracked**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537830/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Coach access to digital self-monitoring data: An experimental test of short-term effects in behavioral weight loss tr...</span></span></span>

After 12 weeks, participants whose coaches had access to their records had lost an average 6.2% of their initial weight, compared with 5.3% among those whose coaches could not access the data. Dietary self-monitoring was also more consistent when coaches could see it: 98.05% versus 93.51% of days. Activity self-monitoring differed more modestly, at 98.70% versus 97.40% of days. Participants in the data-sharing condition also reported significantly greater supportive accountability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39358838/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coach access to digital self-monitoring data: an experimental test of short-term effects in behavioral weight-loss treatment - PubM...</span></span></span>

Not everything changed. Actual measured physical activity did not significantly differ between groups, nor did programme attendance, working alliance with coaches or frequency of weight self-monitoring. That is informative because it argues against a simplistic interpretation in which coach visibility automatically improves every behaviour. Instead, making records visible produced selective improvements in monitoring and a modest improvement in the primary outcome.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39358838/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coach access to digital self-monitoring data: an experimental test of short-term effects in behavioral weight-loss treatment - PubM...</span></span></span>

The study also addresses an obvious concern about accountability: perhaps knowing that a coach can inspect your records makes the relationship uncomfortable and encourages avoidance. The researchers found no significant deterioration in working alliance, attendance or treatment satisfaction. Within this particular programme and population, making data visible increased perceived accountability without detectable damage to those measures of engagement or rapport.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537830/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Coach access to digital self-monitoring data: An experimental test of short-term effects in behavioral weight loss tr...</span></span></span>

There are still limits to what this experiment proves. Coaches with access to detailed records could potentially provide better-informed feedback, goal-setting and problem-solving, so the effect cannot automatically be attributed entirely to the participant's feeling of being observed. The authors themselves identify both supportive accountability and better-informed coaching as plausible explanations. The study was also short-term and conducted within a structured behavioural weight-management programme, so its precise effect sizes should not be generalised to every self-improvement goal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11537830/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Coach access to digital self-monitoring data: An experimental test of short-term effects in behavioral weight loss tr...</span></span></span>

Even with those qualifications, it provides unusually concrete support for the core idea: **holding the same sort of progress data is not necessarily equivalent to knowing that another person can see it**.

## Why visibility does not require public posting

The practical implication of this evidence is narrower than “tell everyone your goals”. The strongest dataset does not show that a large audience is necessary. In Harkin and colleagues' analysis, privately collected progress that was subsequently reported to **at least one person** was associated with substantially better goal-attainment effects than private, unreported monitoring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That means social visibility can remain discreet. Someone trying to establish a regular [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) habit might record completed sessions and send the weekly total to one friend. Someone studying could tell a tutor or colleague how many planned sessions were actually completed. The relevant change is not the size of the audience but the transition from “only I will see whether this happened” to “another person will also have access to the result”.

This also explains why **reporting progress is different from announcing an intention**. The evidence reviewed by Harkin and colleagues concerns monitoring information generated during goal pursuit: steps taken, weight measured, behaviours recorded, symptoms logged or other indicators of actual progress. What becomes socially visible is evidence about performance, not merely an aspirational statement made before any work has occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

For self-improvement, that distinction is valuable. An accountability arrangement does not need followers, a public streak or continual encouragement. It needs a sufficiently clear measure that another person can see whether the intended action occurred. The best-supported version is therefore quite modest: **track something meaningful, preserve the result and make that result visible to someone whose involvement feels legitimate and supportive**. The evidence suggests that this small shift from private monitoring to socially visible monitoring can strengthen the connection between intention and follow-through, while leaving plenty of room for privacy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/8/3_PDFsam_Does%20monitoring%20goal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>
