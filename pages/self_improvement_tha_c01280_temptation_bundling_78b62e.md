## Introduction

Temptation bundling is a way to make a beneficial but effortful activity more attractive now rather than relying on its distant rewards. The basic rule is simple: pair something you should do, such as exercising, with something you genuinely want to do, such as listening to an absorbing audiobook, and reserve the enjoyable activity for the useful one. Instead of asking future health, fitness or productivity benefits to outweigh immediate effort, the bundle supplies an immediate reason to begin. Researchers describe this as a response to “present bias”: the tendency for immediate costs and pleasures to carry disproportionate weight when people make choices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

The best direct evidence comes from [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) experiments. An early university study found large initial increases in gym attendance when participants could access tempting audiobooks only at the gym, although the effect weakened over time and was disrupted sharply by a holiday break. A later field experiment involving thousands of commercial-gym members found smaller but more durable improvements. Together, the studies make temptation bundling useful but not magical: it can change the immediate experience of a difficult activity, yet good pairings must survive repetition, interruptions and the temptation to consume the reward elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25843979/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PubMed...</span></span></span>

## How temptation bundling works

Many worthwhile activities have an awkward timing problem. Their costs arrive first: exercise takes effort, studying demands concentration and tedious administration consumes attention. Their most important benefits may appear weeks, months or years later. That mismatch makes postponement attractive even when a person sincerely values the long-term outcome. Research on time preferences has found an association between greater present bias and lower levels of [physical activity]({{ 'physical-capacity/' | relative_url }}), illustrating why appeals to future health alone may struggle against the immediate price of getting started.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30567532/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Temptation bundling changes that calculation by attaching an immediate pleasure to the beneficial activity. In Katherine Milkman, Julia Minson and Kevin Volpp's original formulation, the appealing component was a “want”, while exercise was a “should”. Crucially, the two activities were consumed together. An audiobook did not arrive as a prize after ten workouts; it made the workout itself more enjoyable and created curiosity about what happened next. The researchers argued that this can produce two benefits at once: making the useful activity more tempting while confining a potentially time-consuming indulgence to periods when it accompanies something valuable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

That distinction separates temptation bundling from ordinary reward systems. “Exercise this evening and then have a treat” is a delayed reward. “Listen to this novel only while walking or exercising” is a bundle. The latter can affect both anticipation and experience: wanting the next chapter supplies a reason to start, while listening can make the session itself feel less unpleasant. In the first exercise experiment, the advantage of bundling was greater among participants who found the combined workout more enjoyable, which is consistent with that mechanism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

More broadly, studies by Kaitlin Woolley and Ayelet Fishbach help explain why this approach is plausible. Across research involving exercise, studying, healthy eating and personal goals, [immediate enjoyment]({{ 'immediate-enjoyment/' | relative_url }}) was more closely associated with persistence than delayed benefits, even though people often said they pursued those activities because of their long-term value. Other experiments found that choosing a more enjoyable form of a useful activity, or adding an immediate positive experience to it, could increase persistence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27899467/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Immediate Rewards Predict Adherence to Long-Term Goals</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Immediate Rewards Predict Adherence to Long-Term Goals - PubMed...</span></span></span>

This does not mean long-term benefits are unimportant. They help determine which goals are worth pursuing in the first place. The practical insight is that the forces that make someone choose a goal may differ from those that keep them doing it on a rainy Tuesday evening. A person can exercise because cardiovascular health matters to them while still needing something more immediate to make today's workout attractive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27899467/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Immediate Rewards Predict Adherence to Long-Term Goals</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Immediate Rewards Predict Adherence to Long-Term Goals - PubMed...</span></span></span>

## What the exercise experiments found

The first major test was conducted at a university gym in 2011 and later published in *Management Science*. Participants chose several highly engaging audio novels. In the strongest treatment, those books were loaded onto an iPod that remained in a monitored gym locker, meaning the participant could listen only while exercising there. A second group received the audiobooks on their own devices and was encouraged to impose the same restriction voluntarily. A control group received an equivalently valued bookshop gift card.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

At first, the results were striking. Participants with gym-only access to their audiobooks visited the gym 51% more often than controls, while those merely encouraged to restrict listening to workouts visited 29% more often. The experiment also found evidence that people valued the constraint itself: after the study, 61% chose to pay for the option of having continuing gym-only access to the tempting audiobooks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25843979/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PubMed...</span></span></span>

But the headline percentages are only half the story. The effects faded as the study progressed, particularly after the US Thanksgiving holiday interrupted normal routines. Milkman has subsequently described that closure and break from the programme as effectively wiping out the earlier advantage. The original experiment therefore demonstrated that a tightly enforced bundle could be powerful at first while also exposing an important weakness: a pairing that depends on one place, one device or an uninterrupted routine can be fragile.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

The voluntary condition also matters. Its smaller initial effect suggests that knowing the rule and enforcing it are not the same thing. “I will listen only at the gym” is easy to revise when the story becomes gripping and the sofa is available. The locked-away device turned the idea into a commitment mechanism: the enjoyable activity literally became unavailable elsewhere. That design feature helps explain why temptation bundling is more than simply making exercise pleasant. Restriction can preserve the motivational value of the temptation for the next workout.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

A much larger study published in 2020 tested whether the idea could work without monitored lockers. Researchers worked with 24 Hour Fitness on a four-week programme. Across the broader research programme, 6,792 participants were involved; experimental conditions included receiving a free audiobook plus explicit encouragement to [temptation bundle]({{ 'routine-breaks/' | relative_url }}), receiving the audiobook without that explanation, and comparison participants who received neither component. Gym check-in records provided an objective behavioural measure rather than relying on people's recollections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Giving participants an audiobook and encouraging them to use temptation bundling increased the probability of exercising in a given week by roughly 10–14% and average weekly workouts by about 10–12% during the programme and the follow-up period, which extended as far as 17 weeks after the intervention. These effects were less dramatic than the first experiment's initial 51% increase but were observed in a much larger, more realistic commercial-gym setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

There was also an illuminating complication. Explicitly teaching temptation bundling only modestly outperformed simply handing gym members a free audiobook. Many people who received the book without instructions apparently inferred its intended use from the exercise setting. The authors called this “information leakage”: the object itself suggested the strategy. That makes the experimental comparison less clean, but it also has a practical implication. Sometimes the easiest way to encourage a bundle is to place an appealing activity directly into the context where the beneficial behaviour occurs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Taken historically, the two experiments suggest a movement from a strong, externally enforced intervention towards a lighter, more scalable one. The 2011 design demonstrated what can happen when access to the temptation is tightly controlled. The later commercial-gym study showed that much softer implementation — a free audiobook, a suggested rule and ordinary personal devices — could still produce measurable behaviour change. The trade-off is familiar in behavioural interventions: stronger constraints may generate larger short-term effects, while simpler systems are easier to deploy widely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

## Building pairings that do not undermine the goal

The research supports a narrower lesson than “add something fun to everything difficult”. A useful bundle has to make the target activity easier to repeat without interfering with what that activity is supposed to accomplish. Audiobooks and steady-state cardio make a natural pair because listening usually leaves the body free to exercise. The same audiobook could be a poor match for learning material that requires close verbal attention. The right question is therefore not just whether the reward is enjoyable, but whether the two activities are compatible.

Several design principles follow from the evidence.

* **Choose a temptation you actually anticipate.** A mildly pleasant podcast may not change whether you leave the house. A serial, story-driven programme that creates genuine curiosity has more chance of influencing the next decision. The original researchers deliberately used engrossing audio novels for this reason.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>
* **Prefer simultaneous rewards where the activities are compatible.** Listening during a walk changes the experience of the walk immediately. This fits broader evidence showing that [enjoyment]({{ 'enjoyment/' | relative_url }}) during goal pursuit is strongly related to persistence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27899467/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Immediate Rewards Predict Adherence to Long-Term Goals</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Immediate Rewards Predict Adherence to Long-Term Goals - PubMed...</span></span></span>
* **Reserve the temptation for the target behaviour.** If the next episode, novel or favourite programme is freely available everywhere else, it loses much of its ability to pull you towards the beneficial activity. The original gym-only condition is the clearest demonstration of the value of restriction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>
* **Do not let entertainment degrade performance.** A bundle that helps someone stay on a treadmill is useful; one that fragments attention during demanding study may defeat its own purpose. When the beneficial activity requires deep cognitive processing, a better temptation may be a pleasant environment, favourite drink or other reward that does not compete for the same mental resources.
* **Make the pairing portable enough to survive disruptions.** The first exercise study's collapse after Thanksgiving is a warning against systems that work only in one narrow context. A walking audiobook that works outdoors, on a treadmill and while travelling may be more resilient than a reward locked to a single facility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/better-behavior" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apa.org">[American Psychological Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on apa.org.</span></span></span>
* **Refresh the temptation when it becomes stale.** [Immediate rewards]({{ 'immediate-rewards/' | relative_url }}) work because they are immediately rewarding. Once an audiobook becomes dull or a playlist ceases to feel special, the bundle may need replacing rather than more discipline.

There is another useful distinction: the temptation does not have to be unhealthy or guilt-inducing. The original theory emphasised “want” activities that people might otherwise overconsume, because restricting them creates a second benefit. In everyday use, however, the core mechanism only requires something immediately enjoyable. An entertaining podcast, favourite café, scenic route or particularly enjoyable form of exercise can serve the same practical purpose if it raises the immediate appeal of the beneficial activity. Research on immediate rewards supports that broader principle, although the strongest direct evidence specifically labelled “temptation bundling” remains concentrated in exercise and audiobooks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

## Where the evidence is strong — and where it is not

Temptation bundling has more direct field evidence than many catchy self-improvement ideas, but the evidence base is still fairly narrow. Two important exercise programmes support the approach, including one large-scale field experiment, and related motivation research independently supports the importance of immediate enjoyment. That makes the mechanism credible and the technique reasonable to try for activities with compatible pleasures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25843979/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PubMed...</span></span></span>

What the evidence does not establish is that any imaginable bundle will improve any beneficial behaviour, or that an initial boost automatically becomes a permanent habit. The original trial's effects decayed, and the later study found relatively modest gains rather than a transformation of exercise behaviour. Researchers studying present bias in physical activity likewise caution that immediate incentives can help initiate behaviour without necessarily guaranteeing long-term maintenance once the incentive or surrounding structure disappears.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25843979/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PubMed...</span></span></span>

The most sensible interpretation is therefore practical rather than grand. Temptation bundling is a way to redesign the immediate experience of an activity whose benefits arrive too late to motivate today's decision reliably. It is particularly well suited to behaviours such as walking, running, indoor cycling or routine chores where an enjoyable audio or visual experience can coexist with the task. Its strength is not that it eliminates [self-control]({{ 'self-control/' | relative_url }}), but that it asks self-control to do less work: instead of choosing between effort now and pleasure now, the person gets some of both at the same time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

    params.push('mkevt=1');
    params.push('mkcid=1');
    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
