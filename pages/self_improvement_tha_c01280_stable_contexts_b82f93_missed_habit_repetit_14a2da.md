## Introduction

Missing one planned repetition does **not** appear to break a developing habit or send habit formation back to day one. The best-known direct evidence comes from Phillippa Lally and colleagues’ 12-week study of everyday habit formation. When participants missed a single opportunity after several successful repetitions, the resulting change in [automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }}) was very small, and their progress resumed when they performed the behaviour again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_stable_contexts_b82f93_missed_habit_repetit_14a2da-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Missed Days illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_stable_contexts_b82f93_missed_habit_repetit_14a2da-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_stable_contexts_b82f93_missed_habit_repetit_14a2da-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

That finding matters because habit formation is better understood as **gradual learning**, not as maintaining an unbroken streak. Each repetition in a stable context can strengthen the relationship between a cue and a response. An isolated miss means one fewer learning opportunity; it does not erase the associations created by earlier repetitions. The more important distinction is between an occasional lapse and becoming sufficiently inconsistent that the cue-response pattern is no longer being reinforced reliably. Exactly how much [consistency]({{ 'consistency/' | relative_url }}) is necessary, however, remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit%20" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucl.ac.uk">[University College London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University College London How long does it take to form a habit? &#124; UCL News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University College LondonHow long does it take to form a habit? &#124; UCL News - UCL – University College LondonAugust 4, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 4, 2009</span></span></span>

## What the 12-week study found about missed opportunities

Lally and colleagues recruited 96 volunteers and asked each to choose a new health-related behaviour that could be performed once each day in response to a clear daily cue. Examples included eating fruit with lunch, drinking water with lunch and running for 15 minutes before dinner. Participants attempted the behaviour for 84 days and repeatedly rated how automatic it felt. Eighty-two participants supplied enough data for the main analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

The researchers were specifically interested in whether missing an opportunity interrupted the development of automaticity. For this analysis, they used a fairly precise definition of a miss: the participant had failed to perform the behaviour after performing it on each of the preceding three opportunities. They identified **140 such omissions among 55 participants**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://studylib.net/doc/8103714/modelling-habit-formation-in-the-real-world" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: studylib.net">[studylib.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">studylib.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Habits Form: Real-World Habit Formation Study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 16, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 16, 2009</span></span></span>

Immediately after one of these misses, participants' automaticity scores fell by an average of only **0.29 points** on the study's automaticity scale — a very small change. Where the researchers also had the necessary data after the participant resumed the behaviour, the automaticity score was actually 0.55 points higher than it had been before the omission; that increase was not statistically significant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://studylib.net/doc/8103714/modelling-habit-formation-in-the-real-world" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: studylib.net">[studylib.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">studylib.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Habits Form: Real-World Habit Formation Study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 16, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 16, 2009</span></span></span>

The researchers then compared those episodes with stretches in which participants performed the behaviour on three consecutive opportunities. Across those uninterrupted sequences, automaticity increased by an average of **0.79 points**. In other words, the increase surrounding a single missed opportunity was broadly comparable with the increase seen during uninterrupted repetition. The authors concluded that one missed opportunity did not materially affect habit formation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scispace.com/papers/how-are-habits-formed-modelling-habit-formation-in-the-real-4iq5e623zp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scispace.com">[SciSpace]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scispace.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">how are habits formed modelling habit formation in the real 4iq5e623zp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SciSpace(PDF) How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world (2010) &#124; Phillippa Lally &#124; 1734 CitationsOctober 1, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2010</span></span></span>

That conclusion is narrower than the popular claim that “missing days doesn't matter”. The study tested **isolated omissions after recent successful repetitions**. It did not establish that lengthy gaps, repeated missed opportunities or highly irregular performance are harmless. Indeed, the researchers found that more consistent performance was associated with better-fitting habit-development curves, and Lally later emphasised that very inconsistent participants did not successfully develop habits in the study.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

## Why habit growth is gradual rather than all-or-nothing

The missed-day result makes more sense once habit formation is viewed as a learning curve rather than a streak.

Lally's participants did not suddenly cross from “no habit” to “habit” on a particular day. Their reported automaticity tended to rise relatively quickly at first and then increase by progressively smaller amounts as it approached a plateau. Researchers describe this as an **asymptotic curve**: repetition continues to contribute, but each later repetition generally produces a smaller incremental change than repetitions earlier in learning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

There was also enormous variation between individuals. Among participants whose data could be modelled, estimated times to reach 95% of their eventual automaticity ranged from **18 to 254 days**. A later account of the study reports a median of about 66 days among those whose curves fitted the model. Crucially, this was not a universal deadline: the original analysis itself demonstrated that people followed markedly different trajectories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.12504" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine‐based versus time‐based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - Keller - 2021 - British Journa...</span></span></span>

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis reinforces that broader point. Across 20 studies involving 2,601 participants, the few studies that directly estimated time to habit formation reported medians of roughly 59–66 days and means of 106–154 days, with individual estimates ranging from 4 to 335 days. The review also cautioned that much of the evidence base had a high risk of bias and that relatively few studies had directly measured the time required to reach automaticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

So there is little scientific reason to interpret a missed Tuesday as “back to day one”. Automaticity is a continuously varying measure, not a counter that resets when a repetition is omitted. The cue-response learning established by previous repetitions does not appear to disappear simply because one opportunity passes without the response. Reviews of habit research describe habits as learned context-behaviour associations strengthened through repetition in particular situations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4566897/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The more defensible interpretation is therefore:

* **One isolated miss:** the Lally study found no material disruption to the trajectory of automaticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>
* **Frequent repetition:** remains important because habit learning depends on repeated opportunities to associate the response with its context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4566897/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>
* **Persistent inconsistency:** is more concerning, although research has not established a simple threshold such as “never miss twice” or “perform the behaviour at least 80% of the time”. UCL's summary of the original work explicitly says that the required level of consistency was not known.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit%20" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucl.ac.uk">[University College London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University College London How long does it take to form a habit? &#124; UCL News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University College LondonHow long does it take to form a habit? &#124; UCL News - UCL – University College LondonAugust 4, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 4, 2009</span></span></span>

This distinction prevents the evidence from being stretched too far. “One miss doesn't reset a habit” is supported by the study. “It doesn't matter how often you miss” is not.

## A missed repetition is a lost opportunity, not lost progress

A useful way to interpret a lapse is as **one learning opportunity that did not occur**. Suppose the intended pattern is: finish breakfast → take a vitamin. Performing that response repeatedly after breakfast gives the recurring situation an opportunity to become associated with the action. If Wednesday's dose is missed, Wednesday supplies no reinforcing repetition. But Monday's and Tuesday's learning has not thereby been shown to vanish.

This fits the broader theoretical account of habit formation. Repeated performance in a particular context strengthens a context-response association until encountering that context increasingly prompts the behaviour with less conscious deliberation. Habit interventions consequently focus on reliable **context-dependent repetition**, rather than perfect adherence for a predetermined number of consecutive days.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/62246/chapter-abstract/550940636" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on oup.com.</span></span></span>

Researchers Gardner, Lally and Wardle drew essentially this practical conclusion from the 12-week data: occasional omissions did not seriously impair formation because increases in automaticity resumed after performance resumed. Their recommended learning phase therefore centres on repeatedly performing the chosen behaviour in the chosen context until the association becomes more automatic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3505409/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Making health habitual: the psychology of ‘habit-formation’ and general practice - PMC...</span></span></span>

This also explains why repeated lapses should not simply be treated as multiple harmless versions of the same event. If omissions become common, there are fewer successful cue-response pairings from which automaticity can develop. The 2024 systematic review identifies repetition and context stability among the important determinants of habit development while noting that the precise consistency required remains unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## How to resume without redesigning the whole routine

After one missed repetition, the evidence gives little reason to restart a habit programme, reset a conceptual “habit clock” or compensate with extra repetitions. The most evidence-consistent response is much simpler: **perform the intended behaviour the next time the original cue occurs**.

For example, if the plan is “after breakfast, read for ten minutes” and Monday is missed, Tuesday's breakfast is another opportunity to reinforce the same relationship. Changing immediately to lunchtime, increasing the target to 20 minutes or inventing a new reminder can unnecessarily change the very cue-response pairing that is being learned.

That does not mean a routine should never be redesigned. [Repeated misses]({{ 'repeated-misses/' | relative_url }}) provide different information from an isolated lapse. If the intended cue regularly fails to produce the behaviour — perhaps the cue is easy to overlook, the action is impractical at that moment, or the context varies too much — then the problem may be the design of the routine rather than the occasional interruption of it. Habit research consistently places reliable repetition in an identifiable context at the centre of formation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23311908.2022.2041277" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tandfonline.com.</span></span></span>

For an isolated missed day, though, continuity is the more evidence-based response. The original 12-week dataset offers no support for treating one omission as a catastrophic break. UCL's own summary is similarly restrained: one missed opportunity did not significantly affect formation, whereas people who were **very inconsistent** did not succeed in forming habits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit%20" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucl.ac.uk">[University College London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University College London How long does it take to form a habit? &#124; UCL News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University College LondonHow long does it take to form a habit? &#124; UCL News - UCL – University College LondonAugust 4, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 4, 2009</span></span></span>

## The useful lesson from missed days

The strongest lesson is not that consistency is unimportant. It is that **consistency and perfection are different things**.

Habit formation appears to tolerate occasional noise. What it needs is enough repeated experience of the same response following a recognisable cue for automaticity to strengthen over time. The Lally study's 140 observed omissions provide unusually concrete evidence for that distinction: one missed opportunity produced only a tiny immediate change in automaticity and did not materially alter subsequent habit development.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://studylib.net/doc/8103714/modelling-habit-formation-in-the-real-world" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: studylib.net">[studylib.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">studylib.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How Habits Form: Real-World Habit Formation Study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 16, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 16, 2009</span></span></span>

At the same time, this result comes from one relatively small real-world study, using self-reported automaticity and mostly simple health behaviours. Later reviews regard Lally's work as foundational but also emphasise the limited number of high-quality longitudinal studies that directly track habit formation. The 2024 systematic review found substantial variation between people and behaviours and rated 11 of its 20 included studies as having a high risk of bias.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23311908.2022.2041277" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tandfonline.com.</span></span></span>

The sensible self-improvement rule is therefore more modest than either “never miss a day” or “misses don't matter”. **Do not mistake one lapse for the loss of the habit-building work already done. Resume the same cue-response pattern at the next reasonable opportunity.** What builds automaticity is the repeated pattern over time, not the preservation of a flawless streak.
