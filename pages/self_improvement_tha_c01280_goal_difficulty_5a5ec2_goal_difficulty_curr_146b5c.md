## Introduction

The right goal is not simply “hard”. It is hard **relative to you**. A target can be routine for someone who already performs near that level, a useful stretch for someone a little below it, and effectively impossible for a beginner. Research on goal setting therefore makes most practical sense when difficulty is judged against current ability, recent performance, available resources and understanding of the task—not against an impressive-looking number in isolation. A meta-analysis of 78 goal-setting studies found that both prior performance and ability were significantly related to the goals people set for themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/014920639201800309" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsMeta-Analysis of the Antecedents of Personal Goal Level and of the Antecedents and Consequences of Goal Commitment - J. C. W...</span></span></span>

For self-improvement, that points to a practical rule: use your **recent demonstrated performance as the starting point**, then choose a target that requires a credible improvement. When your performance changes, recalibrate. And when you do not yet know how to perform the task well, the problem may be skill acquisition rather than insufficient ambition.

## Why the same target can be easy, challenging or impossible

Numbers hide an important distinction between **goal level** and **[goal difficulty]({{ 'goal-difficulty/' | relative_url }})**. Goal level is the objective target: running five kilometres in 25 minutes, completing 20 press-ups, writing 1,000 words or scoring 80% on an examination. Goal difficulty is what achieving that number demands from a particular person.

Suppose three people set a target of running five kilometres in 25 minutes. One currently runs it in 24 minutes, another in 27 minutes and the third cannot yet run five kilometres continuously. They have identical targets but very different goals in practical terms. The first person has little stretch at all. The second needs a meaningful but potentially credible improvement. The third may first need to develop basic endurance before a 25-minute performance target becomes useful.

This distinction appears directly in research comparing uniform and ability-adjusted goals. In a laboratory study of 138 university students, researchers compared an “ability-based” approach with assigning everybody the same performance target. Ability-based goals produced better results overall, with particularly clear benefits for lower-ability participants; lower- and moderate-ability participants given the common target also showed larger performance declines in later rounds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01608061.2012.698116" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineThe Performance Effects of an Ability-Based Approach to Goal Assignment: Journal of Organizational Behavior Manage...</span></span></span>

The implication is not that people with lower current performance should permanently receive easier goals. It is that a stretch should start from where performance actually is. Once ability improves, the appropriate target should move with it.

That also explains why copying another person's goal can be misleading. “Read 50 books this year”, “run a sub-four-hour marathon” or “practise for two hours every day” says little about appropriate difficulty without knowing the person's starting point, relevant skills, time and constraints. Difficulty has to be calibrated rather than borrowed.

## Use recent performance to set a credible stretch

The most useful benchmark is usually not what you believe you *ought* to be capable of. It is what you have recently demonstrated under reasonably representative conditions. Evidence on self-efficacy reinforces the value of this approach. A meta-analysis examining changes within individuals found that previous performance was a stronger predictor of subsequent self-efficacy than self-efficacy was of subsequent performance. In other words, confidence partly follows evidence of what you have actually managed to do.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12035" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Meta‐Analytic Investigation of the Within‐Person Self‐Efficacy Domain: Is Self‐Efficacy a Product of Past Performance or a Driver of...</span></span></span>

Start by collecting a small baseline. Depending on the goal, that might mean your last several runs rather than your lifetime personal best, the number of focused study sessions you actually complete in a normal week, or the amount of uninterrupted practice you can currently sustain. A single unusually good or bad day is a weak calibration point; recent repeatable performance gives you a better picture of present capacity.

Then ask what improvement would require you to change your behaviour without requiring an unexplained leap in ability. Research illustrates why that distinction matters. In a muscular-endurance study involving secondary-school students, participants received goals ranging from no specific target through improvements of 10%, 20% and 40%. The 20% target was designed as difficult but realistic, while 40% was treated as improbable or unattainable. Across practice periods, the difficult-but-realistic group showed the largest gains; the improbable group performed substantially worse than that group after four and six weeks, although the difference was no longer present after eight weeks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9258843/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effect of goal difficulty, goal specificity and duration of practice time intervals on muscular endurance performance - PubMed...</span></span></span>

That does **not** establish 20% as a universal formula. The percentage came from one task and population. Indeed, experiments examining different ways of defining difficulty have found that results depend partly on whether goals are expressed as absolute performance levels or improvements over previous performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597885710035" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Effects of Varying Goal Difficulty Operationalizations on Goal Setting Outcomes and Processes - ScienceDirectJanuary 1...</span></span></span> The useful lesson is the method: anchor the target to evidence about current performance rather than adopting a fixed percentage that supposedly works for everybody.

A simple calibration cycle is:

1. **Establish your current range.** Look at several recent attempts under normal conditions.
2. **Choose a genuine stretch.** The target should demand more than your normal performance, not merely formalise something you already do.
3. **Check the route to improvement.** You should be able to identify plausible changes—more practice, better [consistency]({{ 'consistency/' | relative_url }}), improved technique or another concrete strategy—that could close the gap.
4. **Run the goal long enough to generate evidence.** One miss does not prove that a goal is excessive, just as one exceptional success does not prove that it is too easy.
5. **Recalibrate from the new evidence.** Consistent success is a reason to raise the challenge; persistent failure despite appropriate effort is a reason to examine the target, strategy or skill gap.

The point is adaptive difficulty. A well-calibrated goal is temporary because your ability should not remain fixed.

## Attainability matters without requiring easy goals

“Attainable” can sound like permission to avoid ambitious targets, but that is not what the evidence implies. The useful distinction is between a goal whose difficulty stimulates extra effort and one whose distance from current performance makes the target cease to function as a credible guide.

A revealing real-world example comes from Boston Marathon qualifying standards. Researchers analysed marathon performances from 1970 to 2015 and exploited changes in the qualifying times runners faced as they entered different age categories. After accounting for previous performance, runners tended to improve when the Boston qualifying target became more attainable; where it became more challenging, performance declined. The study therefore provided evidence that attainability itself can influence performance rather than merely reflecting who happens to be a stronger runner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167487016303063" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Goal attainability and performance: Evidence from Boston marathon qualifying standards - ScienceDirectFebruary 1, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2017</span></span></span>

The case is especially useful because the qualifying target is objectively identical for everyone in an age-and-sex category but subjectively very different depending on previous race times. A runner whose recent marathon is only slightly outside a qualifying mark faces a different motivational problem from someone who is an hour away from it. The official Boston Athletic Association still uses age-based qualifying standards, illustrating how even a prominent external benchmark recognises that equivalent absolute standards would not represent equivalent difficulty across populations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.baa.org/races/boston-marathon/qualify/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baa.org">[Boston Athletic Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Boston Athletic Association Qualify</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Boston Athletic Association Qualify</span></span></span>

There is nevertheless no well-established universal probability—such as “choose a goal you have a 70% chance of achieving”—that defines the ideal stretch across self-improvement activities. Goal-setting research varies considerably in tasks, populations and definitions of difficulty. Treat precise universal difficulty ratios with caution.

A better test is whether the target is **demanding but explainable**. If you are currently at A and want to reach B, can you describe a plausible route between them using skills and resources you possess or can reasonably acquire during the goal period? If the only explanation is “I will somehow try much harder”, the target may be aspiration rather than calibration.

## Current ability includes knowing what to do

Ability is not limited to strength, intelligence or experience. It also includes the knowledge and strategies required to perform the task. This becomes particularly important when somebody is attempting something new.

Research on moderately and highly complex tasks has repeatedly qualified the usual advice to set difficult performance goals. In one experiment using a class-scheduling task in which participants had to acquire knowledge to perform well, a specific difficult **learning goal** produced better performance than being told to do their best. Yet a specific difficult **outcome goal** performed worse than the do-your-best condition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/job.70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiley.com.</span></span></span> Seijts and Latham consequently argue that where people primarily need to acquire knowledge and skill, learning goals should direct attention towards discovering effective strategies rather than prematurely demanding a particular outcome.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/ame.2005.15841964" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.aom.org">[Academy of Management Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.aom.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aom.org.</span></span></span>

This changes how “match the goal to your ability” should be implemented. Imagine that you want to give confident presentations but have barely presented before. “Deliver a flawless 30-minute presentation without notes next month” may be measurable and ambitious, but it assumes that you already know how to organise the material, rehearse effectively, control pacing and respond to an audience. A better-calibrated stretch might initially concern learning and practice: rehearse a five-minute section repeatedly, test two ways of structuring an opening, record yourself and identify specific improvements, or practise answering likely questions.

The same principle has recently been incorporated into an expert statement on goal setting for [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) and [physical activity]({{ 'physical-capacity/' | relative_url }}): challenging performance goals are most appropriate when the person has the necessary ability, whereas people still acquiring knowledge or skills may benefit more from learning-oriented or more open goals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13018065/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

So a target can be “too hard” in two different ways. You may know exactly what to do but lack enough current capacity to hit the number, or you may not yet know **how** to produce the desired result. The second problem is not solved simply by lowering the outcome number. It may require changing what the goal asks you to accomplish.

## Warning signs that difficulty is badly calibrated

No questionnaire can tell you the perfect difficulty in advance. Calibration is better treated as an ongoing judgement based on behaviour and performance. Several patterns are particularly informative.

**The goal is probably too easy when success is nearly automatic.** If you repeatedly hit the target without extra concentration, effort, preparation or improvement in strategy, it is functioning more as a minimum standard than a stretch goal. Difficult goals generally have their advantage precisely because they can direct attention, increase effort and persistence, and encourage useful strategies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/j.1467-8721.2006.00449.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sage Journals New Directions in Goal-Setting Theory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage Journals New Directions in Goal-Setting Theory</span></span></span>

**The goal may be too hard when misses are large and repetitive despite serious, appropriate effort.** Missing a stretch target occasionally is not evidence of failure; a genuine stretch permits misses. More revealing is a stable pattern in which performance remains far below the target and the gap does not narrow. Research on muscular endurance provides one concrete example: the improbable 40% improvement target initially produced smaller gains than the difficult-but-realistic 20% target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9258843/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effect of goal difficulty, goal specificity and duration of practice time intervals on muscular endurance performance - PubMed...</span></span></span>

**Difficulty is suspect when the goal requires abilities you have not yet acquired.** If you repeatedly respond to failure with “I don't know what else to try”, the limiting factor may be strategy or knowledge rather than motivation. On complex tasks, simply increasing performance pressure can be counterproductive; learning goals are better suited to discovering how the task works.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/ame.2005.15841964" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.aom.org">[Academy of Management Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.aom.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aom.org.</span></span></span>

**Emotional strain is information, but not a complete difficulty gauge.** Harder goals can increase pressure. One perceptual-motor experiment found that progressively more difficult goals were accompanied by increased cognitive anxiety and reduced self-confidence, while very hard goals also produced better performance than a do-your-best instruction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9118936/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Goal difficulty, anxiety and performance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Goal difficulty, anxiety and performance</span></span></span> Feeling challenged therefore does not automatically mean a goal is excessive. The more useful question is whether strain is accompanied by adaptation and improving performance or by persistent breakdown.

**A target that was appropriate can become outdated.** Improving ability makes the same number easier. Conversely, losing practice time or encountering new constraints can turn yesterday's stretch into today's implausible demand. This is why [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) is part of calibration rather than merely a score at the end. Recent experiments on sustained attention, for example, have successfully used goals that become progressively harder over time rather than assuming one difficulty level should remain appropriate throughout practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37872435/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Effects of goal-setting on sustained attention and attention lapses</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Effects of goal-setting on sustained attention and attention lapses</span></span></span>

## The practical test: stretch from evidence, not aspiration

A useful stretch goal sits just beyond normal performance **for the person attempting it now**. That definition deliberately leaves out a universal percentage or success probability because the research does not justify one across different tasks and populations.

The strongest practical approach is therefore iterative. Establish what you can currently do, set a target that requires a meaningful but plausible improvement, observe what actually happens, and adjust. Research showing links between prior performance, ability and personal goal levels supports starting from demonstrated capacity, while experiments with ability-adjusted goals show why imposing the same target on people with different starting points can produce inferior results.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/014920639201800309" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsMeta-Analysis of the Antecedents of Personal Goal Level and of the Antecedents and Consequences of Goal Commitment - J. C. W...</span></span></span>

The crucial distinction is between **stretching an existing capability and demanding a capability that does not yet exist**. If you possess the necessary skill and repeatedly reach the goal without much additional effort, raise the difficulty. If you understand the task and are moving closer despite occasional misses, the stretch may be about right. If serious effort produces repeated large misses, reduce or restructure the target. And if you do not yet know how to improve, make learning the immediate goal before demanding a harder outcome.

That is what matching difficulty to ability looks like in practice: not lowering expectations, but placing the next challenge where it can actually change what you are capable of doing.
