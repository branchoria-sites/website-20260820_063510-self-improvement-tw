## Introduction

End-of-day preparation is useful only while it makes tomorrow easier to start. Once the first task, first concrete action, necessary materials and a reliable memory cue are ready, further organising can become a disguised form of procrastination: work that feels responsible but no longer meaningfully reduces tomorrow’s starting friction.

That distinction matters because planning genuinely can help. Specific implementation plans improve goal initiation, and concrete plans for unfinished work can reduce intrusive thoughts about it. But the research does not imply that more planning is always better. Reviews of procrastination explicitly identify excessive planning as one way action regulation can go wrong, while research on external reminders shows that reminders themselves have costs and should be used selectively.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/bookseries/abs/pii/S0065260106380021" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta‐analysis of Effects and Processes - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

The practical policy is therefore simple: **prepare for resumption, not for certainty**. End the shutdown routine when tomorrow’s first useful action can begin without another planning session.

## How preparation expands beyond its useful purpose

Preparation has an unusually convincing disguise. Renaming folders, improving a task system, rearranging tomorrow’s calendar and researching a better workflow all look more productive than obvious avoidance. The problem is not that these activities are inherently useless. It is that their value depends on whether they remove a real obstacle to subsequent action.

Research on procrastination provides a useful warning here. A scoping review of work-related procrastination describes procrastination partly as impaired action regulation: difficulties can occur in evaluating, planning, carrying out or completing work. Crucially, this can involve **excessive planning**, with planning becoming more important than doing the task itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8542725/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

That makes “I am preparing” an unreliable test of whether preparation is worthwhile. A better question is: **what decision or obstacle will this preparation remove tomorrow?** Opening the correct spreadsheet and writing “Next: reconcile the three unmatched invoices” has an identifiable payoff. Spending another 20 minutes redesigning the project dashboard may not.

The risk becomes greater when preparation promises relief from an uncomfortable task. Procrastination research increasingly treats delay not simply as poor time management but as a form of short-term mood regulation: when a task feels difficult, frustrating or aversive, avoiding it can provide immediate emotional relief while shifting the cost to the future self.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12011" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Procrastination and the Priority of Short‐Term Mood Regulation: Consequences for Future Self - Sirois - 2013 - Social and Personality...</span></span></span> An elaborate preparation ritual can fit that pattern without looking like avoidance. Instead of confronting the uncertain draft, difficult analysis or awkward decision, a person can continue working on the safer problem of *how work should be organised*.

Perfectionistic concerns can reinforce the same tendency. A meta-analysis involving roughly 10,000 participants found a positive association between procrastination and perfectionistic concerns, while a seven-day diary study found that perceived discrepancies between actual and ideal performance helped explain the relationship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/per.2098" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryA Meta‐analytic and Conceptual Update on the Associations Between Procrastination and Multidimensional Perfectionism...</span></span></span> Applied cautiously to end-of-day setup, this suggests a recognisable failure mode: “ready enough to restart” quietly becomes “everything must be perfectly arranged before I can stop”.

That is setup creep. Its defining feature is not how long the routine takes but whether additional effort is still reducing a genuine initiation barrier.

## The minimum setup needed for an easier restart

The strongest evidence for preparation supports specificity, not elaborateness. Gollwitzer and Sheeran’s meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found that implementation intentions — plans specifying when, where and how an intended behaviour will occur — produced a medium-to-large overall improvement in goal attainment and helped with initiating action. The mechanism involves making the relevant cue more accessible and strengthening the connection between that cue and the intended response.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/bookseries/abs/pii/S0065260106380021" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta‐analysis of Effects and Processes - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

For end-of-day preparation, that points towards a deliberately small package:

* **Choose the restart target.** Identify the task you intend to resume first rather than reprioritising your entire workload.
* **Specify one observable [next action]({{ 'next-action/' | relative_url }}).** “Continue budget” is vague; “open the August forecast and check the three flagged assumptions” is actionable.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Put the required material within reach.** Leave the correct document, dataset, notebook or physical material easy to locate.
* **Externalise anything you genuinely need to remember.** Record the unresolved thought, constraint or question rather than expecting tomorrow’s memory to reconstruct it.
* **Stop.** Do not treat successful setup as permission to start optimising the rest of tomorrow.

</div>

The memory step can be extremely small. Research on “intention offloading” — using external aids to carry future intentions — finds that reminders such as calendars, notes, alerts and strategically positioned objects can substantially improve fulfilment of delayed intentions. Importantly, the same review frames reminder-setting as a cost-benefit decision: reminders consume time and effort too, and setting them for everything would create unacceptable overhead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9971128/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Outsourcing Memory to External Tools: A Review of ‘Intention Offloading’ - PMC...</span></span></span>

That is a useful model for the whole shutdown routine. A cue earns its place when it saves more future effort than it costs to create. A note reading “Next: verify whether the 4.2% figure includes refunds” may save tomorrow’s self several minutes of reconstruction. Copying every possible future action into three different productivity systems probably does not.

The same principle applies to materials. “Materials ready” does not require an immaculate desk or inbox zero. It means that whatever blocks the *[first action]({{ 'first-action/' | relative_url }})* has been removed. If tomorrow starts with editing a presentation, having the current presentation and source figures identifiable is enough. Sorting unrelated downloads or choosing new folder icons does nothing for that restart.

## Why a small plan can be enough

There is a particularly relevant finding in research on unfinished goals. Masicampo and Baumeister found that unfinished goals could produce intrusive thoughts and interfere with unrelated tasks, but having participants make specific plans for completing those goals eliminated those cognitive effects in their experiments. Participants did not have to finish the underlying goal first; a credible plan for later action was enough to reduce interference.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Consider it done! Plan making can eliminate the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals - PubMed...</span></span></span>

This is important for shutdown routines because it challenges the intuition that peace of mind requires comprehensive closure. Often the work itself cannot be completed today. The useful substitute is not exhaustive replanning but sufficient closure about **how work will resume**.

Workplace evidence points in the same direction. Unfinished tasks are associated with poorer psychological detachment from work, and research on incomplete work goals suggests that concrete plans for when, where and how they will be resolved can help people disengage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29261139/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> Psychological detachment matters because off-job recovery depends partly on being able to stop engaging mentally and behaviourally with work demands rather than continually extending the workday into leisure time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-020-09691-5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on springer.com.</span></span></span>

This creates an important boundary. End-of-day planning can help you stop thinking about work; end-of-day planning that itself refuses to stop defeats part of the purpose.

More generally, time-management research supports planning without establishing a case for endless planning. A meta-analysis found moderate associations between time management and job performance, academic achievement and wellbeing, as well as a negative association with distress. A two-week workplace study likewise found benefits from daily planning, while showing that the usefulness of particular planning approaches depends on circumstances such as interruptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7799745/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Does time management work? A meta-analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Central (PMC)Does time management work? A meta-analysis</span></span></span> The sensible conclusion is not “plan as much as possible”, but “use planning to make execution easier”.

## A practical stopping rule for end-of-day preparation

A shutdown routine needs a stopping rule because preparation otherwise has no natural finish line. There is always another task that could be clarified, another contingency that could be anticipated and another system that could be tidied.

A robust stopping rule is:

**Stop preparing when you can name tomorrow’s first task, perform its first physical or digital action without replanning, access the materials required for that action, and recover any essential unfinished thought from an external cue.**

That rule deliberately ignores whether the whole day is optimised.

Suppose you finish work midway through a difficult report. You decide that tomorrow’s restart is the results section. You leave a note saying, “Next: compare the regional conversion rates with Table 3; explain the North–South gap.” The current report and Table 3 are saved where you can immediately reach them. At that point the setup has accomplished its purpose. Rearranging the bibliography, cleaning the downloads folder, colour-coding the remaining tasks and rebuilding tomorrow’s calendar may create a stronger *feeling* of readiness, but they do not materially improve the first restart.

A useful diagnostic is to test every additional preparation action against three questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **What specific friction will this remove tomorrow?**
2. **Does that friction affect the first meaningful action, or merely make the system look more complete?**
3. **Would stopping now leave enough information to restart confidently?**

</div>

If the third answer is yes and the first two reveal no concrete benefit, stopping is the productive action.

This also guards against the planning fallacy in a small but useful way. Research on task-duration prediction shows that people tend to construct optimistic future scenarios while underusing evidence from their previous experience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0065260110430014%23%21" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect The Planning Fallacy: Cognitive, Motivational, and Social Origins</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirect The Planning Fallacy: Cognitive, Motivational, and Social Origins</span></span></span> Elaborate evening schedules can therefore create false precision: a carefully packed calendar may imply that tomorrow is more controllable than it really is. Preparing the first action asks much less of forecasting. You do not need to know exactly how the entire day will unfold to know what file to open first.

## When setup creep is already happening

The clearest sign of setup creep is repeated work on the *representation* of work while execution remains unchanged. The task list is being rewritten, the calendar adjusted and the workspace improved, yet the same difficult project is still waiting.

At that point, adding a more sophisticated planning method is usually the wrong intervention. Reduce the routine instead. Preserve only the elements that carry information across the overnight boundary: the restart target, next action, essential materials and memory cue. Everything else must justify itself by removing a demonstrated source of friction.

This distinction also prevents an unhelpful swing to the opposite extreme. The answer to overplanning is not zero preparation. Specific plans can improve initiation; reminders can reliably support future intentions; and credible plans for unfinished work can make it easier to disengage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/bookseries/abs/pii/S0065260106380021" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta‐analysis of Effects and Processes - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span> The target is **minimum effective preparation**.

That is the larger self-improvement principle at work here. A useful system should make the desired behaviour easier to perform, not become a substitute behaviour that demands continuous maintenance. End-of-day setup has succeeded when tomorrow’s starting point is obvious enough that you no longer need to think about preparing for it today.
