## Introduction

A habit can become easier or harder without changing the person’s motivation at all. Sometimes the decisive change is mundane: putting an object within reach, moving food a few metres away, removing a login step, preparing equipment in advance, or inserting a short delay before an impulsive action. Behavioural researchers often describe these small physical and procedural obstacles as **friction costs**. They matter because intentions have to survive the practical effort required at the moment of action. The OECD notes that even minor obstacles can encourage delay and inertia, while reducing clicks, questions or other required actions can increase follow-through.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/tools-and-ethics-for-applied-behavioural-insights-the-basic-toolkit_9ea76a8f-en/full-report/component-6.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oecd.org">[OECD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oecd.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">component 6</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The BASIC Manual: Tools and Ethics for Applied Behavioural Insights: The BASIC Toolkit &#124; OECDJune 18, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 18, 2019</span></span></span>

This is one reason effective self-improvement need not depend on repeatedly winning battles of willpower. Instead of asking only, “How do I become more disciplined?”, it can be useful to ask a narrower implementation question: **Which behaviour is currently easiest to perform, and can a few steps be moved from the behaviour I want to the behaviour I do not?**

## What behavioural friction means


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Friction is the effort separating an intention from an action. In its most literal form it is physical: standing up, walking across a room, opening a cupboard or retrieving something from another floor. But the same principle can apply procedurally. Finding a password, navigating several screens, completing another form or waiting through a delay all make an action slightly more troublesome.

</div>

The important point is that friction need not make an action impossible. It changes its **relative convenience at the moment of choice**. The UK Behavioural Insights Team’s EAST framework therefore recommends reducing the “hassle factor” when trying to encourage behaviour. Its examples include sending taxpayers directly to the relevant form rather than making them navigate through an intermediate webpage. The OECD similarly recommends examining the actual sequence people must follow and reducing unnecessary actions, clicks and questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://behaviourchange.hee.nhs.uk/downloads/toolkit/BIT-Publication-EAST_FA_WEB%2818%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: behaviourchange.hee.nhs.uk">[Behaviour Change]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">behaviourchange.hee.nhs.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Behaviour Change EASY</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TIMELY ATTRACTIVE SOCIAL EAST Four simple wayApril 14, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 14, 2014</span></span></span>

For personal habits, this suggests a practical distinction between two kinds of environmental change:

* **[Reduce friction]({{ 'reduce-friction/' | relative_url }}) for the behaviour you want.** Put running shoes where they are encountered before a planned run; keep the document you need to work on readily accessible; prepare ingredients before the point when cooking competes with ordering food.
* **Add modest friction to the behaviour you want less of.** Put tempting food somewhere that requires getting up; keep a distracting device out of immediate reach; log out of an attention-grabbing service rather than leaving it continuously available.

These interventions are deliberately small. Their purpose is not to create an impregnable barrier. It is to alter what happens during the brief interval in which an intention either becomes behaviour or disappears.

That distinction also prevents “friction” becoming a vague synonym for every influence on behaviour. A motivational speech may change how much somebody wants to [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}); a reminder may make the intention salient; a social commitment may create accountability. Friction specifically concerns how much effort or inconvenience lies on the path to acting.

## A few metres changed workplace snacking

One unusually concrete illustration comes from a 2016 field study conducted in a heavily used micro-kitchen at Google’s New York office. Employees could obtain drinks from two otherwise identical beverage stations. One was about 6 feet 5 inches (roughly 2 metres) from a snack station; the other was 17 feet 6 inches (roughly 5.3 metres) away. The snacks remained available either way. The researchers were testing whether those additional metres were enough to influence what employees did.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0195666316301465" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Proximity of snacks to beverages increases food consumption in the workplace: A field study - ScienceDirectAugust 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2016</span></span></span>

They were. Among men, the observed likelihood of taking a snack was 23% when they used the nearer beverage station versus 12% at the farther station. Among women, it was 17% versus 13%. Nothing had been banned, nobody had to pay more and the distant station was hardly an expedition. The relevant environmental difference was simply that pairing a drink with a snack required a little more movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0195666316301465" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Proximity of snacks to beverages increases food consumption in the workplace: A field study - ScienceDirectAugust 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2016</span></span></span>

That makes the study useful for understanding friction rather than merely workplace nutrition. An additional eleven feet or so seems trivial when considered abstractly. At the exact moment when somebody already has their drink, however, those feet separate an effortless add-on from a separate action. The intervention therefore works upstream of a classic [self-control]({{ 'self-control/' | relative_url }}) struggle: fewer people arrive at the point of physically taking the snack.

The finding is consistent with a broader experimental literature on food proximity. A 2019 Cochrane review identified 18 randomised studies manipulating proximity. In a meta-analysis of 15 comparisons from 12 studies involving 1,098 participants, placing food farther away was associated with moderately lower consumption. Greater distances tended to produce larger effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD012573_altering-availability-or-proximity-food-alcohol-and-tobacco-products-change-their-selection-and" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cochrane.org">[Cochrane]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cochrane.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption &#124; Cochran...</span></span></span>

That broader evidence matters because a memorable workplace experiment can otherwise invite an overly tidy conclusion. The Cochrane authors rated the certainty of the evidence for the consumption result as **low**, noting risk-of-bias concerns, the predominance of laboratory research and a need for better long-term real-world studies. The safer conclusion is therefore not that moving something a precise number of metres reliably produces a particular percentage reduction. It is that **proximity is capable of changing consumption, while the size and durability of that effect depend on context**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD012573_altering-availability-or-proximity-food-alcohol-and-tobacco-products-change-their-selection-and" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cochrane.org">[Cochrane]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cochrane.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption &#124; Cochran...</span></span></span>

## Friction works beyond physical distance

The same implementation logic can operate in digital environments. A distracting action may require virtually no physical movement, yet designers can still change the number of moments separating impulse from consumption.

A preregistered field experiment involving 112 participants compared strategies for reducing smartphone screen time. A design-friction intervention using greyscale produced an immediate significant reduction in objectively measured screen time relative to a self-monitoring control, whereas goal-setting produced a smaller, more gradual reduction. The study did not find corresponding immediate improvements in subjective wellbeing or academic performance, which is an important boundary: changing a behaviour is not automatically the same thing as producing every hoped-for downstream benefit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36577008/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digital Strategies for Screen Time Reduction: A Randomized Field Experiment - PubMedDecember 26, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 26, 2022</span></span></span>

Research on the self-nudge app One Sec provides another example of inserting a break into digital consumption. Experimental work testing features including a delay before content consumption found that time-based friction reduced consumption behaviour; importantly, offering users an explicit opportunity to dismiss the content was particularly effective. A message alone was not. The result fits the broader implementation principle: changing the action sequence can matter more than simply telling somebody what they ought to do.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9974409/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Directing smartphone use through the self-nudge app one sec</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Central (PMC)Directing smartphone use through the self-nudge app one sec</span></span></span>

Procedural friction can matter on a much larger scale too. In a randomised experiment involving Massachusetts health-insurance enrolment, simplifying the process with a “check the box” intervention increased enrolment by 11%, outperforming personalised and generic reminder letters. The effect was concentrated among people eligible for zero-premium plans, for whom additional payment-related administrative barriers did not remain. That is a particularly clean illustration of why motivation or economic incentives alone cannot explain behaviour: even when the financial price is zero, the process can still carry a meaningful behavioural cost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nber.org/papers/w30885" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nber.org">[National Bureau of Economic Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nber.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nber.org.</span></span></span>

For everyday self-improvement, the analogy should not be pushed too far: enrolling in health insurance is not the same as establishing a morning routine. But the implementation lesson transfers. When somebody repeatedly intends to perform an action yet fails at the same procedural point, adding more reasons to want the outcome may be less useful than inspecting that point of failure.

## Where friction helps — and where its effects fade

Friction is most useful when the desired or undesired behaviour is already close to a threshold. If someone is mildly tempted to check a distracting app, having to retrieve the phone from another room may be enough to interrupt the impulse. If someone strongly intends to use it, the walk will probably only delay them. Likewise, laying out exercise clothes can remove a small obstacle to an intended workout; it cannot reliably manufacture a desire to exercise where none exists.

That is why friction should be treated as an **implementation lever, not a universal theory of behaviour**. The food-proximity evidence illustrates the limitation particularly well. Systematic reviews generally find that positioning can influence food choice or consumption, but they also flag weak study designs, limited real-world evidence and uncertainty about longer-term or compensatory effects. A 2016 systematic review concluded that food order and proximity can influence choice while calling for stronger studies that measure actual intake and examine whether people compensate later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27185414/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

There is also no law saying that *less* friction is always better. Recent evidence offers an interesting counterexample. A 2026 field experiment involving 27,227 people signing up for a carpool platform found that a more effortful sign-up process produced 25% fewer sign-ups but more subsequent use: participants assigned to the higher-friction process took 1.6 times as many carpool trips per week over four months on an intention-to-treat basis. A second experiment found greater next-day return among participants who faced a more effortful sign-up. The researchers describe this as a potential “buy-in effect”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cepr.org/publications/dp21442" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cepr.org">[CEPR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cepr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cepr.org.</span></span></span>

That finding does not overturn the larger evidence that unnecessary hassle suppresses action. It shows that **the outcome being optimised matters**. Removing friction can increase initial uptake; in some circumstances, requiring a modest initial investment may screen for commitment or strengthen subsequent engagement. For habit design, therefore, “make everything effortless” is too crude a rule.

A better implementation test is to identify the behaviour and the precise stage that matters:

**For initiation**, remove avoidable preparation. If exercise repeatedly dies between “I should go” and “I am ready to leave”, preparing clothing and equipment beforehand targets the relevant friction.

**For unwanted impulses**, add a small interruption. Distance, an extra retrieval step or a short digital delay can create a point at which an automatic sequence can stop.

**For repetition**, avoid making the desired routine unnecessarily elaborate. A system that requires several preparatory rituals may work while enthusiasm is high but become fragile on busy days.

**For consequential actions**, retain useful friction. Confirmation screens, deliberate review and other pauses can protect against mistakes. Convenience is not automatically desirable when impulsive action itself is the problem.

## Design the path, not just the intention

The practical value of friction is that it turns an abstract goal into something observable. Instead of asking why discipline failed, trace the literal path from intention to behaviour: Where is the object? How many actions are required? What has to be found, opened, remembered or prepared? Which alternative is immediately available?

Then make a deliberately small change. A useful habit might be moved one step closer: equipment prepared, materials placed at the point of use, an unnecessary screen removed. An unwanted habit might be moved one step farther away: the object relocated, automatic access disabled or a brief pause inserted. Behavioural-insights guidance explicitly recommends mapping real behaviour in this way rather than assuming people follow the process designers imagine for them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/tools-and-ethics-for-applied-behavioural-insights-the-basic-toolkit_9ea76a8f-en/full-report/component-5.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oecd.org">[OECD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oecd.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Introductory guide to BASIC: Tools and Ethics for Applied Behavioural Insights: The BASIC Toolkit &#124; OECD...</span></span></span>

The evidence does not support the magical version of this idea, in which moving a biscuit tin or adding a login screen permanently transforms behaviour. Effects vary, people adapt, strong preferences can overwhelm modest barriers, and downstream outcomes may not change merely because one behaviour does. But that is precisely why friction is a useful systems tool rather than a motivational slogan. It asks for no new personality trait. It changes the small piece of the environment that repeatedly meets a person at the moment of choice.

For self-improvement that survives ordinary fluctuations in motivation, that can be enough to matter: **make the behaviour you want slightly easier to begin, and make the behaviour you want less of slightly less automatic.**
