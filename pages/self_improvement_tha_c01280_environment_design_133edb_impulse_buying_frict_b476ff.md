## Introduction

Adding a little friction to online shopping can interrupt an impulse before it becomes a purchase. The principle is straightforward: if [saved cards]({{ 'saved-cards/' | relative_url }}), one-click checkout and retailer prompts make buying almost automatic, selectively reversing those conveniences creates a moment in which the shopper has to decide again. This is not merely intuition. A 35-month study of an online retailer found that adopting one-click buying increased purchasing by making customers buy more often and purchase more items, with effects that persisted over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2023.4716" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: informs.org">[PubsOnline]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">informs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pubs Online Fewer Clicks, More Purchases &#124; Management Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubsOnlineFewer Clicks, More Purchases &#124; Management ScienceFebruary 22, 2023</span></span></span>

The useful target is therefore not maximum inconvenience. It is **a small interruption between wanting and paying**: typing payment details, signing back into an account, reviewing a basket or waiting before completing an unplanned order. Experimental research on online shopping also suggests that postponement and reflection can reduce purchase impulsivity in environments containing scarcity and social-proof prompts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/dark-patterns-in-online-shopping-do-they-work-and-can-nudges-help-mitigate-impulse-buying/996B92402604A7E3D417ECBAE2C38362" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press Dark patterns in online shopping: do they work and can nudges help mitigate impulse buying? &#124; Behavioural Public Policy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University PressDark patterns in online shopping: do they work and can nudges help mitigate impulse buying? &#124; Behavioural Publi...</span></span></span> For someone whose problem is spontaneous rather than necessary spending, carefully chosen checkout friction can make the online environment support the decision they would prefer to make after the immediate urge has passed.

## Why low-friction checkout encourages repeat purchasing

Online retailers have good commercial reasons to remove checkout steps. Every forgotten password, form field and confirmation page gives a customer another opportunity to abandon a transaction. But the same efficiency has a behavioural consequence: it reduces the distance between an initial impulse and an irreversible purchase.

The clearest evidence comes from Murat Unal and Young-Hoon Park's study,<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2023.4716" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: informs.org">[Fewer Clicks, More Purchases]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">informs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pubs Online Fewer Clicks, More Purchases &#124; Management Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubsOnlineFewer Clicks, More Purchases &#124; Management ScienceFebruary 22, 2023</span></span></span>, published in *Management Science*. Using quasi-experimental data covering 35 months around an online retailer's introduction of one-click buying, the researchers found that customers adopting the feature subsequently purchased more frequently and bought more items. The effect was economically significant and persistent rather than a short-lived novelty. Their clickstream analysis also indicated greater engagement with the retailer, including more visits and page views, and purchases spreading across more product categories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2023.4716" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: informs.org">[PubsOnline]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">informs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pubs Online Fewer Clicks, More Purchases &#124; Management Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubsOnlineFewer Clicks, More Purchases &#124; Management ScienceFebruary 22, 2023</span></span></span>

That finding matters because one-click purchasing does more than save a few seconds. With an address and payment method already stored, the sequence can shrink from “I like this” → “Do I want to spend £45?” → “find my card” → “enter the details” → “confirm” into something much closer to “I like this” → “buy”. A consumer trying to reduce spontaneous purchases can use the same mechanism in reverse. Removing a saved payment method does not forbid the purchase; it makes the intention survive one additional test.

There is a second mechanism at work: **payment becomes less salient as it becomes more seamless**. Research on the “pain of payment” — the psychological discomfort associated with giving up money — has repeatedly found relationships between payment characteristics and spending. Reviews note that less transparent and more psychologically decoupled payment methods can make the financial outflow less prominent during the purchasing decision. The exact psychological mechanism remains contested, however: recent studies have not consistently found that consciously experienced payment discomfort fully explains why cashless methods can increase purchasing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11444724/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Why does mobile payment promote purchases? Revisiting the pain of paying, and understanding the implicit pleasure via...</span></span></span>

That qualification is important. There is stronger direct evidence that **reducing checkout effort can increase purchasing** than there is evidence that deleting a saved card will produce a predictable reduction in any particular person's spending. The practical strategy is best understood as a plausible reversal of a demonstrated commercial mechanism, not as a guaranteed behavioural treatment.

## Which purchasing conveniences are worth removing

The best friction targets are conveniences that disproportionately help with purchases you later question. Removing every useful shopping feature would simply create irritation and encourage workarounds. A better approach is to identify where an impulse currently has an unusually short path to completion.

**Remove one-click purchasing first.** If a retailer allows an item to move almost directly from product page to confirmed order, disabling that option restores a meaningful decision point. The one-click evidence makes this the most directly supported target: reducing steps increases purchasing, so this is precisely the convenience to question when excessive purchasing is the problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2023.4716" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: informs.org">[PubsOnline]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">informs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pubs Online Fewer Clicks, More Purchases &#124; Management Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubsOnlineFewer Clicks, More Purchases &#124; Management ScienceFebruary 22, 2023</span></span></span>

**Delete stored payment details from high-risk retailers.** Having to retrieve a card and enter its details is modest friction, but that is the point. A purchase you planned to make can still be completed in a minute or two. A late-night, boredom-driven or sale-triggered purchase now has time to lose momentum. Where browsers or digital wallets automatically refill payment information, removing the retailer's saved card alone may not create much friction, so the whole payment path needs to be considered.

**Stay signed out of shops associated with habitual browsing.** Accounts often combine stored addresses, payment information, order histories, recommendations and rapid checkout. Signing in only when there is something specific to buy separates browsing from purchasing without preventing either.

**Turn off retailer-generated purchase triggers.** Friction is not confined to the checkout itself. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) identifies prompts and reminders, scarcity messages and personalised offers as forms of online choice architecture capable of influencing consumer behaviour. Its evidence review finds particularly substantial evidence around scarcity and popularity claims, which can increase urgency, purchasing intentions and interaction with products.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-choice-architecture-how-digital-design-can-harm-competition-and-consumers/evidence-review-of-online-choice-architecture-and-consumer-and-competition-harm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: At the trigger:">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">At the trigger:</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.uk.</span></span></span> Unsubscribing from sale emails, disabling retailer push [notifications]({{ 'notifications/' | relative_url }}) and removing shopping apps that mainly generate browsing occasions therefore complements checkout friction: fewer impulses enter the purchase funnel in the first place.

**Do not let a preselected option make the decision for you.** UK Department for Business and Trade research examined 558 consumer journeys and found defaults on 49% of the sampled websites and apps. In a subsequent experiment involving 5,889 participants, people encountering a preselected default were 60–70% more likely to choose the more expensive option than those seeing no default; importantly, many observed defaults in the prevalence study actually favoured cheaper or similarly priced choices, so defaults are not inherently harmful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-prevalence-and-potential-harm-of-defaults-in-online-shopping" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The prevalence and potential harm of defaults in online shopping</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The prevalence and potential harm of defaults in online shopping</span></span></span> The practical lesson is narrower: treat preselection as something to review rather than as a recommendation.

This distinction helps prevent “friction” becoming an indiscriminate rule. A saved grocery delivery address used for a planned weekly shop may be useful infrastructure. A stored card on a fashion site repeatedly opened after promotional notifications may be exactly the convenience worth removing.

## The interruption has to occur before the urge wins

Checkout friction is valuable because it changes *when* deliberation happens. Trying to reconsider an impulse after an order confirmation is much harder than inserting a decision before payment. The intervention does not need to persuade someone that the product is bad; it needs to create enough time for the question “Do I still want to do this?” to arise.

Experimental evidence offers some support for this interruption model. A study published in *Behavioural Public Policy* first tested limited-quantity scarcity, high-demand messages and positive testimonials in a hypothetical online-shopping environment and found greater purchase impulsivity under all three manipulations than in the control condition. A second experiment tested reflection, distraction and postponement interventions against several such designs. Effects varied depending on the intervention and dark pattern, but within-person comparisons showed lower impulsivity after intervention exposure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/dark-patterns-in-online-shopping-do-they-work-and-can-nudges-help-mitigate-impulse-buying/996B92402604A7E3D417ECBAE2C38362" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University Press Dark patterns in online shopping: do they work and can nudges help mitigate impulse buying? &#124; Behavioural Public Policy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University PressDark patterns in online shopping: do they work and can nudges help mitigate impulse buying? &#124; Behavioural Publi...</span></span></span>

Postponement is particularly relevant to self-imposed checkout friction. Rather than requiring a permanent “no”, it changes “buy now” into “decide later”. That is useful because online shops themselves frequently try to collapse the decision window. The CMA's review describes scarcity claims such as low-stock warnings, high-demand notices and limited-time offers as mechanisms that can generate time pressure, encourage quicker purchasing and reduce further searching.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-choice-architecture-how-digital-design-can-harm-competition-and-consumers/evidence-review-of-online-choice-architecture-and-consumer-and-competition-harm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: At the trigger:">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">At the trigger:</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Regulators have also documented how consequential extremely low purchase friction can become. In the US Federal Trade Commission's case involving *Fortnite*, the agency alleged that confusing controls and purchases completed with a single button produced unwanted charges. The FTC reported that the company had received more than a million complaints about unwanted charges; internal recommendations included adding confirmation steps, while one stated concern about such changes was that they could reduce impulse purchases. The resulting order required Epic Games to pay $245 million for consumer refunds and change its billing practices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2022/12/245-million-ftc-settlement-alleges-fortnite-owner-epic-games-used-digital-dark-patterns-charge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ftc.gov">[Federal Trade Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ftc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ftc.gov.</span></span></span>

That case concerns unwanted and sometimes unauthorised transactions rather than ordinary adult shopping, so it should not be treated as direct evidence for a personal spending programme. It does, however, provide a striking real-world example of the underlying design principle: **whether a purchase requires one action or a sequence of deliberate confirmations can materially affect what gets bought**.

## How to add friction without blocking useful shopping

The most sustainable setup is asymmetric: routine, planned purchases remain reasonably easy, while the purchases most likely to be impulsive encounter an extra step.

A practical version can be built around three layers:



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_environment_design_133edb_impulse_buying_frict_b476ff-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Spending Friction illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_environment_design_133edb_impulse_buying_frict_b476ff-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_environment_design_133edb_impulse_buying_frict_b476ff-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 3</span></figcaption></figure>

1. **At the trigger:** unsubscribe from promotional emails and texts, disable shopping-app notifications and remove retailer apps whose main effect is prompting recreational browsing. The CMA's evidence review gives reason to take scarcity, popularity messages and reminders seriously rather than assuming that consumers simply ignore them. [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-choice-architecture-how-digital-design-can-harm-competition-and-consumers/evidence-review-of-online-choice-architecture-and-consumer-and-competition-harm)

2. **At the checkout:** disable one-click ordering, remove stored payment details from problem retailers and avoid automatic sign-in where it makes buying nearly instantaneous. This directly restores some of the steps that commercial checkout optimisation removes. [PubsOnline](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2023.4716)

3. **At the final decision:** for an unplanned discretionary purchase, leave the item in the basket or on a wish list and return later. A delay need not be universal or rigid: the point is to prevent a temporary state of urgency from determining the transaction. Experimental work provides some support for postponement as an intervention against impulse-promoting online designs. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/dark-patterns-in-online-shopping-do-they-work-and-can-nudges-help-mitigate-impulse-buying/996B92402604A7E3D417ECBAE2C38362)

The amount of friction should match the problem. Someone occasionally buying a £15 item they do not need may require nothing more than deleting a saved card. Someone repeatedly making larger unplanned orders might use a longer delay before discretionary purchases. Conversely, adding repeated barriers to medication, essential household goods or genuinely time-sensitive travel would defeat the purpose. The objective is not to make all commerce unpleasant.

It is also worth keeping the intervention simple enough that it cannot be effortlessly bypassed. Removing a retailer's stored card achieves little if the browser instantly fills the same details; deleting a shopping app achieves little if promotional emails provide a direct route back. Rather than adding ten minor restrictions, it is often more coherent to identify the shortest route from **trigger → product → payment** and place one meaningful interruption in that route.

## Friction works best as a pause, not a punishment

There is a temptation to make anti-[spending systems]({{ 'spending-systems/' | relative_url }}) severe: block every shopping site, delete every account and turn each purchase into an ordeal. That confuses the mechanism with the goal. Friction is useful when it distinguishes a passing impulse from a durable intention. If an item still seems worthwhile after retrieving a card, checking the total and returning after a delay, the extra steps have done their job even if the eventual decision is to buy it.

The broader online environment makes that modest intervention more relevant. Regulators describe digital interfaces as “choice architecture” precisely because presentation, defaults, prompts and effort influence decisions. A 2024 international review of 642 subscription websites and apps found that nearly 76% displayed at least one potential dark pattern and nearly 67% displayed more than one, although the review did **not** determine that every identified practice was unlawful. [Federal Trade Commission](https://search.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/07/ftc-icpen-gpen-announce-results-review-use-dark-patterns-affecting-subscription-services-privacy) The UK CMA similarly warns that digital design can use pressure selling, hidden charges and other techniques in ways that do not benefit consumers. [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/online-choice-architecture)

For self-improvement, the useful response is not to try to become psychologically immune to every interface. It is to recognise that convenience is itself part of the environment. When spontaneous online spending is a recurring problem, removing saved payments, disabling one-click ordering, reducing retailer prompts and inserting a short delay converts an almost [automatic action]({{ 'automatic-action/' | relative_url }}) back into an explicit decision. That is a small environmental change, but it targets the purchase at exactly the point where a few extra seconds or steps can matter.
