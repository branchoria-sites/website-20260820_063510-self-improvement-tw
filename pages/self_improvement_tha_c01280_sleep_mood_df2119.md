## Introduction

Poor sleep changes more than alertness. It changes the emotional conditions in which behaviour change has to happen. Experimental research shows a particularly consistent reduction in **positive affect** — feelings such as enthusiasm, attentiveness and energy — after sleep loss. Negative mood can also increase, but that effect is less uniform and depends more on how sleep is lost and how mood is measured. The most comprehensive recent meta-analysis, covering 154 studies and 5,717 participants, found that total sleep deprivation, partial [sleep restriction]({{ 'mental-habits/' | relative_url }}) and sleep fragmentation all reduced positive affect, while effects on negative affect were more mixed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38127505/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sleep loss and emotion: A systematic review and meta-analysis of over 50 years of experimental research - PubMedDecember 21, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 2023</span></span></span>

That distinction matters for self-improvement. A tired person may not simply feel “worse”. They may feel less interested, less energised and less willing to invest effort in something whose benefits arrive later. Habits still have to be chosen and executed under those conditions. Sleep therefore belongs inside the design of behaviour change: demanding actions should not be planned as though mood, motivation and perceived effort remain constant from one day to the next.

## What sleep-loss meta-analyses actually report

Two major quantitative reviews give the clearest picture, and together they show why “lack of sleep makes you grumpy” is too crude a summary.

A 2021 meta-analysis combined 241 effect sizes from 64 studies examining sleep deprivation or sleep restriction. Sleep loss produced a **large reduction in [positive mood]({{ 'positive-mood/' | relative_url }})** (Hedges’ g = −0.94) and a **moderate increase in negative mood** (g = 0.45). Total sleep deprivation produced a larger increase in negative mood than partial restriction. The same review also found a small reduction in adaptive emotion regulation, although those emotion-regulation studies were concentrated in younger participants, which limits how confidently that result can be generalised to adults.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33367799/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The effect of sleep deprivation and restriction on mood, emotion, and emotion regulation: three meta-analyses in one - PubMedJune 1...</span></span></span>

A larger preregistered meta-analysis published in *Psychological Bulletin* in 2024 widened the evidence base to 1,338 effect sizes from 154 experiments involving people aged 7 to 79. Across total deprivation, restricted sleep and fragmented sleep, positive affect consistently fell, with standardised mean differences ranging from −0.27 to −1.14. Anxiety symptoms increased, and emotional arousal in response to stimuli was blunted. By contrast, effects on negative affect and depressive symptoms varied according to the sleep manipulation and other study characteristics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38127505/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sleep loss and emotion: A systematic review and meta-analysis of over 50 years of experimental research - PubMedDecember 21, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 2023</span></span></span>

The important point is therefore asymmetric. **Losing positive mood is one of the most dependable emotional consequences of losing sleep. Becoming markedly more negative is possible, but not inevitable.** A person trying to change behaviour may consequently encounter a quieter problem than obvious irritability: [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) seems less appealing, studying feels flatter, [meal preparation]({{ 'meal-prep/' | relative_url }}) has less pull, and a useful activity offers less immediate emotional reward.

This asymmetry is also visible in controlled laboratory datasets. Research comparing chronic sleep restriction, total sleep deprivation and deliberately mistimed sleep found systematic reductions in attentiveness, joviality, self-assurance and other positive mood states. Negative moods changed less consistently, although hostility and general negative affect rose under some conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36062257/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Contrasting Effects of Sleep Restriction, Total Sleep Deprivation, and Sleep Timing on Positive and Negative Affect - PubMedAugust...</span></span></span>

These estimates should not be treated as a formula for predicting how one individual will feel after one poor night. The studies differ substantially in sleep schedules, participant ages, laboratory conditions, measurement instruments and timing of mood assessments, and meta-analyses report considerable heterogeneity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/8193556/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> What they establish more securely is the direction of the population-level risk: insufficient sleep tends to make desirable emotional states harder to sustain.

## Why losing positive mood can undermine a habit

Positive affect is not simply a pleasant extra added after self-regulation has succeeded. It helps determine whether activities feel worth approaching in the first place. That makes the reduction in positive mood especially relevant to habits that require initiation, concentration or repeated effort before they become automatic.

Consider a person who intends to spend 45 minutes revising after work. On a well-rested day, the task may still be demanding, but interest, attentiveness and confidence help offset its cost. After curtailed sleep, the objective task can be identical while its subjective economics have changed: exerting effort feels more expensive and the prospective reward may feel less motivating.

Experimental work supports that interpretation. In one study of 77 healthy young adults, total sleep deprivation increased **effort discounting**: participants became more inclined to choose smaller rewards that required less effort rather than larger rewards requiring more work. Their preference for immediate rather than delayed rewards did not change in the same way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23729933/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sleep deprivation alters effort discounting but not delay discounting of monetary rewards - PubMedJune 1, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2013</span></span></span>

Another experiment found that sleep deprivation increased the perceived cost of sustained attentional effort. Participants performed worse on a vigilance task, and sleep loss further reduced the value they assigned to rewards that required longer periods of attention. Higher incentives could partly improve performance, showing that tired performance reflects an interaction between impaired capacity and willingness to allocate effort rather than a simple on/off loss of ability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29601889/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Partial restriction can produce a similar pattern. In an experiment in which 24 healthy young adults received either three nights with three hours of sleep opportunity or three nights with nine hours, restriction reduced willingness to expend **cognitive** effort for reward. Interestingly, willingness to expend physical effort was not significantly reduced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36394069/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Sleep Restriction Reduces Cognitive but Not Physical Motivation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Sleep Restriction Reduces Cognitive but Not Physical Motivation</span></span></span>

That finding argues against describing tiredness as a universal collapse in motivation. Behaviour change is more likely to suffer selectively. A mentally demanding evening habit — planning finances, learning a language, writing, studying or making a complicated food choice — may become disproportionately difficult even when the person remains capable of carrying out simpler or more physical actions.

## A bad night can change how the day feels

Laboratory deprivation studies establish causality more cleanly, but ordinary life rarely involves staying awake for 24 or 40 hours. Everyday sleep varies in smaller increments, often alongside work, relationships and other stressors. Naturalistic research helps bridge that gap.

A systematic review of prospective daily-life studies found reciprocal associations between subjective sleep and next-day mood: sleep quality, sleep duration and time taken to fall asleep were linked with subsequent affect, while mood could also predict later sleep.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30404728/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> A 2024 systematic review of 121 studies using digital technologies and wearables similarly found broadly bidirectional sleep–affect relationships, but reported that sleep was more often the stronger predictor of subsequent daytime affect than the reverse. Results were also stronger for subjective sleep measures than for actigraphy, a reminder that perceived sleep and objectively recorded sleep are not interchangeable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39066098/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

One daily-life study illustrates why this matters during habit formation. Nights that were shorter than a person’s usual sleep predicted a larger reduction in positive affect when stressful events occurred the next day, and smaller increases in positive affect when good events occurred. Negative-affect responses were not altered in the same way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7704897/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

For behaviour change, that means the consequences of sleep loss may become most visible at the moment a new habit meets normal resistance. A difficult meeting runs late, the weather is poor, dinner still needs making and the scheduled workout now competes with the sofa. Short sleep has not necessarily removed the intention to exercise. It may instead have reduced the positive emotional buffer that would normally make the inconvenience tolerable.

## Mood and self-control are connected, but not identical

It is tempting to compress all of this into “sleep reduces willpower”. The evidence supports a more careful claim.

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of 61 independent studies found that both sleep quality and sleep duration were associated with [self-control]({{ 'self-control/' | relative_url }}). Within-person associations were stronger for sleep quality than for duration: on occasions when an individual slept better, self-control also tended to be better. The authors nevertheless emphasised that the causal pathways and relevant moderators still required further investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34157493/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Sleep and self-control: A systematic review and meta-analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Sleep and self-control: A systematic review and meta-analysis</span></span></span>

Mood is one plausible part of that pathway rather than a complete explanation. Sleep loss can simultaneously lower positive affect, make cognitive effort more costly, impair sustained attention and influence aspects of emotion regulation. A failed habit after a poor night can therefore arise from several interacting changes. Treating the event as pure lack of discipline ignores this state dependence.

The distinction matters because it changes the governance of a behaviour-change system. If every missed session is interpreted as evidence that the goal is wrong or the person is insufficiently committed, transient sleep-related impairment can provoke unnecessary redesign or abandonment. A more useful system distinguishes between **structural failure** — a habit that repeatedly does not fit the person’s life — and **state-dependent failure**, where execution temporarily worsens under conditions such as acute sleep loss.

## Do not confuse less pleasure with more negativity

One of the most useful findings from this literature is that positive and negative affect are not simply opposite ends of one scale. Sleep loss can diminish positive feelings without producing an equally large increase in negative feelings.

That helps explain an experience that can otherwise be misread during self-improvement: “I do not hate this habit; I just cannot be bothered today.” The evidence suggests this state can be psychologically meaningful. Reduced attentiveness, energy or joviality may lower the pull of a planned action even if anger, sadness or anxiety have barely changed.

A small experimental study of forced nocturnal awakenings demonstrates why individual variation matters as well. Forty-two healthy adults completed nights of uninterrupted sleep and experimentally disrupted sleep. The sleep disruption did not reduce reward learning uniformly across the sample. Instead, participants whose **positive affect declined** after disruption showed poorer reward learning, while those who preserved positive affect showed a different pattern. Changes in negative affect did not explain the result in the same way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6519913/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

That study is [too small]({{ 'too-small/' | relative_url }}) to justify an individual diagnostic rule, but it reinforces the broader pattern: preservation or loss of positive affect may be particularly relevant to whether rewards continue to reinforce behaviour after poor sleep.

## Plan demanding habits around recovery, not heroic effort

The practical implication is not that every bad night should cancel every planned behaviour. Doing so would make habits fragile. The better principle is to **change the demand while preserving the behavioural thread**.

When sleep has been markedly curtailed, especially over several nights, use the next day differently:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Protect the cue, reduce the dose.** If the normal study session is 45 minutes, a shorter review may preserve the routine without demanding the same concentration.
* **Move high-effort choices when possible.** Tasks requiring planning, complex judgement or prolonged attention are stronger candidates for postponement than simple, already-defined actions.
* **Pre-decide the reduced version.** Establish what counts on a poor-sleep day before fatigue arrives — for example, “If I slept very badly, I will still open the course and do ten minutes.” This avoids renegotiating the entire goal while tired.
* **Use sleep as context when interpreting performance.** One failed session after severe sleep loss is weak evidence that a behaviour-change strategy has failed. A repeated pattern under normal sleep is more informative.
* **Return to the normal dose after recovery.** A reduced day should function as a resilience rule, not quietly become the permanent standard.

</div>

Recovery sleep itself can improve mood. In a laboratory study of 221 healthy adults, five nights restricted to four hours in bed progressively worsened mood. Participants were then assigned recovery opportunities ranging from zero to 12 hours; mood improved in a dose-responsive fashion, with larger improvements after longer recovery opportunities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11381564/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

But recovery should not be treated as an unlimited erase button. In related experimental work, improvements after recovery were transient when participants were exposed again to severe sleep restriction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/43/Supplement_1/A110/5847266" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on oup.com.</span></span></span> Among adolescents subjected to five nights of restricted sleep, two subsequent recovery nights restored some positive mood measures more readily than the increased negative moods produced by the most severe restriction condition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33245773/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Sleep duration and mood in adolescents: an experimental study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Sleep duration and mood in adolescents: an experimental study</span></span></span> The exact recovery trajectory varies by outcome, sleep dose and population.

For routine planning, prevention is therefore more dependable than repeatedly borrowing sleep and trying to repay it. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society recommend that adults generally obtain at least seven hours of sleep per night on a regular basis, while recognising individual variation and that longer sleep can be appropriate during recovery from accumulated sleep debt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aasm.org">[AASM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aasm.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aasm.org.</span></span></span>

## Treat sleep as part of the habit system

Self-improvement systems often track only whether a behaviour happened: workout completed, pages written, alcohol avoided, vegetables eaten. For demanding habits, that can miss useful information. Recording sleep alongside execution for a few weeks can reveal whether failures cluster after short or fragmented nights.

The purpose is not to turn sleep into another perfectionistic target. It is to avoid drawing the wrong conclusion from variable performance. If a habit repeatedly collapses regardless of sleep, the design may need changing. If it largely succeeds after adequate sleep and fails following substantially curtailed nights, the more rational intervention may be to protect recovery, reduce next-day cognitive demand and keep a smaller version of the routine alive.

This is the broader lesson from the sleep-and-mood evidence: behaviour is attempted from a changing internal state. Sleep loss reliably makes positive emotional states harder to sustain, can increase some negative states, and can make effort feel more costly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38127505/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sleep loss and emotion: A systematic review and meta-analysis of over 50 years of experimental research - PubMedDecember 21, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 2023</span></span></span> A behaviour-change plan that ignores those shifts asks the same performance from meaningfully different conditions.

[Self-improvement that works]({{ 'self-improvement-that-works/' | relative_url }}) therefore does not require pretending that every day is psychologically equivalent. It sets ambitious defaults for well-rested days, resilient minimums for depleted ones, and recovery rules that prevent a temporary reduction in mood or motivation from becoming a permanent abandonment of the habit.

    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
