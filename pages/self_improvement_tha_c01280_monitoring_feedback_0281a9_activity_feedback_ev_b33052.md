## Introduction

[Physical activity]({{ 'physical-capacity/' | relative_url }}) research provides an unusually direct test of whether [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) adds anything to self-monitoring. A wearable can record movement for everyone while researchers vary what participants are shown or told about those records. That makes it possible to compare tracking plus feedback with tracking that is otherwise similar but receives no additional feedback.

The best focused synthesis suggests that feedback does add something, but considerably less than an early version of the evidence implied. A corrected 2024 meta-analysis estimated a **small positive effect on physical activity, Cohen’s d = 0.29 (95% CI 0.16 to 0.43)**, when feedback was added to self-monitoring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12966-024-01569-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer LinkCorrection: Impact of feedback generation and presentation on self-monitoring behaviors, dietary intake, physical activity...</span></span></span> Individual trials help make that abstract result tangible: points-based app feedback has produced extra moderate-to-vigorous activity, while a more recent wearable experiment found about 7% more activity during feedback periods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28255750/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A smartphone &quot;app&quot;-delivered randomized factorial trial targeting physical activity in adults - PubMedMarch 2, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2017</span></span></span>

The useful conclusion is therefore restrained. Recording activity is not the whole intervention. Making those records visible, interpretable or evaluative can improve behaviour further, but the average incremental benefit is modest rather than transformative.

## What Feedback-Versus-Monitoring Trials Actually Tested

The critical comparison is easy to lose in the much larger literature on fitness trackers. A study showing that people given a wearable become more active than people receiving usual care cannot tell us whether **feedback itself** is responsible: the wearable may simultaneously introduce self-monitoring, goals, reminders, rewards and other behaviour-change techniques.

The 2024 review by Rebecca Krukowski, Andrea Denton and Laura König was designed to deal with this problem. It included randomised controlled trials in which participants self-monitored diet, physical activity or weight and researchers either added feedback or compared different forms of feedback. Across the full review, 24 articles representing 19 studies and 3,261 participants met the eligibility criteria. Crucially, intervention groups had to differ in feedback rather than in a whole package of unrelated treatment components.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10765525/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Impact of feedback generation and presentation on self-monitoring behaviors, dietary intake, physical activity, and w...</span></span></span>

For physical activity, the review could pool **nine comparisons from six studies** that provided sufficiently comparable data. The feedback itself was not one standard treatment. Depending on the trial, it could mean graphical displays, website feedback, text messages, computer-generated reports or other responses to the participant's recorded behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10765525/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Impact of feedback generation and presentation on self-monitoring behaviors, dietary intake, physical activity, and w...</span></span></span>

That variation matters. “Feedback” can describe anything from seeing progress plotted against a target to receiving a message interpreting that progress. For example, a 2016 UK experiment randomised 124 insufficiently active participants to goal setting alone, goal setting plus self-monitoring, or goal setting plus self-monitoring and feedback. The last group received feedback on recorded activity; objectively measured activity was higher than in the goal-setting-only group and marginally higher than in the self-monitoring group. The direct feedback-versus-self-monitoring contrast was **d = 0.33**, although its 95% confidence interval of −0.13 to 0.78 crossed zero.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/BJHP.12198" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">An experimental test of control theory‐based interventions for physical activity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An experimental test of control theory‐based interventions for physical activity - Prestwich - 2016 - British Journal of Health Psycho...</span></span></span>

A different 12-week factorial trial involving 116 low-active adults tested a smartphone self-monitoring app with optional goal-setting and points-based feedback modules. Everyone increased moderate-to-vigorous physical activity by more than 11 minutes per day, but participants receiving points-based feedback achieved still higher activity and more favourable app-use and psychosocial outcomes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28255750/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A smartphone &quot;app&quot;-delivered randomized factorial trial targeting physical activity in adults - PubMedMarch 2, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2017</span></span></span> This is an important distinction: the question is not whether tracking can work, but whether a feedback layer can produce an **incremental gain when tracking is already present**.

## The Corrected Estimate for Added Physical Activity

The central numerical result needs particular care because the published estimate was corrected shortly after publication.

The original January 2024 article reported a pooled feedback effect of **d = 0.73**, suggesting a fairly substantial advantage. In February, the authors issued a correction after discovering that two standard errors had been treated as standard deviations in the meta-analysis. Recalculation reduced the pooled effect to **d = 0.29**, with a 95% confidence interval from **0.16 to 0.43** and p< 0.001.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12966-024-01569-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer LinkCorrection: Impact of feedback generation and presentation on self-monitoring behaviors, dietary intake, physical activity...</span></span></span>

That correction changes the interpretation substantially. A Cohen's *d* of 0.29 represents a small standardised difference: people receiving feedback were, on average, more active than comparable people monitoring their behaviour without that added feedback, but the two groups still overlapped considerably. The revised analysis also produced **low statistical heterogeneity (I² = 9.07%)**, rather than the very high heterogeneity reported in the erroneous calculation. A trim-and-fill analysis did not identify missing studies suggestive of publication bias, although that diagnostic is necessarily limited with such a small evidence base.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12966-024-01569-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer LinkCorrection: Impact of feedback generation and presentation on self-monitoring behaviors, dietary intake, physical activity...</span></span></span>

The review's study-by-study findings reinforce the need for modest language. Six studies across its wider set of feedback-versus-no-feedback comparisons reported benefits of feedback on outcomes such as physical activity, diet goals, self-monitoring or weight, but benefits were not universal. For physical activity specifically, the authors noted that only about half of the relevant studies individually reported a significant advantage from adding feedback.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10765525/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Impact of feedback generation and presentation on self-monitoring behaviors, dietary intake, physical activity, and w...</span></span></span>

There is also a useful complementary benchmark from a broader 2022 systematic review asking what happens when **anything is added to physical-activity self-monitoring**. Across 75 studies included in its post-intervention meta-analysis, additional components produced about **926 extra steps per day** compared with self-monitoring controls; at follow-up, the difference was **413 steps per day**. Human counselling and prescribed goals were associated with larger differences than some app-, website- and print-based additions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9685716/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A systematic review and meta-analysis - PMCSeptember 7, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 7, 2022</span></span></span> Those figures should not be treated as the effect of feedback alone, because the added components included goals, counselling, incentives, texts, websites and structured [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}). They nevertheless support the same broader point: self-monitoring can be a useful base, and additional behaviour-change components can yield further gains.

## A Cleaner Wearable Test Shows the Same Direction

A 2024 field experiment provides an unusually concrete complement to the meta-analysis. Researchers used two wrist-worn devices: a Fitbit Versa, which supplied physical-activity feedback and allowed participants to monitor their progress, and a research-grade GENEActiv accelerometer, which measured activity without supplying feedback. Participants aged 50 or over experienced both conditions in different sequences, helping researchers separate the behavioural response to visible feedback from passive measurement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12165456/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Does feedback from physical activity measurement devices influence physical activity? Among the 81 participants with valid complete data, physical activity was about **7% higher on days when participants wore the feedback-providing device** than when they wore the no-feedback device; the difference was statistically significant at p< 0.001 and remained after adjustment for other factors considered by the researchers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12165456/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Does feedback from physical activity measurement devices influence physical activity? This finding is especially interesting because feedback is normally treated as a desirable feature of consumer wearables, whereas the researchers approached it partly as a **measurement problem**. If seeing your activity changes your activity, a device displaying progress is not a neutral measuring instrument. For behaviour change, that reactivity is precisely the feature of interest: information generated by monitoring feeds back into what the person subsequently does.

But this single experiment should not be read as proving that every activity display reliably causes a 7% increase. It involved only 81 complete cases, people were aged 50 or above, the intervention periods were short, and the feedback and no-feedback conditions used different devices.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12165456/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Does feedback from physical activity measurement devices influence physical activity? ## Why the Effect Is Meaningful but Not Large

A small average effect is compatible with feedback being genuinely useful. Self-monitoring already supplies some information: a person who can see today's step count is not starting from an informational vacuum. Feedback is being asked to improve on an intervention that may itself prompt reflection and behaviour change. The relevant question is therefore not “does information motivate people?” but “how much more does an additional feedback process achieve once monitoring is already happening?”

The trials also show that **feedback is not a single active ingredient with a fixed dose**. The 2024 review found interventions ranging from continuously available device displays to daily texts, weekly website feedback and much less frequent reports. Some feedback was graphical, some verbal, some personalised and some generic. Because studies varied substantially in what they delivered, the review could not establish a generally superior feedback format or pool the comparisons between human- and algorithm-generated feedback.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10765525/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Impact of feedback generation and presentation on self-monitoring behaviors, dietary intake, physical activity, and w...</span></span></span>

Nor does displaying more information automatically produce more movement. In a five-week randomised Apple Watch study involving 162 adults before exclusions and attrition, participants first experienced a week without step-count feedback and were then assigned to accurate, inflated or deflated step displays, with a fourth group receiving accurate feedback plus a mindset intervention. Simply displaying accurate step counts did **not** produce a detectable increase in steps or other measured physical activity; even substantially deflating the displayed count did not make participants compensate by becoming more active.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9909519/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

That null result is useful rather than contradictory. It shows why the pooled estimate should not be translated into “seeing your numbers makes you exercise more”. Feedback can supply a comparison, reward, prompt or interpretation, but whether that signal changes behaviour depends on what it communicates and what the recipient can do with it.

There are even plausible cases where feedback can weaken effort. In the 2016 control-theory experiment, feedback improved objectively measured activity most clearly among participants who began with weaker intentions, but self-reported data also suggested a risk of early “coasting” — easing off after believing sufficient progress had already been made.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/BJHP.12198" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">An experimental test of control theory‐based interventions for physical activity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An experimental test of control theory‐based interventions for physical activity - Prestwich - 2016 - British Journal of Health Psycho...</span></span></span> The broader review likewise cautioned that feedback can sometimes be experienced as demotivating rather than engaging, and found no clear retention advantage in the studies that examined whether feedback kept participants in an intervention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10765525/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Impact of feedback generation and presentation on self-monitoring behaviors, dietary intake, physical activity, and w...</span></span></span>

## What the Activity Evidence Supports

Taken narrowly, the physical-activity evidence supports three conclusions.

First, **feedback appears to improve physical activity beyond self-monitoring alone on average**. The best directly relevant pooled estimate is small but statistically distinguishable from zero: **d = 0.29 (95% CI 0.16 to 0.43)**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12966-024-01569-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer LinkCorrection: Impact of feedback generation and presentation on self-monitoring behaviors, dietary intake, physical activity...</span></span></span>

Second, that number is more credible than the much larger **d = 0.73** figure that may still appear in older summaries, database records or citations of the original January 2024 publication. The authors explicitly corrected the calculation after identifying the standard-error/standard-deviation mistake. The corrected figure should therefore be used when describing the evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12966-024-01569-8" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer Link]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Springer LinkCorrection: Impact of feedback generation and presentation on self-monitoring behaviors, dietary intake, physical activity...</span></span></span>

Third, the evidence does **not** justify claiming that one particular feedback design is best. Human versus automated feedback, graphical versus textual presentation, feedback frequency and personalisation remain much less securely resolved because direct comparisons are fewer and heterogeneous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-023-01555-6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on doi.org.</span></span></span>

For self-improvement, that is a more useful result than a sweeping endorsement of fitness trackers. Measurement can already influence behaviour, and adding feedback seems capable of improving it further. But the incremental advantage is best thought of as a **small extra push in a feedback loop**, not as a dramatic effect produced merely by showing someone their data. Physical activity trials make the distinction unusually visible: tracking tells a person what happened; well-designed feedback has the potential to make that observation more consequential for what happens next.
