## Introduction

Preparing your environment before a good habit begins means doing the access work while you have time and attention, so that the later moment contains as little unnecessary effort as possible. Put the book where you will read it, lay out [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) clothes, charge the equipment, fill the water bottle, open the working document, portion tomorrow’s ingredients, or decide the first task before the session starts. The point is not elaborate organisation. It is to make the useful action the next obvious action.

This fits a broader finding in behavioural science: changing effort can change behaviour, sometimes substantially. A meta-analysis of 184 field experiments involving more than 2.2 million participants found that interventions targeting effort produced stronger effects, on average, than interventions targeting intrinsic motivation, although the authors warn that publication bias may exaggerate the difference.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10685127/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)A meta-analytic cognitive framework of nudge and sludge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)A meta-analytic cognitive framework of nudge and sludge - PMCNovember 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 29, 2023</span></span></span> Preparation applies that principle at a very small, personal scale: move avoidable effort out of the vulnerable moment when the habit is supposed to begin.

## Separate access effort from productive effort

A useful distinction is between **access effort** and **productive effort**. Productive effort is inherent in the habit: running the kilometres, practising the instrument, reading the chapter, doing the exercises or cooking the meal. Access effort merely gets you into a position to do that work: locating shoes, assembling equipment, clearing a desk, finding a file, choosing a recipe or deciding what to practise.

Preparation should mainly attack the second category. Making a workout easier to *start* is different from making the workout physically undemanding. Likewise, opening tomorrow’s study materials tonight removes administrative effort without removing the intellectual effort of studying. This matters because environmental restructuring is recognised as a distinct behaviour-change technique: the Behaviour Change Technique Taxonomy defines restructuring the physical environment as changing surroundings to facilitate a wanted behaviour, while “adding objects to the environment” covers placing useful objects so that behaviour becomes easier to perform.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501960/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[NCBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An evidence synthesis and economic analysis - NCBI BookshelfMay 1, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 2018</span></span></span>

Evidence from choice architecture makes the underlying mechanism unusually concrete. A systematic review of interventions affecting NHS and other healthcare staff found that proximity interventions — making options easier or harder to reach — were frequently effective at changing food purchasing. In one included hospital intervention, margarine was placed in several accessible positions while butter had to be retrieved from a refrigerator; sales shifted accordingly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30674487/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Choice architecture interventions to improve diet and/or dietary behaviour by healthcare staff in high-income countries: a systemat...</span></span></span> A separate systematic review likewise concluded that changing the order or proximity of foods can influence food choice, while noting that stronger long-term research is needed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27185414/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nudging consumers towards healthier choices: a systematic review of positional influences on food choice - PubMedJune 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2016</span></span></span>

Those studies concern food environments rather than personal habit preparation, so they do not prove that putting trainers beside the door will establish an exercise habit. They demonstrate the narrower and more defensible principle: small differences in accessibility can alter what people do. Preparation exploits that principle by arranging accessibility in advance.

There is a second benefit. Preparation can convert a vague intention into a concrete starting condition. Research on **implementation intentions** — plans linking a specified situation to a specified response — has repeatedly found that deciding in advance when, where and how to act can improve goal attainment. A classic meta-analysis of 94 tests reported a medium-to-large overall effect, while a much larger 2024 meta-analysis covering 642 independent tests found beneficial effects across cognitive, emotional and behavioural outcomes, with stronger effects for contingent “if–then” plans.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0065260106380021" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta‐analysis of Effects and Processes - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Environmental preparation and implementation intentions are not identical. But they can reinforce each other. “I will study tomorrow” still leaves tomorrow’s self to decide where, when and how to begin. “After breakfast, I will sit at the kitchen table and work through the three questions already laid beside my laptop” has both a defined cue and a prepared [first action]({{ 'first-action/' | relative_url }}).

## What to prepare before the moment of action

The best preparation is usually the minimum that removes a predictable obstacle. Rather than trying to create a perfectly optimised environment, work backwards from the first minute of the habit and ask what could otherwise delay it.

A practical preparation policy is:

* **Put required objects at the point of use.** Leave the book on the chair where you read, the instrument ready to play, the walking shoes beside the door or the notebook on the desk. This reduces retrieval effort and can also make the object itself a reminder.
* **Make equipment operational, not merely available.** Charge the headphones, refill the bottle, replace missing supplies, load the dishwasher before tomorrow’s cooking session or install the software before the planned work period.
* **Remove preliminary decisions.** Choose tomorrow’s workout, reading section, practice exercise or first work task beforehand. The later session can then begin with execution rather than selection.
* **Prepare the first physical action.** A document can already be open at the relevant section; ingredients can be grouped together; exercise kit can be laid out. Preparing the beginning is generally more valuable than trying to script every later step.
* **Attach the setup to a stable context.** If possible, leave the prepared materials where and when the behaviour is meant to happen rather than creating a separate reminder that must itself be remembered.
* **Reset after finishing.** Where practical, make restoring the environment part of the previous repetition: put the next tablet beside the breakfast bowl, return the book to the reading chair or replenish supplies after using them. Preparation then becomes part of a repeating loop rather than a separate project.

Research into how people actually choose cues for new routines supports this approach. In a three-week UK study, 39 participants trying to take vitamin C daily commonly selected cues that minimised effort, such as keeping the tablets nearby or visible. The researchers also found that vague plans and reliance on a single cue could be unreliable, and that participants often needed trial and error before finding a cue arrangement that worked in everyday life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7106637/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)What influences the selection of contextual cues when starting a new routine behaviour? An exploratory study - PMCMar...</span></span></span>

That last finding is important because environmental preparation is sometimes presented as though there must be one clever setup that eliminates [self-control]({{ 'self-control/' | relative_url }}) problems. Real routines are messier. A book placed on a bedside table may work until bedtime becomes irregular; gym clothes laid out at night may help until morning exercise starts conflicting with childcare or commuting. Preparation should therefore be treated as a small behavioural experiment: notice where the start keeps failing, remove that particular obstacle, and see whether repetition becomes easier.

## Prepare cues as well as equipment

A prepared environment does more than save seconds. Repeated behaviour can become associated with recurring contextual cues. A major review of habit psychology describes habits as responses developed through repetition in recurring contexts, rather than simply behaviours performed with strong motivation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.annualreviews.org/docserver/fulltext/psych/67/1/annurev-psych-122414-033417.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: annualreviews.org">[Annual Reviews]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">annualreviews.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Annual Reviews Psychology of Habit &#124; Annual Reviews</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Annual Reviews Psychology of Habit &#124; Annual Reviews</span></span></span>

Experimental and longitudinal evidence points in the same direction. In research on intentional habit building, one study followed 95 university students across 2,482 repetitions of study habits, while another analysed 308 habits and 2,368 repetitions from an app. Greater context stability predicted greater [automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }}) and better achievement of repetition goals in both datasets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.883795/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on frontiersin.org.</span></span></span> Earlier real-world research likewise asked participants to repeat a chosen eating, drinking or activity behaviour in the same context over 12 weeks and observed increases in reported automaticity over repeated performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiley.com.</span></span></span>

This suggests a reason not to move the prepared setup around unnecessarily. If reading is meant to happen after breakfast, having the book consistently waiting in the same useful place may be more valuable than storing it neatly out of sight and retrieving it from somewhere different each day. The environment is doing two jobs: reducing access effort now and helping preserve a recognisable context for later repetitions.

There is an important limit to that claim. **Preparation itself does not create the habit.** The relevant learning comes from repeatedly performing the target behaviour in relation to its context. Laboratory work on context-response associations similarly finds that practice strengthens associations that can subsequently support performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38047612/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Measuring context-response associations that drive habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Measuring context-response associations that drive habits</span></span></span> An immaculate desk prepared every evening is therefore not evidence of a writing habit if no writing follows.

## Design for the low-energy version of the day

Preparation has its greatest practical value when it anticipates predictable variation in motivation and attention. On an easy day, finding a charger, selecting a workout or clearing a desk may barely matter. On a rushed or tiring day, the same chain of preliminary actions can become the point at which the intended behaviour disappears.

The useful question is therefore not “What would make this routine perfect?” but “What is likely to stop me reaching the useful part when conditions are poor?” If the answer is that exercise clothes are never clean, preparation means managing the laundry cycle. If cooking fails because there is nothing ready to cook, it means ensuring that the necessary food is available before hunger arrives. If study repeatedly stalls while deciding what to study, preparation means choosing the next concrete unit in advance.

This approach is consistent with the broader evidence on effort-based interventions. The large field-experiment meta-analysis found that altering effort was an important route through which choice environments changed behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10685127/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)A meta-analytic cognitive framework of nudge and sludge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)A meta-analytic cognitive framework of nudge and sludge - PMCNovember 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 29, 2023</span></span></span> Reviews of food choice architecture similarly find that availability and proximity can matter, although results vary by intervention and study quality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27185414/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nudging consumers towards healthier choices: a systematic review of positional influences on food choice - PubMedJune 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2016</span></span></span> The lesson is not that humans always choose the easiest option. It is that unnecessary effort is a behavioural variable rather than harmless background detail.

Good preparation therefore tends to be mundane. A runner does not need an elaborate “performance station”; having usable shoes, appropriate clothing and a known first step may be enough. A person trying to read nightly may need nothing more sophisticated than leaving the current book where the evening routine naturally reaches it. A writer may benefit more from ending today’s session with tomorrow’s file open and a one-line starting instruction than from redesigning the entire office.

## When preparation turns into procrastination

Preparation stops reducing friction when it becomes another activity competing with the habit itself. Colour-coding study materials, repeatedly reorganising a workspace, researching the ideal exercise programme or spending an hour constructing a productivity system can feel related to the desired behaviour while postponing its productive component.

That danger deserves attention because people are not consistently good at allocating preparatory effort. Research on the planning fallacy shows that people commonly underestimate the work and time required for future tasks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nber.org/papers/w14228" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nber.org">[National Bureau of Economic Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nber.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nber.org.</span></span></span> Research on procrastination also shows that people recognise self-control problems and sometimes impose structures such as deadlines on themselves, but do not necessarily choose those structures optimally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/1467-9280.00441/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychologicalscience.org">[Association for Psychological Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychologicalscience.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on psychologicalscience.org.</span></span></span> These findings concern planning and procrastination broadly rather than environmental setup specifically, but they give good reason not to assume that more preparation is automatically better.

A useful boundary is **start-readiness**. Preparation is doing its job when the environment is ready enough that the target behaviour can begin with little avoidable delay. Beyond that point, additional setup should have a clear payoff. If the shoes are ready, route chosen and bottle filled, researching another running accessory is unlikely to reduce tomorrow’s starting friction. If the document, notes and first task are already open, further rearranging the desk may simply delay writing.

There is also a risk of making the setup too fragile. A routine requiring a particular playlist, spotless room, precisely arranged equipment and lengthy warm-up ritual has accumulated dependencies rather than removed them. The stronger design is usually one with fewer prerequisites. Preparation should make the threshold lower, not create a ceremonial gateway that must be completed perfectly.

## Make preparation earn its place

The simplest test is counterfactual: **if this setup were not done beforehand, what extra action would be required when the habit was due to start?** If there is a clear answer, preparation probably has value. Charging the device removes a charging delay; placing the materials at the desk removes a search; choosing the first exercise removes a decision. If no meaningful obstacle disappears, the setup may be cosmetic.

That produces a compact policy for preparing ahead: identify the first useful action, remove the predictable access steps before it, place necessary objects in the intended context, decide only what must be decided in advance, and stop preparing once immediate action is possible. Then use actual failures to refine the environment rather than continually redesigning it in theory.

The distinction is small but important for [self-improvement that works]({{ 'self-improvement-that-works/' | relative_url }}). The goal is not to become exceptionally good at preparing to exercise, study, cook, practise or write. It is to arrange the environment so that, when the intended moment arrives, there is less standing between intention and the first productive action.
