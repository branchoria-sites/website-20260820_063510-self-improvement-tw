## Introduction

The [time of day]({{ 'time-of-day/' | relative_url }}) may affect how quickly a repeated behaviour becomes automatic, but the evidence does **not** support a simple rule that morning habits are always easier to form than evening ones. The clearest direct evidence comes from one small 2017 experiment in which students repeated the same stretching exercise either after waking or before bed. Researchers estimated that [automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }}) developed substantially sooner in the morning group: about **106 days versus 154 days**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28650196/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Effects of circadian cortisol on the development of a health habit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of circadian cortisol on the development of a health habit - PubMedNovember 1, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2017</span></span></span>

That difference is interesting because the behaviour itself was held broadly constant while its place in the daily routine changed. The researchers proposed that higher morning cortisol helped explain the result. Yet the study was small, its habit-formation dates extended beyond the 90-day observation period, and the morning and evening cues were not perfectly equivalent. Later research also suggests that routine stability, individual morning-or-evening preference and the nature of the behaviour may matter. The useful lesson is therefore about **finding a favourable, repeatable context**, not automatically moving every self-improvement habit to breakfast time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/kognitionspsychologie/publikationen-alt/fournier2017-healthpsych.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psy.uni-hamburg.de">[psy.uni-hamburg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psy.uni-hamburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fournier2017 healthpsych</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fournier2017 healthpsych</span></span></span>

## What the morning-versus-evening stretching study found

Marion Fournier and colleagues recruited 48 students and randomly assigned them to practise a psoas iliac stretch once a day for 90 days. One group stretched in bed after waking; the other did so before sleeping. Participants recorded their behaviour and completed a daily measure of automaticity using a smartphone app, while saliva samples taken every 30 days allowed the researchers to measure cortisol. Forty-two participants completed the experiment: 19 in the morning group and 23 in the evening group. Adherence was high, at 94.9%, and did not significantly differ between groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/kognitionspsychologie/publikationen-alt/fournier2017-healthpsych.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psy.uni-hamburg.de">[psy.uni-hamburg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psy.uni-hamburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fournier2017 healthpsych</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fournier2017 healthpsych</span></span></span>

The researchers fitted learning curves to each participant's automaticity scores and defined habit formation as reaching 95% of the person's modelled maximum automaticity. On that measure, the morning group was estimated to reach the threshold after an average **105.95 days**, compared with **154.01 days** for the evening group. Morning cortisol concentrations were also higher, averaging 2.16 ng/ml compared with 1.10 ng/ml in the evening group. Statistical mediation analysis suggested that cortisol accounted for part of the relationship between time of day and faster automaticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/kognitionspsychologie/publikationen-alt/fournier2017-healthpsych.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psy.uni-hamburg.de">[psy.uni-hamburg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psy.uni-hamburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fournier2017 healthpsych</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fournier2017 healthpsych</span></span></span>

There is an important detail behind those memorable numbers: participants were followed for only **90 days**. The portions of the fitted curves after day 90 were extrapolations, including both average estimates of the point at which 95% automaticity was reached. In other words, researchers did not literally observe the average morning participant becoming habitual on day 106 and the average evening participant on day 154. They observed 90 days of changing automaticity and used those data to estimate where the curves would subsequently reach the specified threshold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/kognitionspsychologie/publikationen-alt/fournier2017-healthpsych.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psy.uni-hamburg.de">[psy.uni-hamburg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psy.uni-hamburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fournier2017 healthpsych</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fournier2017 healthpsych</span></span></span>

That distinction matters when this study is used in discussions about how many days habits take to form. A 2024 systematic review identified only four studies that directly reported a mean or median time to health-habit formation. It included the stretching study's 106- and 154-day figures but emphasised that the evidence base for habit-formation timelines remains modest and heterogeneous. Of the 20 studies in the broader review, 11 were judged to have a high risk of bias.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## Why timing might affect automaticity

The stretching experiment offers one biological explanation. Cortisol follows a strong daily rhythm: in healthy people, much of its secretion occurs around morning awakening, including a rapid rise during roughly the first 30–45 minutes after waking. Cortisol is involved in systems governing metabolism, cognition and responses to daily demands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.endocrine.org/journals/endocrine-reviews/the-cortisol-awakening-response" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: endocrine.org">[Endocrine Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">endocrine.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Society The Cortisol Awakening Response &#124; Endocrine Society</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Endocrine SocietyThe Cortisol Awakening Response &#124; Endocrine SocietyOctober 1, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1, 2024</span></span></span>

Fournier and colleagues therefore proposed that performing the stretch during this higher-cortisol period could favour the learning processes involved in automatic behaviour. Their mediation results were consistent with that possibility. Separate laboratory research has also connected cortisol responses with greater reliance on habitual rather than goal-directed behaviour under some experimental conditions, although those studies investigate different processes and cannot by themselves prove that ordinary morning routines become habits faster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28650196/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Effects of circadian cortisol on the development of a health habit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of circadian cortisol on the development of a health habit - PubMedNovember 1, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2017</span></span></span>

The biology is not the only plausible explanation, however. **Morning and evening can provide different behavioural contexts.** A habit becomes easier to trigger automatically when the relevant cues and surroundings recur reliably. Research specifically manipulating context stability has found that people building habits in more stable contexts reported greater automaticity; an additional observational dataset showed a similar association.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9226889/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Context Stability in Habit Building Increases Automaticity and Goal Attainment - PMCJune 10, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 10, 2022</span></span></span>

For some people, mornings contain unusually predictable anchors: waking, getting out of bed, showering, making breakfast or starting work. Evening schedules may accumulate more variation from work finishing late, social plans, travel, tiredness or changing bedtimes. That makes it plausible that a morning behaviour could receive a cleaner and more consistently encountered cue. Researchers examining medication habits have explicitly proposed this morning-stability explanation, although they also stress that it has not been established as a general mechanism.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/abm/article/55/3/280/5857734" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OUP AcademicTime-of-Day Differences in Treatment-Related Habit Strength and Adherence &#124; Annals of Behavioral Medicine &#124; Oxford AcademicJu...</span></span></span>

There is supporting evidence for a morning advantage in *performing* planned behaviours, but it should not be confused with direct evidence of faster habit formation. In one study of 92 people making fruit-and-vegetable action plans, enactment declined over the course of the day. Similarly, an observational analysis of 14,879 users of a meditation app found that morning meditation was associated with greater persistence: over the first 180 days, the probability of meditation declined by 63.4% for morning sessions compared with reductions of 67.8% to 74.5% at other times. Because users chose when to meditate, however, this cannot establish that morning timing caused better persistence or faster automaticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/author/6506029556/nina-knoll" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Nina Knoll &#124; ScienceDirect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirect Nina Knoll &#124; ScienceDirect</span></span></span>

Taken together, these findings point towards at least two plausible routes by which time of day could matter: **circadian biology may alter the conditions under which automaticity develops, while daily schedules may make some time periods more reliable sources of cues than others**. The existing evidence does not yet establish how much each contributes.

## Why one timing result should not become another rule

The most important limitation of the stretching study is its scale. Only 42 participants completed it, and the authors themselves called for replication because of the relatively small sample and attrition. They also acknowledged that cortisol did not fully explain the findings and that other psychological or contextual factors could have contributed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/kognitionspsychologie/publikationen-alt/fournier2017-healthpsych.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psy.uni-hamburg.de">[psy.uni-hamburg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psy.uni-hamburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fournier2017 healthpsych</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fournier2017 healthpsych</span></span></span>

There was also a subtle asymmetry in the instructions. Morning participants stretched **after waking**, giving them a preceding event that could act as a clear cue. Evening participants were told to stretch **before going to bed**, which does not provide an equivalent preceding cue. Because reliable cues are central to habit learning, the researchers acknowledged that this difference could have strengthened automaticity in the morning condition independently of cortisol.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psy.uni-hamburg.de/arbeitsbereiche/kognitionspsychologie/publikationen-alt/fournier2017-healthpsych.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psy.uni-hamburg.de">[psy.uni-hamburg.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psy.uni-hamburg.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fournier2017 healthpsych</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fournier2017 healthpsych</span></span></span>

More recent evidence makes a universal morning prescription still harder to defend. A 2024 randomised study of 317 young women examined planning interventions for calcium supplementation and [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) while also considering participants' morningness or eveningness. Behavioural engagement depended partly on the combination of **behaviour, planned timing and individual diurnal preference**: morning-type participants did better with morning cues for exercise, while evening types did better with evening cues. For calcium supplementation, the pattern was different. The study reinforces the idea that the best timing can depend on both the person and the task rather than on the clock alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39014034/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evaluating the impact of individuals&#x27; morningness-eveningness on the effectiveness of a habit-formation intervention for a simple a...</span></span></span>

Evidence from medication routines provides another useful check. In an observational study of 51 people taking twice-daily medication for type 2 diabetes, participants missed fewer morning doses than evening doses. Yet the [exact timing]({{ 'exact-timing/' | relative_url }}) of doses — used as a proxy for habitual [consistency]({{ 'consistency/' | relative_url }}) — was **not significantly less variable in the morning**. Better morning adherence therefore did not clearly translate into evidence of stronger morning habits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32542355/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Even consistency itself is more nuanced than “same minute every day”. The large meditation-app study found morning practice associated with longer persistence, but greater long-term persistence was also associated with **more flexibility in the exact timing** of meditation sessions. That suggests that repeatedly fitting a behaviour into a workable part of the day may sometimes be more sustainable than rigidly protecting an exact clock time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e42482" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jmir.org">[JMIR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jmir.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Journal of Medical Internet Research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Journal of Medical Internet Research</span></span></span>

For practical self-improvement, then, the stretching experiment should change the question rather than supply another magic formula. Instead of asking, “Are habits formed faster in the morning?”, a more evidence-aligned question is: **“At what point in my day can this behaviour repeatedly meet a clear, stable cue with the least friction?”** For one person that may be immediately after waking; for another it may be after arriving home, finishing dinner or brushing their teeth at night.

The morning-versus-evening result is valuable precisely because it shows why [habit timelines]({{ 'habit-timelines/' | relative_url }}) cannot be reduced to a universal number of days. Even the same simple stretch, repeated daily, followed different estimated automaticity trajectories when embedded at different points in the day. But turning **106 versus 154 days** into “morning habits form 48 days faster” would repeat the mistake behind the [21-day myth]({{ '21-day-myth/' | relative_url }}): converting a specific finding from a limited study into a general rule. The stronger conclusion is narrower and more useful — **timing can be one of the conditions that changes the pace of habit formation, and the best timing is likely to depend on biology, context, behaviour and the individual.**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>
