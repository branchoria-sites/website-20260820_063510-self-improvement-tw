## Introduction

A small amount of preparation before stopping work can make tomorrow’s first task materially easier to begin. The useful move is not to plan the whole next day in detail. It is to remove the first few decisions tomorrow will otherwise demand: decide what you will start, identify the first visible action, leave the relevant materials ready, and record anything that must be remembered. This shifts effort from the moment of initiation to a calmer moment when you are already engaged with the work.

The evidence comes from several related strands of research. Specific plans can improve goal initiation; external reminders can reduce reliance on [prospective memory]({{ 'remembering/' | relative_url }}); daily work planning can support performance; and making concrete plans for unfinished work can reduce the tendency for those tasks to keep intruding after hours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0065260106380021" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta‐analysis of Effects and Processes - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

The practical implication is narrow but useful: before you finish today, leave tomorrow with an obvious place to begin.

## Why starting friction matters

Starting a work task is often harder than continuing one because the opening minutes can contain several hidden decisions. Which task comes first? Which document is current? What was I trying to work out? Which tab, file or dataset do I need? What counts as the [next action]({{ 'next-action/' | relative_url }})? Each question is small, but together they create a miniature planning session before any substantive work has begun.

Research on implementation intentions helps explain why advance decisions can matter. An implementation intention links a future situation to a specified response — in effect, deciding in advance what action follows when a particular cue appears. A major meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found a medium-to-large overall benefit for goal attainment and reported effects specifically on initiating goal-directed behaviour. The proposed mechanism is not simply stronger motivation: specifying the cue and response makes the relevant opportunity easier to recognise and the intended response more automatic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0065260106380021" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta‐analysis of Effects and Processes - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

For tomorrow’s work, the same principle can be used without turning the evening into a formal planning ritual. “Work on the report tomorrow” still leaves initiation unresolved. “When I sit down at 09:00, I will open the draft and rewrite the first paragraph of the findings section” has already settled the cue and the [first action]({{ 'first-action/' | relative_url }}). The distinction is between preserving an intention and preparing its execution.

There is also a memory problem. Remembering that something must be done later is known as prospective memory, and research shows that it becomes less reliable when attention and executive resources are occupied by demanding ongoing tasks. Externalising an intention — into a reminder, calendar, note or strategically positioned object — can substantially improve the chances that the intended action is carried out. Reviews of “intention offloading” conclude that external reminders are highly effective, while experimental work has found benefits from both self-generated and externally supplied reminders.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9971128/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Outsourcing Memory to External Tools: A Review of ‘Intention Offloading’ - PMC...</span></span></span>

That makes a prepared workspace more than tidiness. A note at the top of a document saying “Next: compare the June and July figures” is an external memory cue. So is leaving the relevant spreadsheet open, putting the notebook on the keyboard, or placing the file you need at the top of tomorrow’s working folder. The environment carries part of the remembering burden.

## Prepare the first visible next step

The most useful end-of-day preparation usually answers one question: **what should be visibly ready when work resumes?** A good answer is concrete enough that tomorrow’s self can act before reconsidering the whole project.

Suppose you are halfway through writing a proposal. Weak preparation would be to add “continue proposal” to a task list. Better preparation would be to leave the proposal open at the exact section that needs attention and add a short note: “Next: turn the three client objections into headings.” Tomorrow’s first action is then obvious.

The same method transfers easily across different kinds of knowledge work:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Writing:** stop with a sentence describing the next paragraph, argument or source to add.
* **Analysis:** record the next comparison, calculation or data-cleaning step, and leave the relevant file identifiable.
* **Coding:** leave a short note naming the failing test, function or next experiment rather than only “continue debugging”.
* **Administration:** pre-select the first form, email thread or document that needs action.
* **Creative work:** leave a rough sketch, placeholder, question or unfinished transition that gives the next session something concrete to respond to.
* **Meeting-heavy work:** prepare the first independent task before closing down, so the next open period does not begin with another round of prioritising.

</div>

These examples are applications of the same mechanism rather than claims that every workplace should use an identical routine. The goal is to convert a broad project state into a small executable action.

Research on daily planning supports the broader value of specifying tasks, priorities and when work will be done. In a two-week experience-sampling study, Parke and colleagues distinguished ordinary time-management planning — making task lists, setting priorities and deciding how and when to perform tasks — from contingent planning, which anticipates interruptions. Time-management planning was associated with greater engagement and performance, although its benefit weakened when interruptions were frequent; contingent planning was more resilient under interruption.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29154579/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When daily planning improves employee performance: The importance of planning type, engagement, and interruptions - PubMed...</span></span></span>

That finding adds an important qualification. Preparing tomorrow’s first task is most useful when the plan survives contact with the actual day. If mornings are routinely disrupted, “write for two uninterrupted hours at 09:00” may be brittle. A better setup could be: “When I get my first 25-minute uninterrupted block, open the draft at the marked section and write the three objection headings.” The preparation remains concrete without pretending that the schedule is fully controllable.

## Unfinished work does not need to remain mentally open

End-of-day preparation may also help with what happens after work. Unfinished tasks are associated with continued work-related thinking during off-job time. A 2026 meta-analysis covering 17 between-person samples and 14 within-person samples found that unfinished work was positively related to off-job work thoughts, with the strongest associations for affective rumination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41554526/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unfinished work tasks and work-related thoughts during off-job time: meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik effect in a work-recovery conte...</span></span></span>

This does not mean every unfinished task will ruin an evening, nor that all work should be completed before stopping. Many jobs make that impossible. The more interesting evidence is that an unfinished goal can become easier to set aside once a credible plan exists for resuming it.

In one influential series of experiments, people with activated but unfinished goals showed more intrusive thoughts and poorer performance on unrelated activities. When participants were allowed to form specific plans for completing those goals, the cognitive interference effects disappeared. The researchers argued that making a plan can satisfy some of the mental processes that would otherwise keep the unfulfilled goal active.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Workplace research reaches a similar conclusion. Brandon Smit followed 103 employees pursuing 1,127 work goals and found that people had greater difficulty mentally detaching from incomplete goals, particularly important ones. An experimental intervention asking employees to specify where, when and how they would complete unfinished work improved detachment among those who were normally prone to carrying work mentally into leisure time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/joop.12137" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS Psychology Hub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS Psychology HubSuccessfully leaving work at work: The self‐regulatory underpinnings of psychological detachment - Smit - 2016 - Journa...</span></span></span>

So an end-of-day note has two possible jobs. It can guide tomorrow’s start, but it can also tell today’s brain that the unfinished task has not been abandoned. “Finish presentation” is vague enough to remain mentally demanding. “Tomorrow, after the 10:00 call, open slide 8 and replace the temporary chart with the final quarterly figures” provides a defined continuation point.

The distinction matters because productive preparation is not the same as continuing to work late. The purpose is to create closure around the restart point, not to squeeze in another hour of substantive work.

## Keep the setup brief

There is a risk of turning preparation into another form of procrastination. A person can spend 40 minutes reorganising folders, refining tomorrow’s task list and rebuilding a project dashboard while avoiding the simpler act of deciding what to do first.

The stronger approach is deliberately small. For most work, a useful shutdown setup can fit into a few minutes:

1. **Choose tomorrow’s first meaningful task.** Prefer one that advances an important piece of work rather than merely clearing something easy.
2. **Write the next physical or visible action.** “Analyse customer [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }})” is still a project description; “open the survey export and group the free-text responses into five themes” is a start.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

3. **Put the required material within immediate reach.** Open the file, bookmark the source, place the notebook out, or collect the relevant documents.
4. **Record anything you would otherwise need to remember.** Names, figures, questions, unresolved decisions and where you stopped belong outside your head.
5. **Stop.** Preparation has done its job once tomorrow can begin without reconstructing today’s mental state.

</div>

This brevity is consistent with the evidence on planning. Specificity helps when it links a situation to an executable response, but planning is not automatically valuable merely because more of it is done. Research on daily planning shows that circumstances such as interruptions affect how useful a plan remains, while research on prospective memory suggests that well-chosen cues can reduce the need for continuous mental monitoring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29154579/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When daily planning improves employee performance: The importance of planning type, engagement, and interruptions - PubMed...</span></span></span>

A useful test is therefore: **Will this preparation save decisions at the point of tomorrow’s start?** If the answer is yes, it probably belongs. If it merely makes the workspace prettier or the planning system more elaborate, its value is less certain.

## What this can and cannot do

Preparing tomorrow’s first step is a friction-reduction technique, not a cure for every form of procrastination or poor performance. It will not fix an impossible workload, an unclear project brief, chronic interruption, low skill, conflicting priorities or a task you have strong reasons to avoid. Research on [choice overload]({{ 'choice-overload/' | relative_url }}) also cautions against simplistic claims that every additional decision necessarily produces “[decision fatigue]({{ 'decision-fatigue/' | relative_url }})”: whether choice becomes burdensome depends on factors including task difficulty, preference uncertainty and the complexity of the options.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1057740814000916" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirect Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis</span></span></span>

The more defensible claim is narrower. When tomorrow’s task is already worthwhile and sufficiently defined, deciding its first step today can remove avoidable uncertainty at the moment of initiation. External cues can carry part of the memory burden; if–then planning can strengthen the link between a situation and an action; and specific plans for unfinished goals can make it easier to stop mentally rehearsing them after work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0065260106380021" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta‐analysis of Effects and Processes - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

That is why the best version of this habit is modest. Do not try to design tomorrow in full. Leave yourself a runway: one chosen task, one visible next action, the necessary materials, and a clear cue for when to begin.
