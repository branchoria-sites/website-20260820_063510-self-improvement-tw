## Introduction

Practised habits can be especially useful when attention is occupied. If a familiar cue has repeatedly been followed by the same response, encountering that cue can begin to activate the response directly, reducing the need to hold a goal in working memory, reconsider what to do and consciously retrieve each step. That is why a well-rehearsed routine may keep moving when someone is distracted, hurried or simultaneously dealing with another task. Reviews of habit research describe this rapid cue-driven activation as a defining feature of habit memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34283681/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Habits and Goals in Human Behavior: Separate but Interacting Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habits and Goals in Human Behavior: Separate but Interacting Systems - PubMedMarch 1, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2022</span></span></span>

A particularly revealing experiment tested this idea by having people practise a 16-step virtual sushi-making task and then repeat it while simultaneously attending to a distracting secondary task. More practice strengthened objectively measured cue–response associations, and stronger associations predicted more successful performance under this cognitive load. The important point for self-improvement is not that habits make distraction harmless. It is narrower and more useful: **practice can shift some of the work of initiating a familiar response away from moment-to-moment deliberation and towards cues that are already present.**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jeab.893" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Measuring context–response associations that drive habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeasuring context–response associations that drive habits - Labrecque - 2024 - Journal of the Experimental Analysis o...</span></span></span>

## What cognitive load reveals about automatic responding

Mental distraction provides a useful test of [automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }}) because deliberate control has limited capacity. Imagine trying to perform a familiar routine while also keeping track of a conversation, remembering an instruction or monitoring something else. The secondary task competes for attention and working memory that could otherwise be used to recall the goal, work out the [next action]({{ 'next-action/' | relative_url }}) and check each choice.

A genuinely well-practised response has an advantage in that situation. Habit theories propose that repeated performance builds associations in memory between features of the context and particular responses. Once those associations are sufficiently accessible, seeing the relevant cue can rapidly bring the associated response to mind. Wood, Mazar and Neal characterise habit memory in part by this rapid activation: habitual responses can be activated irrespective of whether a person is currently deliberating about the goal that originally motivated them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34283681/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Habits and Goals in Human Behavior: Separate but Interacting Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habits and Goals in Human Behavior: Separate but Interacting Systems - PubMedMarch 1, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2022</span></span></span>

This creates an important contrast:

**Deliberation-heavy action:** cue → remember the goal → retrieve or choose the response → act.

**Practised cue-response action:** familiar cue → practised response becomes accessible → act.

Real behaviour rarely falls perfectly into either category. Complex activities can combine deliberate decisions with highly practised components. Nor does automaticity necessarily mean complete unconsciousness. The useful distinction is how much online cognitive work is required to produce the next appropriate response. Practice can make some of those computations less demanding even while conscious supervision remains possible. A review by Adrian Haith and John Krakauer therefore cautions against treating skill, habit and reduced cognitive load as exactly the same phenomenon: practice can affect all three, but they can be partly dissociable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6443249/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)The multiple effects of practice: skill, habit and reduced cognitive load - PMCMarch 1, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2018</span></span></span>

This distinction also explains why distraction can expose the difference between knowing what to do and having a response that is readily triggered. A person may understand a routine perfectly when concentrating on it yet fail to retrieve the right action when attention is divided. A stronger cue–response association provides another route to performance, one less dependent on reconstructing the plan at that moment.

## How practice strengthened cue-response associations in experiments

Labrecque, Lee and Wood's 2024 experiments offer unusually direct evidence because they did not simply ask participants whether a behaviour *felt* habitual. Participants learnt a computerised sushi-making sequence containing 16 steps. Some received little practice; others repeated the sequence roughly ten times. The researchers then separately measured how quickly participants could identify the correct response after seeing a cue from the sequence. Faster correct responses served as an objective indicator of stronger cue–response associations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jeab.893" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Measuring context–response associations that drive habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeasuring context–response associations that drive habits - Labrecque - 2024 - Journal of the Experimental Analysis o...</span></span></span>

The final test deliberately made thoughtful retrieval harder. Participants had to reproduce the sushi sequence without the earlier instructions while a second screen played attention-grabbing university football highlights. They were required to listen for the announcer's calls of a touchdown and remember the total, creating a genuine dual-task demand rather than merely telling participants to hurry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jeab.893" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Measuring context–response associations that drive habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeasuring context–response associations that drive habits - Labrecque - 2024 - Journal of the Experimental Analysis o...</span></span></span>

More practice strengthened the measured cue–response associations. Crucially, those stronger associations predicted better reproduction of the practised sequence under cognitive load even after the analyses accounted for the amount of practice and participants' intentions to perform correctly. The researchers also examined other possible motivational explanations, including perceived ease, [enjoyment]({{ 'enjoyment/' | relative_url }}), attitudes and fluency. Their central conclusion was that repetition aided performance by creating associations through which recurring contextual cues could activate practised responses, rather than simply making participants more determined to succeed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jeab.893" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Measuring context–response associations that drive habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeasuring context–response associations that drive habits - Labrecque - 2024 - Journal of the Experimental Analysis o...</span></span></span>

The study also contains a useful caution against oversimplification. In the second experiment, stronger cue–response associations were associated with better performance on the distracting secondary task as well, consistent with the idea that automaticity freed some capacity; that relationship did not appear in the first experiment. The evidence therefore supports reduced dependence on deliberation more strongly than a sweeping claim that habits always release a predictable quantity of spare attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jeab.893" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Measuring context–response associations that drive habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeasuring context–response associations that drive habits - Labrecque - 2024 - Journal of the Experimental Analysis o...</span></span></span>

The experimental task was also far simpler and shorter-lived than most important self-improvement behaviours. Participants were university students practising a computerised sequence in controlled conditions. Ten repetitions of a sushi game cannot establish how much rehearsal is required to make [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}), studying or household routines robust to distraction. What the experiments contribute is a cleaner demonstration of the **mechanism**: repeated cue–response pairings can become measurable associations, and those associations can support performance when deliberate processing is deliberately burdened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jeab.893" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Measuring context–response associations that drive habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeasuring context–response associations that drive habits - Labrecque - 2024 - Journal of the Experimental Analysis o...</span></span></span>

## Where automaticity can reduce reliance on deliberate control

The practical implication is to automate the parts of a useful behaviour that should remain predictable when life becomes cognitively crowded. A person who wants to read every evening, for example, gains relatively little protection from distraction by repeatedly deciding, “I should read tonight.” A more habit-compatible arrangement repeatedly puts the same response after a recognisable event: after putting a particular evening drink down, open the book already placed beside the chair. The valuable learning is not simply repetition of *reading*; it is repetition of **this cue followed by this response**.

This suggests three implementation priorities.

* **Practise under a recognisable cue.** Repetition is most useful for cue-driven automaticity when the context contains recurring features capable of retrieving the response. Stable performance contexts are therefore important to both forming and expressing habits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352250X24000939" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Beyond deliberate self-control: Habits automatically achieve long-term goals - ScienceDirectDecember 1, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2024</span></span></span>
* **Make the first response unambiguous.** “Work on my fitness” leaves substantial choice for a distracted mind. “After I put on my morning clothes, put on my trainers and go outside” gives the recurring situation a more specific response to retrieve. The point is not that every later action must be rigidly scripted, but that initiation need not require a fresh decision.
* **Rehearse the association before depending on it under pressure.** Automaticity is a product of learning. A new routine that still requires careful [remembering]({{ 'remembering/' | relative_url }}) cannot reasonably be expected to perform like an established habit merely because the cue has been chosen well. Labrecque and colleagues found stronger cue–response associations after greater practice, not simply after participants were instructed to memorise the sequence carefully.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jeab.893" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Measuring context–response associations that drive habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryMeasuring context–response associations that drive habits - Labrecque - 2024 - Journal of the Experimental Analysis o...</span></span></span>

A related technique, an implementation intention — an explicit “if situation X occurs, then I will perform response Y” plan — illustrates how deliberately linking cues and responses can support efficient action initiation. Classic experiments found that implementation intentions could help participants initiate specified responses even under experimentally imposed cognitive load, and the US National Cancer Institute's review of this literature describes the process as strategically delegating behavioural control to preselected situational cues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11708569/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Implementation intentions and efficient action initiation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Implementation intentions and efficient action initiation</span></span></span> These plans are not identical to habits acquired through repeated behaviour, however. Research has found important differences and limitations, including evidence that implementation intentions can sometimes impair flexibility or fail to produce the same effects under very high cognitive demands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10585941/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## Under load, habits preserve the response you actually practised

Automaticity is not inherently beneficial. Cognitive load does not selectively protect *good* routines; it can also make established unwanted responses harder to override. Reviews of everyday habit performance conclude that pressures such as distraction, fatigue, stress and time pressure can interfere with slower goal-directed control while leaving familiar habitual responses comparatively intact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.jcps.2009.08.003" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[My SCP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">My SCPThe habitual consumer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">My SCPThe habitual consumer</span></span></span>

That creates a practical asymmetry. Suppose someone has repeatedly responded to a difficult work transition by opening a distracting website. On an unusually calm day, a deliberate intention to concentrate may be sufficient to suppress that response. During a hectic day, however, reduced capacity for deliberate control can leave the old cue–response pathway relatively advantaged. Conversely, if the repeated response to the same transition has become opening the next task document and working for five minutes, cognitive load can favour something useful. The protective property belongs to the learned association, not to the virtue of the behaviour.

There is also a trade-off between efficiency and flexibility. Rapid activation is valuable when the familiar cue still calls for the familiar response. It becomes a liability when circumstances change. Research on habit and goal systems emphasises that habit memories are not only quick to activate but relatively resistant to change, while experimental work on deliberately strengthened cue–response plans has likewise raised concerns about reduced behavioural flexibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34283681/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Habits and Goals in Human Behavior: Separate but Interacting Systems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habits and Goals in Human Behavior: Separate but Interacting Systems - PubMedMarch 1, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2022</span></span></span>

For self-improvement, the best candidates for strong automaticity are therefore usually **small, repeatable responses whose usefulness remains fairly stable**: beginning a routine, carrying out a standard preparation step, putting an object back in its designated place or starting a familiar shutdown procedure. Decisions that genuinely require changing information, judgement or trade-offs should retain deliberate control.

The central advantage of practice under distraction is consequently modest but powerful. A good habit does not give someone more working memory, eliminate interruptions or guarantee correct behaviour when overloaded. It changes what the mind must accomplish with the attention that remains. Instead of repeatedly remembering and reconstructing a familiar response, the person can increasingly allow a stable cue to call up something already practised. That is where cue–response learning earns its place in self-improvement: **prepare useful defaults while attention is available, so that fewer decisions have to be rebuilt when it is not.**<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38047612/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Measuring context-response associations that drive habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Measuring context-response associations that drive habits - PubMed...</span></span></span>
