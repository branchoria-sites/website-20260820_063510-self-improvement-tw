## Introduction

A routine can fail at several different points, and the point of failure matters. **Starting failure** means the intended behaviour never gets under way. **Completion failure** means it begins but repeatedly breaks down before the intended dose or endpoint. **[Cue failure]({{ 'cue-failure/' | relative_url }})** means the situation that was supposed to prompt action is absent, vague or overlooked. **Obstacle failure** occurs when a recurring interruption or constraint defeats an otherwise workable plan. **Recovery failure** happens afterwards: one miss is allowed to become several because there is no dependable route back.

These are not interchangeable versions of “poor discipline”. They describe different mechanisms, so they call for different adjustments. Habit research distinguishes the automatic triggering of an action from the automatic execution of its subsequent steps, while research on [prospective memory]({{ 'remembering/' | relative_url }}), planning and interruption shows that remembering to act, carrying an action through and resuming it after disruption place different demands on a system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26991427/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habitual instigation and habitual execution: Definition, measurement, and effects on behaviour frequency - PubMedMarch 18, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 18, 2016</span></span></span>

The practical implication for adaptive self-improvement is simple: **locate the break before redesigning the routine**. Otherwise, an apparently sensible change can solve the wrong problem.

## Starting and completion failures are different problems

Suppose someone intends to [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) for 30 minutes after work. On some weeks, the exercise never starts; on others, it starts reliably but ends after five or ten minutes. Counting both outcomes simply as “failed workouts” throws away useful diagnostic information.

Research on habits provides a particularly helpful distinction. Benjamin Gardner and colleagues separate **habitual instigation** — automatically selecting and initiating an action — from **habitual execution**, in which the sequence of actions involved in performing it becomes automatic. In their prospective study of 229 students across flossing, snacking and breakfast consumption, measures of habitual instigation predicted behaviour frequency more consistently than measures of habitual execution. The authors therefore argued that maintaining behaviour may depend especially strongly on making its initiation habitual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26991427/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habitual instigation and habitual execution: Definition, measurement, and effects on behaviour frequency - PubMedMarch 18, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 18, 2016</span></span></span>

That distinction changes what should be adjusted.

A **starting failure** points towards the transition into the routine. The person may have a sincere goal yet repeatedly fail to convert it into action at the relevant moment. The useful questions are therefore: *What exactly is supposed to trigger the start? Does that trigger actually occur? Is the [first action]({{ 'first-action/' | relative_url }}) obvious? Is another behaviour winning the same moment?*

Implementation intentions address precisely this intention-to-action gap by specifying in advance that when situation Y occurs, behaviour X will begin. A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests reported a medium-to-large overall effect on goal attainment, while a separate meta-analysis of prospective-memory research found that implementation intentions improved remembering to perform intended future actions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: socmot.uni-konstanz.de">[uni-konstanz.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">socmot.uni-konstanz.de</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Social Psychology and MotivationImplementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes &#124; Social Psychol...</span></span></span>

A **completion failure**, by contrast, says that initiation is already occurring. Repeatedly changing alarms or adding more reminders may therefore add little. The diagnostic question moves downstream: *What happens after the start?*

For example, someone who consistently opens a textbook at 19:00 but abandons a planned 90-minute study session after 20 minutes does not primarily have a remembering problem. The intended session may be too long for the available energy or time; distractions may enter once work has begun; the task may contain an unclear next step; or a predictable interruption may terminate it. More generally, research on goal pursuit treats persistence as a distinct self-regulatory issue rather than simply the reverse of starting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34280324/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Persistence and Disengagement in Personal Goal Pursuit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Persistence and Disengagement in Personal Goal Pursuit - PubMedJanuary 4, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 4, 2022</span></span></span>

The adjustment should therefore match the observed break:

* **Never starts:** strengthen or clarify the trigger, make the first action more definite, or [reduce friction]({{ 'reduce-friction/' | relative_url }}) at initiation.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Starts but consistently stops at roughly the same point:** inspect workload, duration, sequencing and competing demands after initiation.
* **Starts and completes only when circumstances are unusually favourable:** the nominal routine may exceed what ordinary conditions can support.
* **Gets interrupted and never returns:** treat this as a resumption problem rather than automatically reducing the entire routine.

</div>

The important unit of evidence is not merely whether the routine received a tick. It is **where behaviour diverged from the plan**.

## Cue failures expose the trigger; obstacles expose the environment

Cue failures can look like low motivation because their visible result is the same: nothing happens. Mechanistically, however, a cue failure occurs before the person has had a meaningful opportunity to carry out the intended response.

Habit research describes habits as learned cue–response associations: recurring features of a context can activate behaviour with progressively less reliance on conscious deliberation. Experiments by David Neal and colleagues found that [strong habits]({{ 'strong-habits/' | relative_url }}) were triggered by performance contexts associated with past behaviour, rather than simply by participants' current goals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S002210311100254X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How do habits guide behavior? Perceived and actual triggers of habits in daily life - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

This makes **“when I have time”** a weak routine specification. It requires the person to notice an ill-defined state, decide that sufficient time exists, remember the intention and choose it over competing possibilities. “After I put my breakfast plate in the dishwasher, I take my medication” or “when I close my work laptop, I put on my running shoes” provides a more concrete event around which an association can form.

Evidence on context stability supports this principle. Research on everyday habit building found greater [automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }}) and better attainment of repetition goals when behaviours were performed in more stable contexts. The authors emphasised that context can involve both place and position within the flow of the day, not merely a clock time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9226889/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Context Stability in Habit Building Increases Automaticity and Goal Attainment - PMCJune 10, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 10, 2022</span></span></span> In Lally and colleagues' well-known real-world study, participants repeated a chosen eating, drinking or activity behaviour in a consistent context; automaticity generally increased with repetition, although the speed of development varied considerably between individuals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiley.com.</span></span></span>

A useful diagnostic test is therefore to ask what happened **immediately before the intended behaviour**. If the answer varies every day, the routine may not possess a stable cue at all.

An **obstacle failure** is different because the cue may work perfectly. The person remembers, intends to act and perhaps even starts — but the same foreseeable condition repeatedly prevents successful performance. A late meeting eliminates the exercise window; childcare interrupts study; required equipment is elsewhere; or the phone repeatedly diverts attention.

In that case, strengthening the cue treats the wrong mechanism. What is missing is a response to the barrier.

Research calls one version of this **coping planning**: anticipating likely barriers and specifying how they will be handled. A systematic review found variable evidence overall but concluded that coping planning appeared effective when people received support in making the plans, and that combining action plans with coping plans appeared more effective than action planning alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2013.766832" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tandfonline.com.</span></span></span> A later meta-analysis of 41 physical-activity planning trials, including 5,439 participants, likewise found a positive overall effect for planning interventions, while also finding meaningful variation between settings and intervention designs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9223740/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

This is why “exercise after work” and “if work finishes too late for the normal session, do the short home session” perform different jobs. The first identifies an intended action. The second protects that action against a known failure condition.

UK guidance from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) reflects the same distinction: behaviour-change interventions should use action planning, but also develop coping plans to prevent and manage relapse and review goals in light of previous progress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ph49/chapter/recommendations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nice.org.uk">[NICE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nice.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nice.org.uk.</span></span></span>

The diagnostic divide is therefore:

**If the moment to act keeps disappearing from awareness, redesign the cue. If the moment arrives but the same circumstance defeats the routine, redesign its response to that circumstance.**

## Recovery failure turns a miss into a breakdown

The most misleading routine failure can happen *after* the original miss.

Missing Monday's run because of an emergency does not necessarily reveal anything wrong with the normal Monday routine. The consequential failure may instead occur on Tuesday, when the person has no rule for what happens after a miss and postpones restarting until the next week.

This distinction prevents an isolated lapse from being given too much diagnostic weight. In Lally and colleagues' habit-formation study, missing one opportunity to perform the chosen behaviour did **not materially affect the habit-formation process**. Consistent repetition mattered, but uninterrupted perfection was not required.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiley.com.</span></span></span>

Research on relapse makes a related point, although it comes from a higher-risk domain and should not be mechanically generalised to ordinary routines. The classic relapse-prevention model distinguishes a lapse from a full relapse and proposes that how someone responds to an initial lapse can influence what follows. Interpreting one lapse as evidence of a broad, stable personal deficiency can promote abandonment, whereas treating it as a failure to cope with a particular situation leaves room to develop a better response to that situation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6760427/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> Empirical work on smoking lapses has also found that restorative coping after an initial lapse was associated with a lower likelihood of another lapse on the same day.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8916628/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

For ordinary self-improvement, the narrower lesson is useful: **the recovery mechanism deserves to be designed separately from the normal routine**.

A recovery rule needs to answer a concrete question: *What is the next valid opportunity?* It might be “resume at tomorrow's normal cue”, “do the reduced version at the next available window” or “after travel, restart with the first normal weekday morning”. The aim is not to compensate dramatically for the missed attempt. It is to remove ambiguity about re-entry.

There is also a cognitive reason why re-entry can be difficult. Research on interrupted tasks shows that interruptions create a prospective-memory problem: after attention has moved elsewhere, the person must retrieve the suspended intention and resume it. Experiments have found that abrupt interruptions can interfere with encoding the intention to resume, while longer or more demanding interruptions increase resumption costs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.1441" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Interruptions create prospective memory tasks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryInterruptions create prospective memory tasks - Dodhia - 2009 - Applied Cognitive Psychology - Wiley Online Library...</span></span></span>

Small “restart cues” can therefore matter. Laboratory studies found that preparing before an interruption — effectively encoding where to return — shortened subsequent resumption time, and that preserving contextual cues associated with the suspended goal could aid recovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581903000235" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span> The evidence concerns interrupted tasks rather than multi-day lifestyle routines, so the translation should be cautious, but the mechanism suggests a practical design principle: make the return point explicit rather than expecting the suspended intention to remain equally accessible indefinitely.

NICE guidance similarly recommends well-rehearsed action plans that can be put into practice following relapse, alongside environmental changes and routines that support maintenance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ph49/chapter/recommendations" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nice.org.uk">[NICE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nice.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nice.org.uk.</span></span></span>

## Diagnose the break before changing the system

The same disappointing weekly record can conceal four quite different systems. Imagine that “three study sessions planned, one completed” appears in each case.

In the first case, the student forgets the sessions until bedtime: **cue failure**. In the second, the reminder appears but the student repeatedly decides to start later and never does: **starting failure**. In the third, studying begins on schedule but an hour-long target repeatedly collapses after 15 minutes: **completion failure**. In the fourth, one session is missed during an unusually busy day and the student then abandons the routine for the rest of the week: **recovery failure**.

Those outcomes should not trigger the same redesign.

A compact troubleshooting sequence is more informative than asking whether motivation was “high enough”:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Did the intended cue occur and get noticed?** If not, investigate the cue.
2. **Once the opportunity appeared, did the behaviour begin?** If not, investigate initiation.
3. **Once begun, where did performance break down?** If it repeatedly stops early, investigate the size, sequence or execution conditions.
4. **Did a recurring external condition interfere?** If so, design around that obstacle or create a coping response.
5. **After a miss or interruption, did the routine resume at the next reasonable opportunity?** If not, design the recovery rule.

</div>

This classification does not imply that every failure has one perfectly identifiable cause. Motivation, capability and environment interact, and evidence for techniques such as planning varies across behaviours and populations. Habit researchers themselves caution against assuming that all repeated behaviour is controlled by habit or that making an action “habitual” solves every maintenance problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/spc3.12975" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiley.com.</span></span></span>

The value of the classification is more modest and more useful: it narrows the hypothesis. Instead of responding to every disappointing outcome with a larger goal, stronger reminder, stricter rule or wholesale new system, it asks which transition is repeatedly failing.

That is the mechanism that makes repeated adjustment informative. **Starting problems call attention to initiation; completion problems to what happens after initiation; cue problems to how the opportunity for action is detected; obstacle problems to predictable interference; and recovery problems to the route back after disruption.** Once those failures are separated, self-improvement becomes less a judgement about whether the routine “worked” and more a test of which part of its design needs to change.
