## Introduction

There is no universal day on which a complex behaviour suddenly “clicks” into being a habit. The familiar numbers — 21 days, 66 days, two months — describe very different things, and none is a scientifically established deadline. The strongest direct evidence instead shows a learning process whose duration varies sharply between people, behaviours and circumstances. A 2024 systematic review found individual habit-formation times ranging from **4 to 335 days**, while noting that only four of its 20 included studies directly reported how long formation took.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

For complex routines, that variation matters more than any average. [Exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}), [meal preparation]({{ 'meal-prep/' | relative_url }}) or a multi-step evening routine can contain several decisions and actions, and research increasingly distinguishes between automatically **starting** such a sequence and automatically **executing every part** of it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjhp.12189" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS Psych Hub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS Psych HubHabitual instigation and habitual execution: Definition, measurement, and effects on behaviour frequency - Gardner - 2016...</span></span></span> The useful lesson for self-improvement is therefore not to find the correct countdown. It is to understand what researchers actually timed.

## Where the famous 66-day figure came from

The 66-day figure comes primarily from a University College London study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues, published online in 2009 and in the *European Journal of Social Psychology* in 2010. Ninety-six volunteers selected a new eating, drinking or activity behaviour and attempted it daily in a consistent context for 12 weeks. Examples included performing an action “after breakfast”. Participants recorded both whether they performed the behaviour and how automatic it felt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

Crucially, the researchers were not measuring the day on which a behaviour switched from “not a habit” to “habit”. They modelled each person's repeated [automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }}) scores using an asymptotic curve: automaticity was expected to increase substantially at first, then improve by progressively smaller amounts as it approached a personal plateau. The reported formation time represented the estimated point at which someone had reached **95% of that eventual automaticity level**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

That distinction changes the meaning of “66 days”. It was the median estimated time to reach that modelling criterion, not a biological threshold, a prescription to practise for exactly 66 days, or evidence that everybody should feel automatic on day 67. Individual estimates ranged from **18 to 254 days**. The curve could be fitted for 62 participants and was judged a good fit for 39, another reason not to treat one summary statistic as a universal law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

The shape of the curve is arguably more useful than its midpoint. Habit formation was gradual and asymptotic rather than an on/off transition. Early repetitions could therefore produce meaningful gains in automaticity well before the estimated plateau, while later repetitions could continue strengthening the association without producing a dramatic subjective moment when the habit suddenly became effortless.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

The same study also undermines the idea that a streak must be perfect. Missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially disrupt the formation process. Consistent repetition mattered, but a single omission did not reset an invisible habit clock to zero.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

## From 21 days to a 335-day range

The older 21-day rule has an even weaker connection to behavioural habit research. Maxwell Maltz's 1960 *Psycho-Cybernetics* discussed observations such as patients becoming accustomed to changed appearances after surgery and explicitly referred to a **minimum of about 21 days** for perceptible changes in a mental image. It was not an experiment establishing that repeated behaviours become automatic in three weeks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/318795/psycho-cybernetics-by-maxwell-maltz-md-fics/9780399176135/excerpt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: penguinrandomhouse.ca">[Penguin Random House Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">penguinrandomhouse.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on penguinrandomhouse.ca.</span></span></span>

Modern evidence does not simply replace 21 with 66. The 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis examined 20 health-habit studies involving 2,601 participants. Across the four studies that directly reported formation time, the headline results included median times of 59–66 days and mean estimates of 106–154 days. At the individual level, however, observed estimates stretched from **4 to 335 days**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/12/23/2488" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Time to Form a Habit: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Health Behaviour Habit Formation and Its Determinants...</span></span></span>

That widest range came from a study of healthy eating habits in which the median among successful formations was 59 days, but only **23% of participants reached the study's predetermined formation threshold**. This is an important qualification: a formation-time statistic can describe those who crossed a particular measurement threshold without telling us that everyone attempting the behaviour eventually did so.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Other results show why averages should not be transferred casually between routines. In a 90-day study of a daily stretching behaviour, researchers modelled automaticity at about **106 days when the stretch was performed after waking and 154 days when performed before bed**. Because those estimates extended beyond the observation period, they involved extrapolating the fitted learning curves rather than watching every participant reach a literal finishing line.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28650196/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Effects of circadian cortisol on the development of a health habit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Effects of circadian cortisol on the development of a health habit</span></span></span>

The systematic review itself therefore deserves a careful reading. Its 4-to-335-day span is not a new universal “true range” within which every conceivable habit must fall. It is the range found in the available studies it reviewed. Only four studies directly supplied formation-time estimates, the behaviours were predominantly health-related, and the review judged most included studies to have a high risk of bias. The authors explicitly identified the limited direct evidence on formation duration as a research gap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/12/23/2488" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mdpi.com">[MDPI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mdpi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Time to Form a Habit: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Health Behaviour Habit Formation and Its Determinants...</span></span></span>

In other words, the progression from “21 days” to “66 days” to “4–335 days” should not be read as science repeatedly discovering a better countdown. It reflects a move away from a simple countdown altogether.

## Why complexity breaks countdown rules

Complex behaviours make a single formation time particularly difficult to interpret because “the habit” may not be one indivisible action. Going to the gym, for example, can involve recognising a cue, deciding to exercise, changing clothes, travelling, beginning the session and then completing a sequence of exercises. Those components need not acquire the same degree of automaticity at the same rate.

Research distinguishes **habitual instigation** — a cue automatically prompting the impulse to begin — from **habitual execution**, in which the sub-actions involved in performing the behaviour unfold automatically. In a study of 123 adults followed for a month, exercise-instigation habit was the unique habit predictor of exercise frequency; execution habit was not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26148187/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> A separate study covering flossing, snacking and breakfast consumption similarly found instigation-specific habit measures more predictive of behaviour frequency than execution-specific measures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjhp.12189" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS Psych Hub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS Psych HubHabitual instigation and habitual execution: Definition, measurement, and effects on behaviour frequency - Gardner - 2016...</span></span></span>

This means a complex routine can legitimately become “habitual” without every component becoming mindless. A regular exerciser might automatically head for a workout when a familiar cue occurs but still deliberately choose what exercises to perform. Conversely, someone could consciously decide to begin an activity yet move through a highly rehearsed sequence almost automatically once started. A 2024 review of habit theory argues that intricate real-world behaviours commonly combine automatic and reflective control in precisely this way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/spc3.12975" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Compass]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What is habit and how can it be used to change real‐world behaviour? Narrowing the theory‐reality gap - Gardner - 2024 - Social an...</span></span></span>

Complexity also changes the conditions under which automaticity is observed. In a study of 459 participants rating 25 behaviours — including exercise, handwashing and medication adherence — perceived complexity was negatively associated with automaticity overall. Frequency, contextual stability and perceived reward were positively associated with automaticity, while the relationships involving stable context and reward varied with behavioural complexity. Exercise was rated the most complex of the behaviours examined, whereas handwashing was among the simplest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01556/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on frontiersin.org.</span></span></span>

These findings do **not** establish a formula such as “[complex habits]({{ 'complex-habits/' | relative_url }}) take twice as long”. That study was observational rather than a trial timing the formation of each behaviour from scratch. Instead, it shows why complexity is a plausible source of variation and why assigning one population average to everything from drinking water to establishing a workout routine loses important information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6667662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

For multi-step behaviours, even the instigation/execution distinction is an approximation. A complex sequence can contain lower-level actions that develop their own cue-response relationships. A review of health-habit research gives the example of bicycle commuting: taking a bicycle from a shed and walking it towards the road could themselves become separately cued components within the larger routine. The same logic can apply to preparation, travel, set-up and completion stages in many self-improvement routines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4566897/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## What a formation time actually tells you

The most defensible interpretation of habit-formation research is therefore narrower than popular habit challenges imply. A reported number of days is an estimate produced for **a specified behaviour, population, measurement method, cue structure and definition of sufficient automaticity**. Change any of those and the number may change too.

For a simple action repeated daily under a stable cue, one automaticity curve can be a reasonably intuitive description. For a complex routine, a single number can conceal several learning processes. The cue to begin may become powerful while execution remains deliberate; preparatory actions may automate before the main activity; or changing locations and schedules may repeatedly alter the contexts in which learning occurs. Research on exercise reinforces this point: preparatory habit and temporal [consistency]({{ 'consistency/' | relative_url }}) have predicted subsequent exercise behaviour even when automaticity of the exercise performance itself did not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28374463/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med The role of habit in different phases of exercise</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med The role of habit in different phases of exercise</span></span></span>

This leads to three useful ways to read any claim about how long a habit “takes”:

* **Ask what endpoint was measured.** Reaching 95% of a modelled automaticity plateau, crossing a questionnaire threshold and reporting that an action feels habitual are not interchangeable definitions.
* **Look at the spread, not just the middle.** A median of 66 days accompanied by an 18-to-254-day range communicates substantial individual variation; the wider review range of 4–335 days makes fixed deadlines still less defensible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>
* **Ask what part of a complex routine became automatic.** Automatically beginning a study session or workout can be behaviourally important even when the content of that session still requires conscious decisions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26148187/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The practical significance is not that people should replace a 21-day challenge with a 335-day challenge. It is that elapsed time is a poor standalone measure of progress. In the original Lally study, automaticity strengthened through repeated behaviour in a consistent context and approached a plateau gradually. In broader research, frequency, context stability and behavioural characteristics help explain why different trajectories emerge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

For complex self-improvement behaviours, “this still takes some thought after two months” is therefore not evidence that habit formation has failed. The research gives no universal date by which the whole sequence should become effortless. A more evidence-consistent expectation is that automaticity can accumulate unevenly: the trigger to start, the preparation and individual components may become easier at different rates, while parts that require judgement remain intentional. That is not an exception to habit formation. For complex behaviour, it may be what successful habit formation normally looks like.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjhp.12189" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS Psych Hub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS Psych HubHabitual instigation and habitual execution: Definition, measurement, and effects on behaviour frequency - Gardner - 2016...</span></span></span>
