## Introduction

A preselected shopping option can change what people buy even when they never consciously decide that the preselected choice is better. In a large UK government-commissioned experiment, shoppers shown a more expensive option already selected were about 60% more likely to choose the costlier protection plan and 70% more likely to choose the costlier shipping option than shoppers required to make an active choice. The same experiment found that people moved through the shopping journey faster when an option was preselected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672208443aa14203d06ef49d/research-into-potentially-harmful-online-choice-architecture.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets Research into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AssetsResearch into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span></span></span>

## What the UK online-shopping experiment found

The Department for Business and Trade commissioned Alma Economics to examine defaults in UK e-commerce. The research, conducted in 2024, had two linked stages. First, researchers manually followed consumer journeys through 558 popular UK shopping websites and apps. They recorded 412 instances of defaults, spread across 49% of the sampled services. Defaults were particularly common in retail: 69% of the retail websites and apps examined contained at least one. Shipping and delivery were the most common defaults within the customer-service category.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-prevalence-and-potential-harm-of-defaults-in-online-shopping" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The prevalence and potential harm of defaults in online shopping</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The prevalence and potential harm of defaults in online shopping</span></span></span>

Importantly, this observational [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) did **not** show that online defaults were generally designed to make shoppers spend more. About 64% directed people towards a cheaper or similarly priced option. The researchers therefore described the real-world picture as more nuanced than a simple story about harmful design. Defaults can save effort when they select an option that suits the shopper; the problem arises when the default and the shopper's interests diverge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672208443aa14203d06ef49d/research-into-potentially-harmful-online-choice-architecture.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets Research into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AssetsResearch into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span></span></span>

The second stage isolated that possibility experimentally. A randomised controlled trial involved 5,889 participants using a simulated shopping platform designed to resemble a familiar e-commerce site. Participants encountered decisions involving either a protection plan or shipping. In the control condition they had to choose actively; in treatment conditions, the more expensive choice was either genuinely preselected or merely made to look visually prominent in a way that mimicked a default.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672208443aa14203d06ef49d/research-into-potentially-harmful-online-choice-architecture.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets Research into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AssetsResearch into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span></span></span>

The distinction produced the study's most striking result. **Actual preselection strongly increased selection of the expensive option:** roughly 60% for the protection plan and 70% for shipping relative to the control condition. By contrast, merely making an option look like a default had much weaker effects. For protection plans, the effect of the mimicking treatment was statistically indistinguishable from zero; for shipping, participants were only about 5% more likely to choose the visually prominent expensive option.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672208443aa14203d06ef49d/research-into-potentially-harmful-online-choice-architecture.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets Research into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AssetsResearch into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span></span></span>

That comparison matters. It suggests that the result was not simply “people buy whatever catches their eye”. There was something consequential about having the decision already made on the shopper's behalf. The preselected choice became the option that had to be actively rejected.

There was another revealing difference: preselection reduced the overall time participants spent on the shopping journey by about 12%. The researchers interpreted the time results as consistent with shoppers spending less time deliberating when a default was available, whereas the control condition forced them to select an option themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672208443aa14203d06ef49d/research-into-potentially-harmful-online-choice-architecture.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets Research into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AssetsResearch into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span></span></span>

Yet the study did not find statistically significant effects of preselection on its self-reported measure of willingness to pay or on its estimated consumer-surplus measure. Nor did most participants report feeling badly about their decisions afterwards. The evidence is therefore strongest for a **change in observed choice**, not for a claim that everyone influenced by a default later regrets the purchase or suffers measurable financial harm. Participants exposed to preselection were, however, less likely to say they would choose the same option again.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672208443aa14203d06ef49d/research-into-potentially-harmful-online-choice-architecture.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets Research into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AssetsResearch into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span></span></span>

## Why preselection changes consumer decisions

Defaults work partly because they change the task. Compare two checkout screens. On the first, neither standard nor premium delivery is selected, so the shopper must answer, “Which delivery option do I want?” On the second, premium delivery is already selected. Now the immediate task can become, “Is there enough reason to change this?” Those are not psychologically identical decisions even though exactly the same alternatives remain available.

A broader meta-analysis of 58 default studies, covering 73,675 participants, found a substantial average default effect but also considerable variation between settings. Its analysis highlighted several possible channels. A default may be treated as an implicit **endorsement** — a signal that the choice architect considers the option appropriate — or as an existing **status quo** that the person becomes reluctant to give up. Staying with a default can also be easier than changing it, particularly when the shopper is moving quickly through several decisions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/when-and-why-defaults-influence-decisions-a-metaanalysis-of-default-effects/67AF6972CFB52698A60B6BD94B70C2C0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University PressWhen and why defaults influence decisions: a meta-analysis of default effects &#124; Behavioural Public Policy &#124; Cam...</span></span></span>

The UK experiment offers unusually useful evidence about attention. For shipping, people who noticed the default were 31 percentage points less likely to select the expensive default than those who did not notice it. The researchers therefore concluded that making defaults more noticeable could reduce their influence. This does not prove that awareness completely neutralises defaults, but it supports a simple practical point: **recognising that a choice has been made for you changes the decision environment**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672208443aa14203d06ef49d/research-into-potentially-harmful-online-choice-architecture.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets Research into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AssetsResearch into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span></span></span>

Age also mattered. The experiment found that older participants had a greater probability of selecting the expensive option when it was preselected. The detailed analysis found an interaction between age category and the preselection treatment in both the protection-plan and shipping experiments. The authors consequently highlighted older consumers as a group that may warrant particular attention when retailers or regulators assess default design.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/672208443aa14203d06ef49d/research-into-potentially-harmful-online-choice-architecture.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets Research into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AssetsResearch into potentially harmful online choice architecture</span></span></span>

These findings should not be turned into the stronger claim that consumers are powerless against defaults. Defaults vary greatly in effectiveness, and people can reject them. Indeed, the wider meta-analysis found studies with weak, insignificant and even negative effects alongside studies showing large ones. What preselection does is alter the probability of a choice — sometimes substantially — without eliminating the alternative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/when-and-why-defaults-influence-decisions-a-metaanalysis-of-default-effects/67AF6972CFB52698A60B6BD94B70C2C0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University PressWhen and why defaults influence decisions: a meta-analysis of default effects &#124; Behavioural Public Policy &#124; Cam...</span></span></span>

## How to review defaults before completing a purchase

The self-improvement application is straightforward: do not rely on [remembering]({{ 'remembering/' | relative_url }}) to resist every expensive suggestion. Instead, create a small checkout routine that makes the default visible before money changes hands. This uses the same environment-design principle in reverse: turn passive continuation back into an active decision.

A useful review can be kept short:

1. **Identify anything already selected.** Before paying, scan delivery, warranties or protection plans, subscriptions, quantities and other configurable options. Treat a filled radio button, ticked box or already-added item as a decision that still needs your approval.
2. **Ask what you would choose from a blank screen.** Mentally remove the preselection: “If neither option were selected, would I pay extra for this?” That question helps separate your preference from the website's starting point.
3. **Compare the incremental cost with the incremental benefit.** “Premium delivery is only £3 more” is less informative than “Am I paying £3 because receiving this sooner is actually worth £3 to me?” The same logic applies to protection plans and upgrades.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
