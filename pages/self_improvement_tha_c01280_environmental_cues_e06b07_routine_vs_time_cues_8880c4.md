## Introduction

For building a habit, there is no good evidence that “after breakfast” is inherently better than “at 9 am”. The strongest direct comparison found that **routine-based cues and clock-time cues produced similar increases in [habit automaticity]({{ 'habit-limits/' | relative_url }})** over 12 weeks. What mattered more was whether people repeatedly performed the intended behaviour when their chosen cue occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33405284/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine-based versus time-based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - PubMedJanuary 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2021</span></span></span>

That distinction is useful for self-improvement. A routine cue links an action to another recurring event — “after breakfast, I will eat a piece of fruit”. A time cue links it to the clock — “at 9 am, I will eat a piece of fruit”. Either can provide the stable environmental context from which a habit develops. The practical question is therefore less “Which type of cue is scientifically superior?” and more “Which cue will I reliably encounter and act on?”

## How routine and time cues differ

A **routine-based cue** is an event in the ordinary sequence of the day: getting out of bed, finishing breakfast, brushing your teeth, arriving at your desk or clearing away dinner. The event signals that the new behaviour should follow. A **time-based cue** uses a particular clock time instead: 8 am, 1 pm or 9.30 pm.

There are plausible reasons to expect routine cues to have an advantage. An established routine is already something a person repeatedly encounters without having to watch the clock. Habit researchers have therefore suggested that event-based cues may be easier to detect than time cues, which can require conscious time monitoring. A routine can also tolerate some variation in scheduling: if breakfast happens at 7.30 one morning and 8.15 the next, “after breakfast” still identifies the relevant moment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/bjhp.12504" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS PsycHub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS PsycHubHabit formation following routine‐based versus time‐based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - Keller - 2021 - Britis...</span></span></span>

Earlier observational evidence appeared consistent with that idea. A study of 1,244 Australian adults examined the consistency of several cues surrounding [physical activity]({{ 'physical-capacity/' | relative_url }}), including time, location, people, mood and routines. More consistent **routine cues** were associated with greater physical-activity [automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }}), whereas consistent **time cues** were associated with the amount of physical activity rather than its automaticity. Because this was observational rather than experimental evidence, however, it could not establish that choosing a routine cue actually causes a stronger habit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25864705/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cue Consistency Associated with Physical Activity Automaticity and Behavior - PubMedApril 11, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2015</span></span></span>

That uncertainty made a direct randomised comparison particularly useful.

## What the randomised nutrition trial found

The clearest head-to-head test was published by Jan Keller and colleagues in the *British Journal of Health Psychology* in 2021. The prospectively registered study recruited 192 adults aged 18–77 from the general population in Germany and randomly allocated 96 to each of two planning conditions. Participants chose a healthy nutrition behaviour they wanted to develop and were instructed to connect it either to a daily routine or to a particular time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/bjhp.12504" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS PsycHub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS PsycHubHabit formation following routine‐based versus time‐based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - Keller - 2021 - Britis...</span></span></span>

The contrast was deliberately simple. Someone in the routine condition might plan the behaviour for **after breakfast**; someone in the time condition might plan it for **9 am**. Both groups received a brief intervention of about five minutes and were asked to carry out their chosen behaviour in the planned context every day for 12 weeks. Daily questionnaires across 84 days measured whether they enacted the plan and how automatic the behaviour was becoming.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/bjhp.12504" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS PsycHub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS PsycHubHabit formation following routine‐based versus time‐based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - Keller - 2021 - Britis...</span></span></span>

The predicted superiority of one cue type did not emerge. At the group level:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* automaticity increased over time in both conditions;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.nature.com/nature-index/topics/l4/behavior-change-and-habit-formation-in-health-contexts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* enactment of the planned behaviour increased in both conditions;
* there was **no significant between-group difference** favouring routine cues or time cues;
* repeatedly performing the behaviour in response to the planned cue predicted greater automaticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33405284/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine-based versus time-based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - PubMedJanuary 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2021</span></span></span>

</div>

In other words, “after breakfast” and “at 9 am” were both viable ways to organise the repeated behaviour. The important ingredient was not which wording appeared in the plan, but whether the person repeatedly completed the action when that cue arrived.

### Habit formation was highly individual

The same dataset also cautions against expecting habits to click into place after a standard number of days. Among participants whose habit-development trajectories could be modelled as successful asymptotic curves, the median estimated time to reach 95% of peak automaticity was **59 days**. Individual estimates ranged from **4 to 335 days**. Some participants showed continuous progress, others discontinuous development, and some did not successfully form the intended habit during the study.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.12504" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine‐based versus time‐based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - Keller - 2021 - British Journa...</span></span></span>

That broad variation echoes the influential earlier study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues. In that research, participants repeated an eating, drinking or activity behaviour in a consistent daily context for 12 weeks. Estimated times to reach 95% of maximum automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days, and greater [consistency]({{ 'consistency/' | relative_url }}) of performance was associated with better-fitting habit-development curves. Missing a single opportunity did not materially derail the process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

Taken together, these studies shift attention away from finding a supposedly perfect trigger. Habit formation is better understood as **repeated learning in a sufficiently consistent context** than as the result of choosing a special kind of cue.

## Why the result does not prove that cues never matter

The nutrition trial supports equivalence within its tested circumstances, not the stronger claim that routine and time cues must work identically for every behaviour and every person. It involved self-selected healthy eating behaviours, relied on self-reported enactment and automaticity, and had no no-planning control group. The authors also noted that their sample contained comparatively many women and younger participants, limiting how confidently the results can be generalised to other populations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.12504" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine‐based versus time‐based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - Keller - 2021 - British Journa...</span></span></span>

There was also some inevitable fuzziness between the two cue categories. All retained participants in the time condition supplied a valid clock time, but only 55 of 65 retained participants in the routine condition supplied a cue that the researchers classified as an actual routine; several instead entered a general [time of day]({{ 'time-of-day/' | relative_url }}) or an internal state such as feeling stressed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.12504" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine‐based versus time‐based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - Keller - 2021 - British Journa...</span></span></span>

More fundamentally, routine and time cues often overlap in ordinary life. Breakfast may usually happen around 8 am; a lunch break may reliably begin around 1 pm. A person intending to act “after breakfast” is therefore exposed to a bundle of recurring contextual information — activity, approximate time, location and perhaps the same objects and people. The experiment compared two planning instructions, not perfectly isolated psychological stimuli.

This is why the result is best read as **no demonstrated advantage for either planning strategy in this trial**, rather than proof that cue characteristics are irrelevant. Earlier physical-activity research suggests that cue consistency can relate differently to automaticity and behaviour, but the randomised evidence does not justify telling everyone that [routine anchors]({{ 'routine-anchors/' | relative_url }}) are categorically superior to clock times.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25864705/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cue Consistency Associated with Physical Activity Automaticity and Behavior - PubMedApril 11, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2015</span></span></span>

## Choose the cue that recurs most reliably

For practical habit building, the comparison points towards a straightforward decision rule: **choose the cue that gives you the most dependable opportunity to repeat the behaviour in the intended context**.

A routine cue is attractive when the clock moves but the sequence of events remains stable. Someone whose breakfast varies between 7 and 9 am may find “take my vitamin after breakfast” more dependable than “take it at 8 am”. The existing event identifies the opportunity without requiring the person to notice a particular minute on the clock.

A time cue may be better when the schedule itself is highly structured. If a work break reliably starts at 10.30 am, a clock-based plan may be just as easy to encounter and act upon. There is little reason, based on the randomised trial, to replace a dependable time cue merely because routine anchoring sounds more like conventional habit-building advice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33405284/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine-based versus time-based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - PubMedJanuary 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2021</span></span></span>

Reliability also means choosing a cue that genuinely happens often enough. The German Clinical Trials Register description of the experiment captures the underlying principle succinctly: successful habit formation depends on consistently repeating the behaviour when the recurring cue is encountered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drks.de/search/en/trial/DRKS00016720/details" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drks.de">[drks.de]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drks.de</span><span class="citation-popover-title">German Clinical Trials Register</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">German Clinical Trials Register</span></span></span> A theoretically elegant anchor that disappears on busy days provides fewer learning opportunities than an ordinary cue that reliably recurs.

The evidence therefore supports three practical priorities. First, make the cue specific enough that you know when to act: “after I finish breakfast” is clearer than “in the morning”. Second, choose a cue that occurs on most of the days when you want the behaviour to occur. Third, judge the strategy by **enactment**: if the cue repeatedly arrives but the intended behaviour does not follow, changing the cue may be more useful than waiting for automaticity to appear.

## Routine or clock?

For this particular habit-building choice, the evidence favours flexibility rather than a universal rule. Routine cues have a plausible practical advantage when daily timing varies, and observational research has associated consistent routines with stronger automaticity. But the direct randomised comparison did **not** find routine-based planning superior to time-based planning. Both approaches supported increasing enactment and automaticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25864705/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cue Consistency Associated with Physical Activity Automaticity and Behavior - PubMedApril 11, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2015</span></span></span>

The more consequential distinction is between a cue you can repeatedly act on and one you cannot. “After breakfast” is useful only if breakfast reliably occurs and leaves an opportunity for the desired action. “At 9 am” is useful only if 9 am reliably finds you able to act. Habit building is therefore less about choosing between the routine and the clock than about creating a dependable cue–behaviour pairing and giving that pairing enough repetitions to become increasingly automatic.

    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
