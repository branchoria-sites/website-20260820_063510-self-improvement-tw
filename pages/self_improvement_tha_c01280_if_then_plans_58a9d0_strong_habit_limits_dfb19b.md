## Introduction

If-then plans can interrupt an unwanted habit, but their power has limits. The key distinction is **habit strength**. A prepared response such as “If I reach for biscuits while watching television, then I will make tea instead” can give an alternative behaviour a better chance of winning when the familiar cue appears. Yet experiments suggest that this advantage shrinks as the competing habit becomes stronger. In a field study of smoking, implementation intentions reduced smoking among people with weak or moderate smoking habits, but not among those with strong habits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1348/014466608x370591" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Planning to break unwanted habits: Habit strength moderates implementation intention effects on behaviour change - Webb - 2009 - Briti...</span></span></span>

That matters for self-improvement because repeated failure of an obstacle plan does not necessarily mean that the plan was pointless. It can mean that the old cue-response relationship is sufficiently entrenched that a sentence written in advance is being asked to do too much. At that point, changing the environment, identifying the real trigger, strengthening the replacement behaviour or adding structured behavioural support may be more effective than simply rewriting the same if-then plan.

## How prepared responses can interrupt a habit

A habit and an implementation intention have an important feature in common: both can connect a situation with a response. A habit acquires that connection through repeated behaviour in recurring circumstances. An implementation intention deliberately creates a new connection — “when this situation occurs, perform this response” — before the situation arises. Reviews of habit research describe established habits as responses that recurring contextual cues can activate with relatively little dependence on a person's current intentions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-8721.2006.00435.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[sagepub.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sage Journals Habits—A Repeat Performance</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsHabits—A Repeat Performance - David T. Neal, Wendy Wood, Jeffrey M. Quinn, 2006...</span></span></span>

This sets up something resembling competition between two prepared responses. Suppose arriving home has repeatedly led to sitting on the sofa and scrolling on a phone. A new obstacle plan might be: **“If I put my keys down after work, then I will put my phone in the drawer and change into my running clothes.”** The old context points towards one response; the deliberately prepared plan points towards another.

Laboratory research supports the idea that such counter-habitual plans can alter this competition. In three experiments, Marieke Adriaanse and colleagues found that implementation intentions removed the habitual response's normal advantage in mental accessibility when participants encountered the critical situation. Importantly, however, the alternative behaviour did not simply become automatically dominant. The researchers concluded that the plans appeared to create greater flexibility at the decision point, while actual departure from the habit still depended on having a sufficiently strong intention to perform the alternative behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21317315/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Breaking habits with implementation intentions: a test of underlying processes - PubMedApril 1, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 2011</span></span></span>

That is a more useful model than thinking of an if-then plan as an instant replacement habit. The plan can **open a window in which another response becomes possible**. What happens inside that window still depends on the strength of the old habit, the suitability of the alternative and the person's commitment to it.

There is also experimental evidence that implementation intentions can improve suppression of previously learnt responses. A 2018 laboratory study using a task designed to detect habitual “slips of action” found better habit breaking with implementation intentions than with goal intentions alone, although effects varied with the type of outcome involved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691816303626" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An experimental investigation of breaking learnt habits with verbal implementation intentions - ScienceDirectMarch 1, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 2018</span></span></span> Taken together, these studies support a qualified conclusion: planning can interfere with habitual responding, but it does not reliably erase the old response.

## Why stronger habits are harder to displace

The clearest direct test comes from Thomas Webb, Paschal Sheeran and Aleksandra Luszczynska's study of habit strength. Their first experiment created relatively habitual responses in a laboratory task and then changed what participants needed to do. Forming implementation intentions reduced the harmful carry-over of the old response, but **the benefit was smaller among participants with stronger habits**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1348/014466608x370591" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Planning to break unwanted habits: Habit strength moderates implementation intention effects on behaviour change - Webb - 2009 - Briti...</span></span></span>

Their second experiment moved from a laboratory task to smoking. Here, implementation intentions reduced smoking for participants whose smoking habits were weak or moderate but did not significantly reduce it among participants classified as having strong smoking habits. The authors' central conclusion was therefore not that implementation intentions cannot break habits, but that **habit strength moderates their effectiveness**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18851764/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Planning to break unwanted habits: habit strength moderates implementation intention effects on behaviour change - PubMed...</span></span></span>

That result fits the wider science of habits. Strong habits are particularly responsive to cues associated with previous performance. In experiments examining everyday habits, familiar contexts activated strongly habitual behaviours even when participants' current goals pointed elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S002210311100254X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How do habits guide behavior? Perceived and actual triggers of habits in daily life - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span> A person may sincerely intend not to check their phone, snack, smoke or procrastinate, yet the familiar location, time, object or preceding action can still retrieve the practised response.

The contrast between **adding a desired behaviour** and **suppressing an established one** is also visible in dietary research. A systematic review of 23 studies found implementation intentions more effective for increasing healthy eating, with an effect size of *d* = 0.51, than for reducing unhealthy eating, where the effect was *d* = 0.29.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21056605/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> A later meta-regression covering 70 interventions and 9,689 participants likewise found a medium effect for promoting healthy eating (*d* = 0.33) but only a small effect for reducing unhealthy eating (*d* = 0.18).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31125588/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> These results concern eating behaviour rather than habit strength alone, so they should not be treated as a direct replication of the smoking finding. They nevertheless illustrate a recurring practical difficulty: **starting a useful response can be easier than stopping a well-rehearsed competing response**.

## Replacement works better than telling yourself “not to”

The wording of an obstacle plan matters particularly when it confronts a habit. A plan such as **“If I am offered cake, then I will not eat cake”** names the cue but leaves the person without a positive action to execute.

Experiments on unhealthy eating found an additional problem. Adriaanse and colleagues tested plans with the form “if situation X occurs, then I will not perform habitual response Y”. These negation plans produced ironic cognitive and behavioural rebound effects compared with forming a goal intention or specifying a replacement response. In other words, explicitly linking the cue to *not doing* the habitual action could keep the unwanted response mentally active rather than successfully replacing it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21177875/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

For obstacle planning, the safer design principle is therefore to specify **what happens instead**:

> **Weaker:** If I feel like checking social media while working, then I will not check my phone.
> **Stronger:** If I reach for my phone while working, then I will put both hands back on the keyboard and finish the paragraph.

The second plan gives the cue a concrete competing destination. Research on counter-habitual implementation intentions similarly indicates that alternative responses matter: plans can remove some of the habitual response's cognitive advantage, but behaviour changes more reliably when there is a meaningful alternative action backed by a strong goal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21317315/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Breaking habits with implementation intentions: a test of underlying processes - PubMedApril 1, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1, 2011</span></span></span>

Choosing the **right cue** is equally important. In research on dietary change, implementation intentions worked better when plans were tailored to personally relevant reasons or situations associated with unhealthy eating.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19106078/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> If someone believes “evening” is the trigger when the reliable trigger is actually opening a particular cupboard after a stressful day, an otherwise well-written plan may be aimed at the wrong moment.

## When the environment should do some of the work

Repeated failure in the same setting is a reason to examine the setting itself. Habits are strongly tied to recurring contextual cues, which means that changing those cues can interrupt automatic repetition before deliberate [self-control]({{ 'self-control/' | relative_url }}) has to compete with it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.annualreviews.org/docserver/fulltext/psych/67/1/annurev-psych-122414-033417.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: annualreviews.org">[Annual Reviews]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">annualreviews.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Annual Reviews Psychology of Habit &#124; Annual Reviews</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Annual Reviews Psychology of Habit &#124; Annual Reviews</span></span></span>

A striking demonstration came from research on students transferring between universities. [Exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}), newspaper-reading and television-viewing habits tended to persist across the move when important features of the old performance context remained stable. When relevant contextual cues changed, habitual behaviour was disrupted and, in some circumstances, behaviour became more responsive to participants' current intentions. Merely changing intentions could not explain the effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15982113/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Changing circumstances, disrupting habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Changing circumstances, disrupting habits</span></span></span>

This suggests a practical escalation from **planning around a cue** to **altering the cue or the path to the response**. If an entrenched evening-snacking routine survives “If I want chocolate, then I will eat fruit”, not routinely keeping chocolate within immediate reach changes the behavioural contest. If opening a social-media site is nearly automatic during work, signing out, removing shortcuts or keeping the phone elsewhere inserts friction before the habitual response can run. These examples are applications of the context-cue evidence rather than individually tested prescriptions.

Context change is not magic: new routines can themselves become habitual, and old responses may reappear when old cues return. But large-scale evidence supports the broader “habit discontinuity” idea. An analysis of 18,053 people in the UK Understanding Society survey found that after moving home — a major context disruption — behaviour was temporarily more aligned with environmental attitudes in the domain of car use, with the association diminishing as time since the move increased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27120333/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> The useful lesson is not to move house to change a habit. It is that **disrupting [stable cues]({{ 'stable-cues/' | relative_url }}) can create a period in which deliberate choices have more leverage**.

## When to add behavioural or professional support

Some persistent behaviours need more than a self-written obstacle plan, particularly when dependence, clinically significant repetitive behaviour or substantial impairment is involved. Smoking is a useful example because the study showing failure of implementation intentions among strong habitual smokers should not be interpreted as evidence that such people simply need a cleverer sentence. Current NICE guidance recommends behavioural support and evidence-based cessation options for people who smoke, with several pharmacological or nicotine-containing options producing higher quit success when combined with behavioural support.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG209/chapter/treating-tobacco-dependence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nice.org.uk">[Nice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nice.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">treating tobacco dependence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">treating tobacco dependence</span></span></span>

More intensive behavioural methods also show what a strengthened version of “cue → alternative response” can look like. Habit reversal therapy, used clinically for problems including tics and hair pulling, involves more than declaring an intention. It typically builds awareness of triggers or warning signs and practises a competing response that interferes with the unwanted behaviour. The NHS describes habit reversal therapy for tics as learning to recognise when a tic is occurring or imminent and using an intentional competing movement; its guidance on trichotillomania similarly describes identifying triggers and replacing hair pulling with another action.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/tics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nhs.uk">[nhs.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nhs.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nhs.uk.</span></span></span>

That does not mean everyday procrastination or snacking should be medicalised. Rather, these behavioural treatments demonstrate an important principle at the stronger end of the habit spectrum: **a competing response becomes more credible when cue recognition, repeated practice, environmental adjustment and support are developed together**.

A useful escalation rule is therefore to treat repeated failures as information. First, check whether the plan identifies the actual trigger and specifies an executable replacement rather than merely saying “don't”. If the same old response continues to win, reduce exposure to its cues or make the unwanted action less immediately available. If the behaviour involves dependence, causes significant distress or impairment, or repeatedly defeats self-directed attempts to change it, evidence-based behavioural or clinical support is more appropriate than endlessly refining an if-then statement.

## The practical limit of obstacle planning

If-then obstacle plans are valuable partly because they can prepare a response *before* a familiar temptation or distraction arrives. But strong habits expose the boundary of that technique. Experimental evidence indicates that plans can weaken the advantage enjoyed by habitual responses, while direct studies of habit strength show that their behavioural effects diminish as the opposing habit becomes stronger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18851764/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Planning to break unwanted habits: habit strength moderates implementation intention effects on behaviour change - PubMed...</span></span></span>

The distinction is therefore not **“if-then plans work” versus “if-then plans do not work”**. It is a question of what they are being asked to overcome. For a moderately practised distraction, a well-chosen cue and concrete replacement may be enough. For an entrenched behaviour repeatedly triggered by the same surroundings, the more realistic strategy is to combine the plan with changes that weaken the old cue-response pathway: identify the precise trigger, prepare a genuine competing action, alter the environment where possible and add structured support when the behaviour warrants it.

In that sense, a failed obstacle plan can still be useful diagnostic information. When the planned response repeatedly loses to the same cue, the next improvement is often not a more determined promise. It is to **change the behavioural contest itself**.
