## Introduction

Progress tracking does improve goal attainment, but the best broad estimate is **meaningful rather than dramatic**. The strongest experiment-based synthesis is Benjamin Harkin and colleagues’ meta-analysis of 138 randomised studies involving 19,951 participants. Interventions designed to increase progress monitoring improved subsequent goal attainment by an average standardised effect of **d = 0.40**, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.32 to 0.48.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span>

There is an important qualification. Statistical adjustment for possible publication bias reduced the estimated effect to **d = 0.19**, while a separate analysis restricted to better-powered studies produced **d = 0.33**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">White Rose Research OnlineDoes Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence.July 4, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 2026</span></span></span> So the evidence supports tracking as a useful self-regulation tool, but not a guaranteed or enormous boost. A sensible reading is that its average benefit probably lies somewhere between **small and moderate**, with substantial variation depending on how tracking is done and what goal is being pursued.

## What the 138-study meta-analysis found

The Harkin meta-analysis is unusually useful for answering “does tracking actually help?” because it focused on experiments rather than simply comparing habitual trackers with non-trackers. Participants had to be randomly allocated to an intervention intended to increase monitoring or to a control condition, and the study had to measure both the resulting change in monitoring and subsequent goal attainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span>

The intervention worked extremely well at changing the immediate behaviour it targeted: **progress-monitoring frequency increased by d = 1.98** (95% CI 1.71 to 2.24). The effect on the thing people ultimately cared about — goal attainment — was considerably smaller at **d = 0.40** (95% CI 0.32 to 0.48).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/91437/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">White Rose Research OnlineDoes Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence - White Ros...</span></span></span>

That distinction is important. Tracking can produce a very large change in *how much people track* without producing an equally large change in *what they achieve*. A diary, pedometer, checklist or other monitoring intervention is therefore not a twofold increase in success or a 40% improvement in somebody's outcome. **Cohen's d is a standardised difference between groups**, not a percentage increase in the probability of succeeding.

The result was also reasonably similar when the researchers separated different kinds of goal attainment. Among studies measuring behaviour itself, such as whether people performed a desired action, the pooled effect was **d = 0.37** across 35 studies and 5,518 participants. Among studies measuring outcomes, the estimate was **d = 0.44** across 86 studies and 10,593 participants. Both groups of studies nevertheless showed substantial variation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/291335719_Does_Monitoring_Goal_Progress_Promote_Goal_Attainment_A_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Experimental_Evidence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

There was another reason to take the relationship seriously. Across the studies for which the required data could be obtained, increases in monitoring frequency statistically mediated the effect of the interventions on goal attainment: interventions predicted more monitoring, greater monitoring predicted better attainment, and accounting for increased monitoring reduced the direct intervention–attainment association.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul%20harkin%20raw%20final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span> This does not establish every link in the causal mechanism beyond doubt, but it fits the proposition the experiments were designed to test: **getting people to monitor more often appears to be part of why monitoring interventions improve results**.

## Why the effect ranges from modest to meaningful

An average of d = 0.40 is best understood as a moderate shift across a diverse collection of experiments, not as a promise about what will happen to one person's particular goal. The meta-analysis itself provides useful clues about why results differed.

Recording mattered. In the authors' moderator analysis, studies in which progress was **physically recorded** had an average goal-attainment effect of **d = 0.43**, compared with **d = 0.29** where monitoring was not recorded. The difference between those categories was statistically significant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/838723090/Monitoring" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monitoring Goal Progress and Attainment &#124; PDF &#124; Goal &#124; Goal Setting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monitoring Goal Progress and Attainment &#124; PDF &#124; Goal &#124; Goal SettingOctober 19, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2015</span></span></span>

Reporting progress also mattered. Monitoring that remained private and was **not reported** produced an average effect of **d = 0.19**. When privately monitored progress was nevertheless **reported to somebody**, the estimate was **d = 0.47**. The small group of three studies classified as public monitoring produced d = 0.55, although its confidence interval was very wide, so that particular number should not be treated as a precise estimate of the advantage of public accountability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/838723090/Monitoring" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monitoring Goal Progress and Attainment &#124; PDF &#124; Goal &#124; Goal Setting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monitoring Goal Progress and Attainment &#124; PDF &#124; Goal &#124; Goal SettingOctober 19, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2015</span></span></span> The American Psychological Association's summary of the research consequently highlighted recording and reporting as conditions associated with larger effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals?AreaStudy=COMM&amp;Program=CSTC&amp;abtestactive=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apa.org">[American Psychological Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Psychological AssociationFrequently monitoring progress toward goals increases chance of successOctober 28, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 28, 2015</span></span></span>

These subgroup findings help explain why “tracking works” is too crude a conclusion. A mental check-in that disappears immediately may not be equivalent to maintaining a written record. Nor is silently noticing progress necessarily equivalent to knowing that the result will be reported. The overall d = 0.40 combines interventions with quite different levels of structure and accountability.

At the same time, these moderator results require more caution than the headline randomised comparison. Participants were randomised to monitoring interventions versus controls, but the 138 studies were not collectively one enormous experiment randomly assigning people to “recorded” versus “unrecorded” or “reported” versus “unreported” tracking. Those comparisons describe differences **between sets of studies**, so they are suggestive about what makes tracking stronger rather than definitive estimates of the causal contribution of each feature.

## Publication bias pulls the headline estimate down

The most important reason not to present d = 0.40 as the final word is the meta-analysis's own bias analysis.

Comparing published with unpublished research initially looked reassuring. The 129 published studies produced a goal-attainment estimate of **d = 0.40**, while the nine unpublished studies produced **d = 0.42** — essentially no difference. However, a statistical test for funnel-plot asymmetry, Egger's regression, was significant for goal attainment. That raised the possibility that smaller or unfavourable results were underrepresented in the available literature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul%20harkin%20raw%20final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The researchers therefore applied a “trim-and-fill” analysis, a method that estimates how the pooled result might change if apparently missing studies were added to make the distribution more symmetrical. It imputed **31 additional goal-attainment effect sizes** and reduced the estimate from **d = 0.40 to d = 0.19** (95% CI 0.10 to 0.28).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">White Rose Research OnlineDoes Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence.July 4, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 2026</span></span></span>

Taken literally, that is a substantial downgrade: from an effect often described as small-to-medium to one that is clearly small.

But the adjusted figure is not automatically the “true” answer either. Harkin and colleagues pointed out that trim-and-fill can underestimate a genuine positive effect when there is substantial between-study heterogeneity — precisely the situation in this dataset. They therefore interpreted the publication-bias problem as modest rather than severe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">White Rose Research OnlineDoes Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence.July 4, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 2026</span></span></span>

A separate sensitivity check offers something of a middle ground. Seventy studies met the authors' criterion for having at least 55% statistical power to detect a medium-sized effect. Within this better-powered subset, the estimated effect on goal attainment was **d = 0.33**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/291335719_Does_Monitoring_Goal_Progress_Promote_Goal_Attainment_A_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Experimental_Evidence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Those analyses make a range more informative than a single headline figure:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **d = 0.40** — the main random-effects estimate across all 138 studies.
* **d = 0.33** — the estimate among the 70 studies meeting the specified power criterion.
* **d = 0.19** — the trim-and-fill estimate after adjustment for possible missing studies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">White Rose Research OnlineDoes Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence.July 4, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 2026</span></span></span>

</div>

These numbers are not three competing replications measuring exactly the same thing. They come from different analyses of the same evidence base. Together, however, they make the uncertainty visible: the evidence supports a positive effect, while the exact size is less secure than quoting d = 0.40 alone would suggest.

## Heterogeneity limits what the average can tell you

The pooled effect also conceals unusually wide variation between experiments. For goal attainment, the meta-analysis reported significant heterogeneity, **Q(137) = 837.77, p<.001**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/291335719_Does_Monitoring_Goal_Progress_Promote_Goal_Attainment_A_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Experimental_Evidence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span> In practical terms, the studies were not behaving as though every monitoring intervention produced one common effect plus random sampling noise.

That is unsurprising given what was being combined. Progress monitoring could involve diaries, tracking sheets, electronic devices and other methods, while the underlying goals and outcomes differed considerably. The researchers therefore used a **random-effects model**, which is appropriate when the true intervention effect is expected to vary across studies rather than being identical everywhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span>

The evidence base was also much stronger in some domains than others. In their limitations, Harkin and colleagues specifically noted a shortage of evidence for behaviours outside health-related areas and for particular forms of monitoring. Only **three studies**, for example, examined monitoring the *rate* of progress. They also cautioned that their moderator analyses did not correct for the increased risk of false-positive findings created by conducting multiple tests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/291335719_Does_Monitoring_Goal_Progress_Promote_Goal_Attainment_A_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Experimental_Evidence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This matters for self-improvement goals such as learning an instrument, completing a personal project or building a creative practice. The meta-analysis gives good broad evidence for the proposition that **interventions which increase monitoring can improve attainment**, but its numerical average should not be treated as a precisely validated effect size for every everyday personal goal.

## What number should readers remember?

If one figure is needed, **d = 0.40** is the appropriate headline because it is the prespecified pooled estimate from the 138 randomised studies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span> But it should travel with its qualifications.

The more defensible summary is that deliberately increasing progress monitoring appears to produce a **small-to-moderate average improvement in goal attainment**. The main analysis put the effect at 0.40 standard deviations; a restriction to adequately powered studies put it at 0.33; and a publication-bias adjustment lowered it to 0.19. Substantial heterogeneity means none of these figures predicts exactly how much an individual goal will benefit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">White Rose Research OnlineDoes Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence.July 4, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 2026</span></span></span>

That is still useful evidence for “[self improvement that works]({{ 'self-improvement-that-works/' | relative_url }})”. Progress tracking is not merely a productivity ritual supported by anecdotes: the broad experimental literature indicates that deliberately increasing it can improve follow-through. But the data support **a useful nudge, not a transformation**. The strongest claim the evidence comfortably supports is that tracking shifts the odds in a favourable direction — particularly when progress is actually recorded or reported — while leaving plenty of room for the goal, context and design of the tracking system to determine how large that advantage becomes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/838723090/Monitoring" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monitoring Goal Progress and Attainment &#124; PDF &#124; Goal &#124; Goal Setting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monitoring Goal Progress and Attainment &#124; PDF &#124; Goal &#124; Goal SettingOctober 19, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 19, 2015</span></span></span>
