## Introduction

Reporting progress to another person can make tracking more effective than keeping the same information entirely private. The best broad evidence comes from a meta-analysis of 138 randomised studies involving 19,951 people: progress-monitoring interventions improved goal attainment overall, but the effect was substantially larger when people reported their monitoring results to someone else or monitored them publicly. Privately monitored and reported progress had an average effect size of **d = 0.47**, compared with **d = 0.19** when privately monitored information stayed private.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

That does not mean that broadcasting a goal guarantees success, or that everyone needs a demanding “accountability partner”. Reporting appears useful because it changes what happens to the record: somebody else will see it, making missed actions harder to quietly reinterpret or forget. The most defensible practical approach is therefore modest: track a concrete behaviour, send a brief factual update to a supportive person at an agreed interval, and use the conversation to decide what happens next.

## Reported progress performed better than private tracking

The 2016 *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis by Benjamin Harkin and colleagues provides the clearest evidence specific to this question. It distinguished three arrangements: monitoring in a genuinely public setting; monitoring privately and then reporting the information to at least one other person; and monitoring privately without reporting it. Examples included weighing in front of a weight-loss group, privately measuring something and telephoning the result to a researcher, and privately measuring it without telling anybody.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The differences were notable. Public monitoring produced an average goal-attainment effect of **d = 0.55**, although this estimate came from only three studies and therefore deserves caution. Private monitoring followed by reporting, represented by a much larger body of evidence, produced **d = 0.47** across 95 studies. Private monitoring that was not reported produced **d = 0.19** across 14 studies. The reported-versus-unreported difference was statistically significant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

This is more informative than simply observing that highly motivated people tend to find accountability partners. The meta-analysis included studies in which participants were randomly allocated to interventions intended to increase progress monitoring. Across the full evidence base, those interventions increased monitoring substantially (**d = 1.98**) and improved goal attainment (**d = 0.40**); increases in monitoring frequency statistically mediated improvements in attainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMed...</span></span></span>

There is an important limitation, however. The reported-versus-private figures are **moderator findings across different studies and intervention designs**, not the result of one enormous experiment that randomly assigned thousands of otherwise identical people to “tell someone” or “keep it secret”. Harkin and colleagues explicitly called for direct comparisons that could identify whether accountability itself causes the extra benefit and which psychological mechanism explains it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

Smaller experiments reinforce the need for that caution. In a randomised pilot involving 69 patients using an app to conduct skin self-examinations, some participants had an accountability partner who received monthly progress reports. Self-examination increased across the study, from 58% at baseline to 83% after six months, but there was **no significant difference between intervention groups**: adding an accountability partner did not outperform the app alone in this small trial. Satisfaction was also lowest in the accountability-partner-only group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30062632/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Piloting the Use of Smartphones, Reminders, and Accountability Partners to Promote Skin Self-Examinations in Patients with Total Bo...</span></span></span>

So “accountability always works” is too strong. A better reading is that **reported monitoring is a promising amplifier of tracking, with strong meta-analytic evidence of association between intervention design and larger effects, but individual accountability arrangements do not reliably add value in every setting**.

## Why another person can change the meaning of a tracker

A private tracker supplies information. A reported tracker supplies information **plus a social consequence**. If you planned three study sessions and completed one, a private record can be closed and forgotten. If you have agreed to send “1/3 completed” to somebody on Sunday, the discrepancy has an audience.

Harkin and colleagues proposed several possible explanations for the larger effects of reported and public monitoring: stronger public commitment, a feeling of personal accountability, concern about how one presents oneself to other people, and, in experiments, the possibility that participants respond to what they think researchers expect. The meta-analysis could not establish which mechanism was responsible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

More recent research helps fill in part of that picture. In four longitudinal studies involving **1,035 participants**, Johanna Peetz, Roger Buehler and Tayler Wells examined what happened when people told others about personal goals. In one observational study, disclosure predicted receiving more support and putting in greater goal-pursuit effort. In two studies that instructed participants to share a goal, sharing led to greater support from other people, which was in turn associated with greater effort and progress. In a fourth study, telling a person who was physically present increased subsequent goal-pursuit effort, although it did not increase reported support in that particular experiment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41143471/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Goals Out Loud: Telling Others About a Goal Increases Support Received and Facilitates Goal Pursuit - PubMedOctober 27, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2025</span></span></span>

That evidence concerns **goal disclosure**, rather than repeated progress reporting, so the two should not be treated as identical interventions. It nevertheless identifies a useful interpersonal pathway: once another person knows what you are trying to do, that person can potentially encourage you, remind you, discuss obstacles or otherwise make the goal more socially present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672251382271" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Goals Out Loud: Telling Others About a Goal Increases Support Received and Facilitates Goal Pursuit - Johanna Peetz, Roger Buehler, Ta...</span></span></span>

Publicity can also increase commitment after things go wrong. Three experiments reported by Jinfeng Jiao and Catherine Cole found that people high in “self-monitoring” — here meaning sensitivity to social presentation, rather than simply recording progress — were more likely to persist following a **public** goal failure than a private one. The public/private distinction did not produce the same effect among low self-monitors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cb.1806" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryThe Effects of Goal Publicity and Self‐Monitoring on Escalation of Goal Commitment - Jiao - 2020 - Journal of Consume...</span></span></span> This suggests an important boundary: [social visibility]({{ 'social-visibility/' | relative_url }}) does not exert the same motivational force on everybody.

## Accountability is not the same as announcing a goal

The useful distinction is between **telling somebody what you intend to do** and **showing somebody what actually happened**.

A one-off announcement — “I am going to run three times a week” — creates visibility around the intention. A reporting system creates repeated contact with evidence: “I planned three runs; I completed two.” The latter preserves the [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) function of progress tracking while adding another person as an audience.

That distinction matters because research on public goals is not uniformly positive. The literature includes circumstances in which publicity changes commitment or persistence differently depending on the individual and the form of disclosure. The 2020 experiments on public failure, for example, found effects conditional on participants' self-monitoring tendencies rather than a universal benefit from publicity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cb.1806" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryThe Effects of Goal Publicity and Self‐Monitoring on Escalation of Goal Commitment - Jiao - 2020 - Journal of Consume...</span></span></span> The recent goal-disclosure research likewise stresses that the **audience and manner of disclosure** matter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41143471/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Goals Out Loud: Telling Others About a Goal Increases Support Received and Facilitates Goal Pursuit - PubMedOctober 27, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2025</span></span></span>

There is also a practical difference between accountability and surveillance. The Harkin meta-analysis classified progress as “reported” when privately obtained monitoring information was given to **at least one other person**. It did not require a large audience, public social-media post or competitive group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span> That makes the evidence compatible with a much quieter arrangement: one trusted friend, colleague, coach or study partner can provide the relevant social visibility.

A widely circulated small study by Gail Matthews illustrates this format particularly clearly. Participants were assigned to five goal-setting conditions, ranging from merely thinking about goals to writing goals and sending weekly progress reports to a supportive friend. Among the 149 participants who completed the study, the group sending weekly progress reports reported greater goal accomplishment than the groups using less intensive arrangements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dominican.edu/sites/default/files/2020-02/gailmatthews-harvard-goals-researchsummary.pdf?previewmode=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dominican.edu">[Dominican University of California]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dominican.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of California Goals Research</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dominican University of CaliforniaGoals Research SummaryJuly 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 2026</span></span></span> This is useful supporting evidence, but it should not carry the weight sometimes given to it online: it was a relatively small study with substantial non-completion, and the broader Harkin meta-analysis provides the stronger foundation.

## A useful check-in should make progress visible, not punitive

For self-improvement, the evidence points towards a fairly lightweight design. The purpose is not to recruit someone to police your behaviour. It is to make the tracking record consequential enough that you continue to collect it, look at it and respond to what it says.

A practical accountability arrangement can therefore stay simple:

* **Choose one observable measure.** Report something close to the behaviour: “3 of 4 planned workouts completed”, “four 25-minute study sessions”, or “five days without missing the morning practice”. Concrete numbers reduce the temptation to turn a check-in into a vague assessment of whether the week “felt productive”.
* **Agree on the reporting rhythm beforehand.** A weekly update often makes more sense for recurring habits than constant messaging. The interval should be short enough that a miss can still influence the next attempt.
* **Report misses as readily as successes.** If accountability makes people hide disappointing results, it defeats the purpose of monitoring. “Planned 4, completed 2” is useful information; an elaborate justification is usually not.
* **Ask for a small response rather than supervision.** Acknowledgement, one question about an obstacle, or a brief discussion of the [next action]({{ 'next-action/' | relative_url }}) can keep the process social without turning the other person into an unpaid manager. Goal-sharing research suggests that support from others can be one route from disclosure to greater effort.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41143471/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Goals Out Loud: Telling Others About a Goal Increases Support Received and Facilitates Goal Pursuit - PubMedOctober 27, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2025</span></span></span>
* **Keep the record as well as sending it.** Reporting and recording need not be alternatives. Harkin and colleagues found larger goal-attainment effects both when progress was reported or public and when monitoring information was physically recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

The tone matters as much as the mechanics. The skin-examination trial is a useful warning against assuming that more accountability automatically produces a better experience: adding accountability partners did not significantly improve the behavioural result and the accountability-only condition had the lowest satisfaction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30062632/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Piloting the Use of Smartphones, Reminders, and Accountability Partners to Promote Skin Self-Examinations in Patients with Total Bo...</span></span></span> A check-in that creates disproportionate embarrassment, nagging or interpersonal friction may simply become something the person starts avoiding.

## The best accountability preserves honest feedback

The strongest case for reporting progress is not that social pressure is magically motivating. It is that progress tracking already works by confronting intentions with evidence, and another person can make that evidence harder to disregard.

Across the large progress-monitoring meta-analysis, keeping monitoring information entirely private was associated with a considerably smaller goal-attainment effect than reporting privately collected information to somebody else.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/1/bul" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eprints.whiterose.ac.uk">[White Rose Research Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eprints.whiterose.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whiterose.ac.uk.</span></span></span> More recent research adds a complementary explanation: telling another person about a goal can elicit support and is associated with greater effort and progress, although effects vary by setting and by how disclosure occurs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41143471/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Goals Out Loud: Telling Others About a Goal Increases Support Received and Facilitates Goal Pursuit - PubMedOctober 27, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 27, 2025</span></span></span>

For everyday self-improvement, that argues for **specific, low-pressure accountability rather than maximum publicity**. Record the behaviour you intended to perform, let one suitable person see the result on a predictable schedule, and treat a disappointing report as information for the next attempt rather than as a failure to defend. The added value is not the audience itself. It is the recurring expectation that the gap between the plan and reality will be seen, acknowledged and acted upon.
