## Introduction

A workspace can do part of tomorrow’s [remembering]({{ 'remembering/' | relative_url }}) for you. Instead of ending the day with an unfinished task held mainly as a mental intention — “carry on with the analysis”, “send that document”, “check those figures” — you can encode the intention into something you are likely to encounter when work resumes: the open file, a note beside the relevant passage, an object placed somewhere unusual, or a deliberately positioned digital document.

Researchers call the broader strategy **intention offloading**: creating an external cue so that remembering a future action does not depend entirely on prospective memory. Reviews find that external reminders can substantially improve fulfilment of delayed intentions, particularly when memory load or distraction makes unaided remembering less dependable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27542527/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Cognitive Offloading</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cognitive Offloading - PubMedAugust 16, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 16, 2016</span></span></span>

The practical point is not that every unfinished task needs a reminder. It is that a well-designed workspace can preserve the thread of work across an overnight interruption. The best cue is noticeable when work resumes, connected to the task it represents, and specific enough to recover the intended [next action]({{ 'next-action/' | relative_url }}) without another round of reconstruction.

## Why future intentions are easy to lose

Remembering something you have already learnt and remembering that you need to *do something later* are different problems. The latter is **prospective memory**: remembering to execute an intention when the appropriate time or situation arrives. Unlike an ordinary memory question, there may be nobody prompting you to retrieve the information. You have to notice that the moment for action has arrived and recover what you intended to do.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20438269/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Focal/nonfocal cue effects in prospective memory: monitoring difficulty or different retrieval processes? - PubMed...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters when work crosses a boundary such as an evening, weekend or meeting. At 17:30, the significance of a spreadsheet, half-written paragraph or unresolved question may be completely obvious because its context is active in your mind. Tomorrow morning, you may remember the project while failing to retrieve the precise state in which you left it: which discrepancy mattered, what you had ruled out, or what you intended to try next.

Prospective-memory experiments also show why simply deciding to remember is an imperfect strategy. Remembering future intentions competes with whatever else occupies attention. In Sam Gilbert's experiments on intention offloading, participants became more likely to create reminders when they had more intentions to hold in memory and when interruptions were introduced. Crucially, those were also circumstances in which unaided memory performed worse. Across four experiments involving 1,196 participants, setting external reminders improved performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25404057/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Strategic offloading of delayed intentions into the external environment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Strategic offloading of delayed intentions into the external environment - PubMedNovember 18, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 18, 2014</span></span></span>

More recent work reaches a similar conclusion for time-based intentions. In experiments where people had to remember an action while simultaneously performing a working-memory task, reminders increased accuracy and became more attractive as the delay grew longer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-025-01708-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Strategic reminder setting for time-based intentions: Influence of metacognition, delay length, and cue visibility &#124; Memory &amp; Cog...</span></span></span>

This suggests a useful interpretation of end-of-day preparation. Leaving a cue is not merely organising the desk. It changes the memory problem. Rather than requiring tomorrow’s self to spontaneously retrieve an intention, the environment presents information capable of prompting retrieval.

## Turning files, notes and objects into reminders

An external reminder works because some part of the intention is moved into the environment. The principle is broader than conventional alerts. Research on cognitive and intention offloading explicitly includes diaries, lists, sticky notes, digital alerts and strategically positioned physical objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-022-02139-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Outsourcing Memory to External Tools: A Review of ‘Intention Offloading’ &#124; Psychonomic Bulletin &amp; Review &#124; Springer Nature LinkJu...</span></span></span>

A workspace therefore offers several natural places to store an unfinished intention.

**Put the instruction where the work will happen.** If a report is unfinished, a note inside the report saying “Next: reconcile Table 2 with the June spreadsheet” carries more useful information into tomorrow than a separate task saying “continue report”. For code, the equivalent might be a comment beside the failing function. For research, it could be a note attached to the source that still needs checking.

**Use position as information.** A document deliberately placed at the top of a working folder, a paper left on the keyboard or an item placed beside the bag that must leave with you can function as a cue because its location makes it difficult to encounter the workspace without encountering the reminder. Gilbert's experimental intention-offloading paradigm deliberately models this idea: participants could reposition items in their environment so that their later location prompted the required action. The researchers compare the mechanism with placing an object by the front door so that it reminds you to take it when leaving.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4448673/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Strategic offloading of delayed intentions into the external environment - PMC...</span></span></span>

**Preserve state as well as task identity.** There is an important difference between remembering *that* a project is unfinished and recovering *where to restart it*. A workspace cue is especially useful when it captures the latter. “Budget” identifies a project; “Next: find why contractor costs differ by £4,800 between these two sheets” restores a working problem. That distinction follows from prospective-memory research separating the need to notice that an action is required from the need to remember what that action is.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4448673/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Strategic offloading of delayed intentions into the external environment - PMC...</span></span></span>

A useful cue can therefore contain both components: **the trigger and the action**. “When I reopen this file, check the July total against invoice 184” does more than record information. It links the situation tomorrow will present with the behaviour you want that situation to elicit.

That resembles an **implementation intention**, an if-then plan linking a specified future cue with an intended response. Research has repeatedly found that such encoding can improve prospective remembering, apparently in part by strengthening the association between cue and response.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18604955/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Implementation intentions facilitate prospective memory under high attention demands - PubMedJune 1, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2008</span></span></span> The effect should not be overstated as perfectly automatic: experiments have also found that implementation intentions do not eliminate performance losses under heavy cognitive demands.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.3758/MC.38.2.221" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on doi.org.</span></span></span> The safer conclusion is that a strong cue-action link helps retrieval; it does not make attention irrelevant.

## What makes a workspace cue hard to miss?

The temptation is to equate an effective reminder with a conspicuous reminder. Prospective-memory research suggests a more useful distinction: what matters is not merely whether a cue is visually striking, but whether normal activity directs attention towards the feature that carries the intention.

Research comparing **focal** and **non-focal** prospective-memory cues illustrates this. A focal cue is processed naturally as part of what someone is already doing; a non-focal cue requires attention to a feature that the ongoing activity would not otherwise emphasise. Experiments have found that focal cues can support relatively strong prospective remembering without the same degree of continual monitoring demanded by non-focal cues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20438269/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Focal/nonfocal cue effects in prospective memory: monitoring difficulty or different retrieval processes? - PubMed...</span></span></span> Other work indicates that simply increasing visual salience is not equivalent to making a cue focal to the activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0001691817301580" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

For workspace design, that points towards **encounter probability**, not decoration, as the central criterion. A reminder is strong when resuming work naturally brings you into contact with it.

A sticky note buried among twelve other sticky notes may be colourful yet weak. A sentence at the exact point where you will resume writing may be visually modest yet extremely well placed. Likewise, an open browser tab can be a poor reminder if you routinely return to dozens of tabs, whereas putting the required document in the project's obvious starting location may make encountering it almost unavoidable.

Three questions are particularly useful when leaving a cue:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Will I encounter it naturally?** Put the cue on tomorrow’s path into the task rather than somewhere you must remember to check.
* **Will I understand it without reconstructing today’s context?** “Check this” is fragile; “Check whether the 12 July invoice explains the £4,800 difference” carries its own context.
* **Does it point to an action?** A cue should ideally make the first behaviour apparent, rather than merely announce that unfinished work exists.

</div>

This also explains why deliberately unusual object placement can work. A notebook sitting in its normal storage place contains little new information. The same notebook positioned across the keyboard can acquire signalling value because its location violates expectation. But that advantage depends on preserving the signal. If the desk is routinely covered with displaced objects, unusual placement ceases to be unusual.

## Do not turn the workspace into a reminder wall

Intention offloading is effective, but more offloading is not automatically better. Creating and maintaining reminders has costs, and research finds that people make trade-offs between the effort or cost of setting reminders and the expected memory benefit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-022-02139-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Outsourcing Memory to External Tools: A Review of ‘Intention Offloading’ &#124; Psychonomic Bulletin &amp; Review &#124; Springer Nature LinkJu...</span></span></span> A workspace covered in cues also creates a practical problem: every item competes to be treated as significant.

The aim is therefore not to externalise every future intention. Workspace cues are most valuable for intentions that are both easy to lose and useful to recover immediately: the unresolved question in a document, the file required for the first task, the physical item that must leave the office, or the exact next operation in a complicated piece of work.

There is another reason to be selective. Research suggests that reminder setting is partly **metacognitive**: people decide whether to offload according not only to their actual memory performance but also to their beliefs and confidence about how well they will remember. Those judgements can be imperfect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-022-02139-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[Springer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Outsourcing Memory to External Tools: A Review of ‘Intention Offloading’ &#124; Psychonomic Bulletin &amp; Review &#124; Springer Nature LinkJu...</span></span></span> A 2024 study extending this work to real-world intentions likewise found evidence that metacognition helps guide whether people create reminders for their plans.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39023988/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

A sensible rule is therefore to use cues where tomorrow would otherwise require significant retrieval or reconstruction, not simply wherever a reminder can be added. The scarce resource is not sticky notes or digital annotations; it is the ability of a cue to stand out as meaningful when it matters.

## Preserve the thread, not the whole plan

There is a subtle benefit to recording the next action before stopping. Unfinished goals can remain mentally active after work has ceased. In experiments by E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister, unfulfilled goals produced intrusive thoughts and interference with unrelated activities. Allowing participants to make specific plans for those goals reduced these effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

That finding should not be stretched into a claim that leaving a sticky note will reliably stop work-related rumination; the experiments studied plan making rather than workspace arrangement itself. But it supports the underlying logic of making an unfinished intention concrete before disengaging. You do not need to keep rehearsing “remember where I was” if the information needed for resumption has been captured somewhere dependable.

This is where workspace cues fit particularly well within preparation for tomorrow. The objective is not a comprehensive evening planning system. It is a small transfer of responsibility from memory to environment.

Before stopping, identify the piece of context that tomorrow’s self is most likely to lack. Put that information at the point where tomorrow’s work will resume. If necessary, reposition the relevant file or object so encountering it becomes part of starting work. Then make the cue specific enough to recover an action rather than merely a topic.

The resulting workspace acts less like storage and more like a handover. Today's working memory leaves tomorrow a visible instruction: **this is where you were, and this is what happens next.**
