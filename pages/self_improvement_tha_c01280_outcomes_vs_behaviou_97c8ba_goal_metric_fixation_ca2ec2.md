## Introduction

A goal can improve performance by concentrating attention on what matters. The same mechanism can also become a liability: once a number becomes the dominant definition of success, people may optimise the number rather than the purpose it was supposed to represent. Researchers describe related problems as goal fixation, goal displacement and metric gaming. Goodhart’s law captures the basic measurement problem: when a measure becomes a consequential target, the behaviour surrounding that measure changes, potentially making it less informative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15309576.2020.1749092" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tandfonline.com.</span></span></span>

For self-improvement, gaming need not mean deliberate cheating. It can be as ordinary as choosing easy books to preserve a reading streak, doing low-value tasks to hit a productivity count, or training in ways that improve a fitness-app statistic while neglecting recovery. The important question is therefore not merely, “Did the number improve?” It is, “Does improving this number still mean that the underlying thing I care about is improving?”

## How targets narrow attention

Goals work partly because they are selective. They tell the mind that one result deserves attention and effort. Research on “goal shielding” found that activating a strongly held focal goal can inhibit the accessibility of alternative goals. That can be useful: protecting one priority from distractions helps people persist. But the same selectivity becomes dangerous when the alternatives being screened out are actually important parts of success.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12500810/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forgetting all else: on the antecedents and consequences of goal shielding - PubMed...</span></span></span>

This is why metric fixation is not simply a failure of discipline. It can emerge from successful discipline applied too narrowly. A runner intent on a weekly mileage target may continue adding kilometres when recovery would be wiser. Someone measuring productivity by tasks completed may become extremely efficient at clearing trivial tasks. A student targeting hours studied can spend the required time at a desk without checking whether the study method produces durable learning.

The problem becomes particularly clear when performance has several dimensions but the target captures only one. Experimental work has found quantity–quality trade-offs under some goal conditions: in six experiments reported by Janet Bavelas and Eric Lee, higher goals increased the number of responses on several creativity tasks while the average quality of responses moved further from the experimenters’ ideal. That does not establish a universal law that quantity targets reduce quality — other tasks can produce different results — but it illustrates the underlying danger of defining success on one dimension of a multidimensional activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/22390731_Effects_of_goal_level_on_performance_A_trade-off_of_quantity_and_quality" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) Effects of goal level on performance: A trade-off of quantity and qualityDecember 1, 1978...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 1978</span></span></span>

A review of goal setting in the workplace describes the same practical issue: quantity goals can pull attention away from quality, while multiple goals do not automatically solve the problem because people may concentrate on dimensions that are easier to measure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wol.iza.org/articles/goal-setting-and-worker-motivation/long" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wol.iza.org">[World of Labour]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wol.iza.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World of LabourWorld of Labour - Goal setting and worker motivation...</span></span></span>

The paradox is that this attentional narrowing is also one reason goals can work so well. Recent experiments on sustained attention, for example, have found that specific, difficult task goals can reduce behavioural lapses and improve performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37872435/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Effects of goal-setting on sustained attention and attention lapses</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of goal-setting on sustained attention and attention lapses - PubMedOctober 23, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 23, 2023</span></span></span> The sensible conclusion is therefore not that focus is harmful. It is that **a goal directs attention towards whatever has been specified, not necessarily towards everything that ultimately matters**.

## Why unmeasured priorities disappear

Metrics are proxies. Body weight is not health. Words written are not good writing. Books completed are not knowledge. Hours worked are not useful output. Money saved is not financial wellbeing in its entirety. A proxy can be extremely useful while remaining incomplete.

Trouble starts when the distinction between the proxy and its purpose fades. Research on public-sector targets provides unusually visible examples because organisations leave records of how people react to measurement systems. In their study of targets in the English public health-care system, Gwyn Bevan and Christopher Hood examined how performance regimes could produce gaming alongside genuine improvements. Their central warning was essentially that treating one measurable part as if it represented the whole creates predictable opportunities for distortion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9299.2006.00600.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryWHAT’S MEASURED IS WHAT MATTERS: TARGETS AND GAMING IN THE ENGLISH PUBLIC HEALTH CARE SYSTEM - BEVAN - 2006 - Public...</span></span></span>

Research on UK public-spending controls likewise describes “creative categorisation”: when administrative numbers carried consequences, actors could change how expenditure was classified in ways that helped satisfy the numerical rules. The underlying spending had not necessarily changed in the way an uninformed observer of the headline measure might assume.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15309576.2020.1749092" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tandfonline.com.</span></span></span>

This is the deeper meaning of metric gaming. There are at least three ways a target can appear to improve:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Real improvement:** the underlying objective genuinely gets better and the metric records it.
* **Proxy optimisation:** the measured feature improves, but other important dimensions stagnate or deteriorate.
* **Measurement manipulation:** the way activity is counted, classified or reported changes, producing a better number without corresponding substantive progress.

</div>

Personal goals are usually less formal than institutional performance systems, but the logic transfers readily. Suppose the objective is to read more because you want greater knowledge. “Books finished” is an easy proxy. Once 50 books a year becomes the emotionally important target, however, shorter and easier books become disproportionately attractive. Nothing dishonest has happened. The metric has simply begun influencing the activity it was meant to observe.

The same effect can become almost invisible with daily behaviours. A behaviour goal such as “write 500 words every morning” is more controllable than “publish a successful book”, which is one reason behaviour-based goals are useful. But controllability does not guarantee validity. If 500 words becomes sacrosanct, a writer may resist deleting weak material, researching a difficult point or spending a session restructuring a chapter because those valuable activities do not increase the chosen count.

The lesson is not to abandon behavioural measures. It is to remember their status: **the behaviour is valuable because it serves a larger purpose, not because it can be counted**.

## When a target starts changing the rules of the game

Metric fixation becomes more concerning when the target is difficult and missing it feels consequential. In a well-known series of laboratory studies, Maurice Schweitzer, Lisa Ordóñez and Bambi Douma found that participants with unmet goals were more likely to engage in unethical behaviour than participants instructed simply to do their best. The effect appeared both with and without financial incentives and was particularly pronounced among people who narrowly missed their target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/20159591" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.aom.org">[Academy of Management Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.aom.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aom.org.</span></span></span>

Later reviews have proposed several mechanisms connecting demanding performance goals with unethical behaviour, including reduced attention to moral considerations, moral disengagement, pressure on self-regulation and greater risk-taking. Incentives attached to goal attainment can increase the stakes further.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352250X15001633" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Immoral goals: how goal setting may lead to unethical behavior</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirect Immoral goals: how goal setting may lead to unethical behavior</span></span></span>

This does not mean that setting an ambitious personal target will ordinarily make someone dishonest. The broader claim that goal setting itself is dangerous has been disputed. In a prominent academic exchange, Ordóñez and colleagues argued that the benefits of specific, challenging goals had received more attention than side effects such as narrowed focus, unethical behaviour and distorted risk-taking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amp.2009.37007999" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.aom.org">[Academy of Management Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.aom.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aom.org.</span></span></span> Edwin Locke and Gary Latham, major figures in goal-setting research, responded that this critique relied too heavily on selected studies and anecdotes and that many limitations of goal setting were already recognised within the established theory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amp.2009.37008000" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.aom.org">[Academy of Management Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.aom.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aom.org.</span></span></span>

That dispute matters because the evidence does **not** justify replacing “specific goals are always good” with “specific goals are always dangerous”. Decades of goal-setting research show substantial performance benefits in appropriate conditions, and even critics of overprescribing goals acknowledge their motivating power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11152729_Building_a_practically_useful_theory_of_goal_setting_and_task_motivation_-_A_35-year_odyssey" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

A narrower conclusion is better supported: **the more consequential a metric becomes, the more important it is to examine the behaviours it creates around the edges**. A target can motivate genuine effort and simultaneously create incentives to neglect, redefine or manipulate aspects of performance that it does not capture.

## Warning signs that the metric is taking over

The clearest warning is a growing separation between **looking successful according to the measure** and **becoming successful according to the original purpose**. This can happen without conscious deception, so it is useful to examine the incentives built into a personal tracking system rather than relying on good intentions.

Several patterns deserve particular attention:

* **You choose activities because they count.** The reading target pushes you towards shorter books; the [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) streak makes an easy recorded session more attractive than needed recovery; the task counter rewards splitting trivial work into many items.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Useful work starts feeling like failure because it is invisible.** Planning, reflection, revision, recovery or learning may contribute greatly to the real objective while producing nothing on the dashboard.
* **You resist changing the metric after learning something important.** A target that was originally a tool becomes a commitment to proving that the original plan was right.
* **The threshold creates strange behaviour near the boundary.** Reaching 9,900 steps feels meaningfully different from 10,000 even when the underlying health difference is negligible. Thresholds can turn continuous progress into an artificial pass-or-fail judgement.
* **Quality declines while quantity rises.** More study hours accompany poorer retention; more workouts accompany persistent fatigue; more completed tasks coincide with less important work getting done.
* **Definitions become conveniently flexible.** What counts as a workout, productive hour, completed task or reading session gradually changes whenever the target is in danger.
* **You would behave differently if nobody — including your future self — could see the score.** That question helps expose when maintaining a record has displaced the activity the record was meant to support.

</div>

These signs are applications of a broader measurement principle rather than proof that every personal metric will be gamed. Studies of institutional measurement show why caution is warranted: once indicators become consequential, people adapt to them, sometimes through classification changes or strategic behaviour that weakens the connection between the score and the underlying objective.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/goodharts-law-and-the-gaming-of-uk-public-spending-numbers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kclpure.kcl.ac.uk">[kcl.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kclpure.kcl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on kcl.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

## Keep the number subordinate to the purpose

The practical defence against goal fixation is not to stop measuring progress. It is to design measurement so that no single number quietly acquires authority it does not deserve.

Start by stating what the metric is supposed to represent. “10,000 steps” might represent being consistently active; “500 words” might represent making steady progress on a manuscript; “five study sessions” might represent learning the material. Periodically ask whether the measured behaviour is still serving that purpose. That simple separation between **objective** and **indicator** makes gaming easier to notice.

It also helps to keep important counterweights visible. A runner tracking distance might also pay attention to recovery and signs of excessive fatigue. Someone tracking study time can periodically test recall rather than assuming that more hours mean more learning. A writer counting words can evaluate whether the manuscript itself is improving. The point is not to construct an enormous dashboard. Too many simultaneous targets create their own attentional problems. Instead, use a small number of checks that can reveal when improvement in the main metric is being purchased at an unacceptable cost. Research on multidimensional goals suggests that simply adding more metrics is not a complete solution, particularly when some dimensions remain much easier to measure than others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wol.iza.org/articles/goal-setting-and-worker-motivation/long" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wol.iza.org">[World of Labour]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wol.iza.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World of LabourWorld of Labour - Goal setting and worker motivation...</span></span></span>

Finally, treat targets as revisable instruments rather than contracts with your past self. A useful goal should change behaviour in the service of an objective. When behaviour begins changing primarily in the service of preserving the score, the hierarchy has reversed.

That distinction fits naturally within the broader choice between outcome goals and controllable daily behaviours. Daily behaviours are often better levers because they can actually be performed. Yet even a perfectly controllable behaviour can become dysfunctional when its count becomes more important than its function. The strongest self-improvement system therefore does not merely ask whether a behaviour is measurable and controllable. It keeps returning to the more important test: **is doing more of this, in the way the metric encourages, still producing the improvement the goal was created to achieve?**
