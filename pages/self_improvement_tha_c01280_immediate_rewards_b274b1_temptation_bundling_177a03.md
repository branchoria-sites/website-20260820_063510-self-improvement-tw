## Introduction

Temptation bundling makes [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) more immediately rewarding by reserving something genuinely enjoyable for the workout itself. Instead of asking the distant benefits of fitness to outweigh the immediate appeal of staying on the sofa, the method changes the choice: going to the gym becomes the way to hear the next chapter of an absorbing audiobook, watch a favourite programme or enjoy another compatible pleasure. The reward arrives during the behaviour rather than weeks or years later.

The best evidence comes from two field experiments built around audiobooks and gym attendance. The first found a large initial increase when access to tempting novels was physically restricted to the gym, although the effect weakened over time. A later, much larger experiment found smaller but more persistent gains when gym members were taught to create their own bundles. Together, they suggest that temptation bundling can make exercise easier to repeat, while also showing that the pairing has to remain tempting and credible if it is to last.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

## How temptation bundling works

A [temptation bundle]({{ 'routine-breaks/' | relative_url }}) combines a **“want”** with a **“should”**: an activity that is attractive immediately with one whose most important benefits arrive later. In the original exercise research, the want was listening to a page-turning audiobook and the should was going to the gym. The crucial feature was not merely having entertainment available while exercising. Access to the entertainment was tied to exercise, so wanting to continue the story could create an additional reason to return.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

That distinction separates temptation bundling from a conventional reward. “Exercise today and watch television afterwards” puts a prize behind the workout. “I watch this particular programme only while walking on the treadmill” makes exercise the gateway to the prize and allows both experiences to happen together. This matters because the workout acquires some of the attraction of an activity the person already wants to do. The researchers argued that combining the two can also reduce the sense that the indulgence is wasted time and distract from some of exercise's unpleasantness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

Restriction is therefore part of the mechanism. If the audiobook is equally available on the sofa, during the commute and in bed, there is no need to exercise to discover what happens next. Reserving it for workouts preserves its motivational value. Advice from Wharton based on the research similarly emphasises that temptation bundling works best when the indulgence can genuinely be restricted to the activity that needs extra motivation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/get-done-two-techniques-fast-track-goals/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu">[Wharton Knowledge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wharton Knowledge Get It Done: Two Techniques to Fast-track Your Goals</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wharton KnowledgeGet It Done: Two Techniques to Fast-track Your Goals - Knowledge at WhartonFebruary 28, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 28, 2022</span></span></span>

This does not mean the reward must be an audiobook. The useful design principle is **compatibility**: the pleasure should make the workout nicer without preventing the exercise itself. An engaging podcast or television series can fit steady cardio; favourite music may fit running or resistance training. Entertainment that demands constant visual attention is a much poorer match for activities where attention to surroundings, technique or safety matters. The research establishes the principle more strongly than it establishes any particular medium as universally best.

## What the gym audiobook experiments found

The original experiment by Katherine Milkman, Julia Minson and Kevin Volpp turned the idea into an unusually literal commitment device. The researchers recruited 226 people at a university fitness facility and randomly allocated them to three groups. One group received iPods containing four tempting audio novels that could be accessed **only at the gym**. A second group received the novels on their own devices and was encouraged to impose the gym-only rule on itself. The control group received a US$25 bookshop gift certificate of comparable value and encouragement to exercise. Gym entry records supplied an objective measure of attendance rather than relying on participants to remember their workouts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

The novels were deliberately chosen for their ability to pull listeners onwards. Participants selected from 82 pre-tested titles, including popular page-turners such as *The Hunger Games*, *The Da Vinci Code*, *Twilight* and *The Help*. That detail is central rather than decorative: a bundle has little motivational force if the supposedly tempting half is something the person feels indifferent about.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/researchers-used-hunger-games-encourage-healthier-choices/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu">[Wharton Knowledge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wharton Knowledge Using &#x27;The Hunger Games&#x27; to Encourage Healthier Choices</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wharton KnowledgeUsing &#x27;The Hunger Games&#x27; to Encourage Healthier Choices - Knowledge at WhartonNovember 19, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 19, 2013</span></span></span>

Initially, participants whose audiobook access was physically restricted to the gym visited **51% more frequently than controls**. Participants who were merely encouraged to restrict their listening visited **29% more frequently than controls**. In the first week, the control group averaged about 0.75 gym visits; regression-adjusted increases were 0.48 visits for the restricted-access group and 0.27 for the self-restriction group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2183859" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: papers.ssrn.com">[SSRN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">papers.ssrn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: an Evaluation of Temptation Bundling by Katherine L. Milkman, Julia A. Minson, Kevin Vol...</span></span></span>

The striking percentage should not be mistaken for evidence of a permanent 51% improvement. The treatment effect declined substantially over the nine-week experiment, particularly around the Thanksgiving interruption. Across the seven weeks before Thanksgiving, the estimated increase attributable to the full treatment was about 0.31 visits per week, falling to about 0.21 when the whole treatment period was considered. The authors explicitly concluded that the intervention's benefits tapered over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

That decline is one of the experiment's most useful findings for habit design. The researchers considered the possibility that time away from the audiobook reduced craving for the story, weakening its ability to pull people back to the gym. Whatever the exact explanation, the interruption shows that temptation bundling is not a magical switch that permanently transforms motivation. Its leverage depends partly on the “want” continuing to feel like a want.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

There was another revealing result. At the end of the study, **61% of participants were willing to pay something for a month of gym-only access to an iPod containing a tempting audiobook even though unrestricted access would otherwise have been available free**. In other words, many participants placed value on having their future choices constrained. That supports the interpretation of temptation bundling not merely as entertainment but as a commitment device: restricting an attractive option can be useful precisely because unrestricted access makes it too easy to consume the pleasure without doing the exercise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nber.org/roybal/6418-evaluation-temptation-bundling" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nber.org">[National Bureau of Economic Research]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nber.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">6418 evaluation temptation bundling</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Bureau of Economic ResearchAn Evaluation of Temptation Bundling &#124; NBERSeptember 1, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 1, 2012</span></span></span>

## A larger test found smaller but durable gains

The obvious practical objection to the first experiment is that most people do not have someone standing at a gym desk locking away their entertainment. Could people simply learn the rule and apply it themselves?

A 2020 field experiment led by Erika Kirgios tested a more scalable version with **6,792 members of 24 Hour Fitness**. Participants joined a four-week exercise programme. Depending on condition, they received a free audiobook plus encouragement to temptation bundle, received the audiobook without that explanation, or were in a comparison condition without the audiobook and temptation-bundling encouragement. The experiment used recorded gym check-ins and followed behaviour beyond the intervention itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Providing an audiobook **and teaching people to bundle it with exercise** increased the likelihood of making at least one weekly gym visit by roughly **10–14%** and average weekly visits by about **10–12%**, with effects observed during the programme and for as long as 17 weeks afterwards. These gains are less dramatic than the headline result from the original experiment, but they came from a much larger real-world sample and did not require the gym to lock away participants' entertainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

There is an important wrinkle. Explicitly teaching temptation bundling produced only a modest advantage over simply giving gym members a free audiobook. Follow-up experiments suggested why: receiving an audiobook from an exercise programme itself conveyed a fairly obvious hint about its intended use. Participants could infer that they were meant to listen while working out. The authors called this **information leakage** — the intervention partly taught the idea even when it did not explicitly [explain it]({{ 'explain-it/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

That finding makes the evidence more nuanced, not less useful. The later study supports the broader proposition that linking appealing content with exercise can increase attendance, but it does not show that a verbal lesson about “temptation bundling” is always the decisive ingredient. Sometimes the pairing is intuitive enough for people to discover themselves.

It is also worth keeping the intervention in perspective. Participants in the larger study were already gym members who voluntarily enrolled in an exercise-boosting programme, and temptation bundling was embedded alongside other programme features, including workout planning, reminders and small point-based incentives. The researchers could isolate experimental differences between conditions, but the results should not be read as a guarantee that handing any sedentary person an audiobook will produce the same effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/TemptationBundling.pdf?_bhlid=0f6d7e825737d086f8f902008fda30b0ec1b770b" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faculty.wharton.upenn.edu">[Wharton Faculty Platform]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faculty.wharton.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on upenn.edu.</span></span></span>

## How to build a pairing that lasts

The experiments point to a more precise recipe than the loose advice to “make exercise fun”. A durable temptation bundle needs an attraction strong enough to pull you towards the workout and a rule clear enough to stop that attraction escaping from the bundle.

**Choose something you actively anticipate.** The original researchers did not give participants worthy educational lectures; they deliberately tested absorbing popular fiction. The practical test is whether stopping midway creates some desire to come back. A mediocre podcast that you could happily abandon supplies little immediate reward.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/researchers-used-hunger-games-encourage-healthier-choices/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu">[Wharton Knowledge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wharton Knowledge Using &#x27;The Hunger Games&#x27; to Encourage Healthier Choices</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wharton KnowledgeUsing &#x27;The Hunger Games&#x27; to Encourage Healthier Choices - Knowledge at WhartonNovember 19, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 19, 2013</span></span></span>

**Make access conditional on exercise.** “I often listen to this podcast at the gym” is weaker than “this is my walking podcast”. A clean boundary protects the association. The first field experiment's stronger initial result for physically restricted access than for mere encouragement is consistent with the value of making the rule harder to break, although the study does not prove that rigid restriction will always outperform self-imposed rules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2183859" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: papers.ssrn.com">[SSRN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">papers.ssrn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: an Evaluation of Temptation Bundling by Katherine L. Milkman, Julia A. Minson, Kevin Vol...</span></span></span>

**Pair the reward with the workout, not only its completion.** The special advantage of temptation bundling is that the reward can improve the experience while the beneficial behaviour is happening. A thriller during a long walk gives the walk immediate value minute by minute. This is particularly well suited to exercise because many forms of [physical activity]({{ 'physical-capacity/' | relative_url }}) leave enough spare attention for audio entertainment.

**Keep the pairing easy to start.** If listening requires finding a forgotten password, downloading an episode at the gym or carrying special equipment, the bundle introduces friction exactly where it is supposed to increase attraction. A downloaded audiobook, queued podcast or ready playlist makes the reward available as soon as the workout starts.

**Protect the best temptation, but do not over-restrict everything enjoyable.** The method only needs a distinctive reward. Reserving one series, podcast or author for exercise is easier to maintain than trying to ban all entertainment outside workouts. The aim is to create a reliable gateway, not an elaborate system of self-denial.

**Refresh the reward when its pull fades.** The original experiment's declining effect is a warning against assuming that one pairing will remain motivating indefinitely. Finishing a series, becoming bored with a narrator or taking a long break can remove the very anticipation that powers the bundle. Replacing a stale temptation with another genuinely absorbing one preserves the mechanism rather than treating loyalty to the original reward as the goal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

## What the evidence does — and does not — establish

Temptation bundling has an appealing behavioural logic, but its evidence base is narrower than the popularity of the idea might suggest. The strongest direct evidence for exercise centres on a small university-gym experiment and a much larger field experiment involving existing commercial-gym members. Both support the basic strategy, yet their designs, populations and effect sizes differ. The first produced a large early effect that faded; the second produced more modest improvements that could still be detected after the four-week intervention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

Nor can the studies tell us that every pleasurable form of multitasking improves every kind of exercise. Audiobooks are unusually convenient because they can entertain without occupying the hands or, usually, the eyes. A pairing that compromises running awareness, lifting technique or attention during a complex class would defeat the purpose. The useful generalisation is therefore not “add any indulgence to any workout”, but **attach a compatible pleasure to exercise when doing so makes the exercise itself more attractive**.

Most importantly, temptation bundling addresses a specific problem: the workout is worth doing for future reasons but insufficiently appealing at the moment of choice. It does not replace sensible exercise selection, realistic scheduling or recovery, and it cannot rescue an activity that is impractical or persistently painful. What it can do is alter the immediate economics of an ordinary workout. Instead of sacrificing an hour now solely for benefits later, the exerciser gets something desirable during that same hour.

That is why the audiobook experiments fit so neatly into self-improvement built around [immediate rewards]({{ 'immediate-rewards/' | relative_url }}). The intervention did not make cardiovascular fitness arrive faster. It made **going back to exercise valuable before those delayed benefits arrived**. The strongest version of the idea is simple: find something you already look forward to, make exercise the legitimate route to it, and preserve that connection for as long as it continues to make the next workout more appealing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>
