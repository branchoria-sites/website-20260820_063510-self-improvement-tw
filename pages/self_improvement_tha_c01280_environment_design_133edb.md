## Introduction

Environment design reduces everyday temptation by changing what is visible, close at hand, easy to start and selected by default. The aim is not to eliminate choice or to pretend that willpower never matters. It is to arrange ordinary settings so that fewer moments require a difficult act of resistance in the first place. Research on [self-control]({{ 'self-control/' | relative_url }}) describes this as an *antecedent-focused* strategy: intervene before an impulse has gathered strength rather than waiting until temptation is fully active and then trying to suppress it. Field experiments with students have found that modifying tempting situations can improve goal attainment and reduce experienced temptation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736542/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Situational Strategies for Self-Control</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Situational Strategies for Self-Control - PMCJanuary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2016</span></span></span>



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-overview"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_environment_design_133edb-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Design Around Temptation" width="1024" height="572" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Illustrative overview</span></figcaption></figure>

This gives environmental design a distinctive place in [self-improvement that works]({{ 'self-improvement-that-works/' | relative_url }}). Instead of repeatedly asking, “Can I resist this?”, ask, “Why am I being asked to resist this so often?” Moving distracting apps, changing notification settings, rearranging food, adding steps to an impulse purchase or putting a useful alternative within immediate reach can shift behaviour before the internal argument starts.

## Remove tempting defaults

A tempting option does not need to be irresistible to cause problems. It only needs to be repeatedly available at moments when attention is elsewhere, energy is low or the alternative requires slightly more work. Small differences in effort can accumulate because they affect dozens or hundreds of ordinary choices.

This is the basic mechanism behind *choice architecture*: changing the structure of a decision without necessarily banning alternatives or substantially changing their price. A major meta-analysis covering more than 200 studies found that choice-architecture interventions changed behaviour on average, although results varied substantially by technique and domain. Interventions that altered the structure of choices — including defaults and option-related effort — tended to outperform interventions that merely supplied information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8740589/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)The effectiveness of nudging: A meta-analysis of choice architecture interventions across behavioral domains - PMCJan...</span></span></span>

For personal use, the relevant lesson is not that every “nudge” works. It is that **friction and defaults are behavioural variables you can deliberately edit**. If opening a social-media feed requires one tap from the home screen while reading the document you care about requires finding a folder, locating the file and settling back into it, your environment has quietly made distraction the easier action.

Removing a tempting default can therefore mean:

* taking entertainment apps off the first home screen or signing out of the versions that cause habitual checking;
* turning non-essential [notifications]({{ 'notifications/' | relative_url }}) off rather than deciding whether to ignore each alert;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* keeping tempting food somewhere that requires a deliberate retrieval rather than within sight and reach;
* unsubscribing from promotional retailer alerts and emails that repeatedly manufacture purchase occasions;
* removing saved payment details or one-click purchasing when instant checkout is part of an impulsive-spending pattern.

</div>

The important feature is not inconvenience for its own sake. It is **selective friction**: make an unwanted behaviour just difficult enough to interrupt automatic continuation while leaving genuinely useful access available.

Digital commerce demonstrates how powerful removing friction can be. Research using quasi-experimental data from an online retailer found that introducing one-click buying led customers to purchase more frequently and buy more items, with effects persisting after adoption. The retailer's objective was naturally to make purchasing easier; a consumer trying to curb spontaneous buying can apply the same mechanism in reverse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2023.4716" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubsonline.informs.org">[PubsOnLine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubsonline.informs.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pubs On Line Fewer Clicks, More Purchases &#124; Management Science</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fewer Clicks, More Purchases &#124; Management ScienceFebruary 22, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2023</span></span></span>

Defaults matter too. A 2024 UK Department for Business and Trade study examined 558 online consumer journeys and then ran an experiment with 5,889 participants. Preselected defaults substantially shifted choices: participants were about 60–70% more likely to choose a more expensive option when it had been preselected. That is a useful reminder that seemingly minor interface settings can shape spending without anyone explicitly deciding that the expensive option is preferable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-prevalence-and-potential-harm-of-defaults-in-online-shopping" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The prevalence and potential harm of defaults in online shopping</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The prevalence and potential harm of defaults in online shopping</span></span></span>

## Make the better option easier

Environmental design works best when it does more than obstruct temptation. Removing an easy bad option while leaving the intended behaviour cumbersome can simply create annoyance. A stronger design simultaneously **raises friction on the behaviour you want less of and lowers friction on the behaviour you want more of**.

Suppose someone wants to read after dinner instead of scrolling. Deleting every distracting app might help, but the stronger arrangement also places the book on the chair where they normally sit, with the bookmark ready. Someone who wants to [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) before work might put training clothes beside the bed and pack the gym bag the previous evening. Someone who wants to cook rather than order a takeaway can keep ingredients for two low-effort meals ready rather than relying on the aspiration to prepare something elaborate at 8 pm.

This fits evidence from strategic self-control research. In two field experiments involving students, those assigned to modify their situations — for example by reducing access to distractions — were more successful in meeting academic goals than comparison groups relying on downstream resistance. In the college experiment, lower feelings of temptation partly explained the advantage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000062" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A stitch in time: Strategic self-control in high school and college students</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A stitch in time: Strategic self-control in high school and college students...</span></span></span>

The mechanism is easy to miss precisely because successful environmental design can feel uneventful. If the phone is in another room, you do not heroically resist it every four minutes; you may simply think about it less often. Researchers studying situational self-control have argued that such strategies are often underappreciated because people associate “self-control” with visibly fighting an impulse rather than arranging circumstances so that the fight rarely begins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691615623247/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psychologicalscience.org">[Association for Psychological Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psychologicalscience.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Association for Psychological Science Situational Strategies for Self-Control</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Association for Psychological ScienceSituational Strategies for Self-Control - Perspectives on Psychological Science - APSJanuary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2016</span></span></span>

Convenience can also be attached to desirable behaviour through *temptation bundling*. In a field experiment, participants given access to compelling audiobooks only while at the gym initially visited the gym 51% more often than controls; participants merely encouraged to impose the same rule on themselves initially visited 29% more often. The effects weakened over time, particularly after a disruption around Thanksgiving, so this is not evidence for a permanent behavioural cure. It does show how access rules can restructure the immediate attractiveness of an activity rather than relying entirely on delayed benefits such as future fitness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## Devices: stop inviting distraction

Phones are unusually important environments because their cues travel with the user. A notification can turn “I will check later” into an immediate choice between the current task and a newly salient alternative. Environmental design therefore starts upstream of screen-time limits: reduce the number of invitations.

A randomised field experiment with 237 people compared normal smartphone notifications, notifications delivered in three daily batches and no notifications. People receiving batched notifications reported feeling more attentive, productive, in a better mood and more in control of their phones than the normal-notification group. Eliminating notifications entirely did not produce all the same benefits and increased anxiety and fear of missing out for some participants, illustrating an important point: more restriction is not automatically better.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span></span></span>

Visual design can matter as well. In a preregistered field experiment involving 112 participants, switching smartphones to greyscale — a modest form of “design friction” that makes visually stimulating content less vivid — produced an immediate reduction in objectively measured screen time compared with self-monitoring alone. Goal-setting also reduced use, but more gradually. The study did **not** find immediate causal improvements in wellbeing or academic performance, so reducing screen time should not be treated as an automatic route to a better life; the useful finding here is narrower: changing the device's presentation changed usage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36577008/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

For everyday design, that suggests a hierarchy: first remove unnecessary cues, then increase the number of steps required to enter habitual distractions, while keeping genuinely useful functions easy. Turning off shopping and social notifications, moving high-distraction apps away from the home screen, logging out of websites used reflexively, or using scheduled blocking during focused work all alter the environment before “just one quick check” becomes a live decision.

## Food: use distance, visibility and availability carefully

Food environments provide some of the clearest illustrations of the principle because eating opportunities are heavily shaped by what is present and convenient. But this is also an area where popular claims often outrun the quality of the evidence.

A Cochrane review of 24 randomised studies found that reducing the availability of targeted food options and moving food farther away could reduce selection or consumption. In pooled analysis, greater distance was associated with lower consumption across 12 studies involving 1,098 participants. However, Cochrane rated the evidence as **low certainty**, with many studies conducted in laboratories and substantial concerns about risk of bias. The appropriate conclusion is therefore modest: position and availability probably matter, but exact effect sizes from small experiments should not be treated as universal laws.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD012573_altering-availability-or-proximity-food-alcohol-and-tobacco-products-change-their-selection-and" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cochrane.org">[Cochrane]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cochrane.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cochrane.org.</span></span></span>

Real-world cafeteria studies offer useful, if not always perfectly controlled, examples. At a large hospital cafeteria serving thousands of transactions per day, researchers combined traffic-light nutritional labels with changes that made healthier foods more visible and convenient, then observed changes over 24 months. The sustained shifts in purchasing showed that environmental rearrangement can operate in a setting where people repeatedly make ordinary food choices, rather than only in one-off laboratory tests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24439347/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

A systematic review of workplace and healthcare food environments similarly concluded that proximity and availability interventions were among the more promising choice-architecture approaches, whereas labelling by itself was generally less reliable at changing purchasing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30674487/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

At home, this points towards straightforward design rather than complicated dietary rules. Foods you want to eat routinely can occupy the convenient positions: washed fruit at eye level, prepared ingredients near the front of the fridge, water where it is easy to reach. Foods that reliably trigger unplanned eating do not have to be prohibited; they can simply stop occupying the most visible, immediately accessible positions. The intervention is the layout, not a nightly argument about restraint.

## Spending: restore a moment of resistance

Modern purchasing systems are deliberately designed to compress the distance between wanting and buying. Stored cards, one-click checkout, retailer notifications and payment options displayed directly beside a product can turn a passing desire into a completed transaction with almost no interruption.

Research broadly supports the idea that payment convenience affects behaviour. A 2024 meta-analysis synthesising 71 papers found that consumers generally spent more when using cashless methods than cash, although the magnitude varied by context and has weakened over time as cashless payment has become more familiar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022435924000216" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Less cash, more splash? A meta-analysis on the cashless effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirect Less cash, more splash? A meta-analysis on the cashless effect</span></span></span> A 2026 set of field and online experiments also found that making payment methods more visually salient on product pages increased purchase likelihood, apparently by encouraging people to imagine completing the payment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022435926000059" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

These findings do not prove that deleting a saved bank card will reduce spending by a specific percentage. They do support the broader environmental mechanism: frictionless payment helps purchasing, so restoring a little friction can create a useful decision boundary.

Practical versions include removing shopping apps that prompt browsing, disabling retailer push notifications, deleting stored card details from sites associated with impulse purchases, avoiding “buy now” shortcuts, or placing non-essential items on a written wish list before checkout. The objective is not to make necessary purchases painfully inefficient. It is to distinguish deliberate spending from transactions that happen largely because the path from desire to payment has been engineered to be nearly effortless.

For stronger self-control problems, commitment devices go further by restricting future access rather than merely slowing it. In a well-known randomised study in the Philippines, customers offered a savings account that restricted withdrawals until a chosen date or savings target accumulated substantially larger balances over the following year than controls. But commitment mechanisms carry a cost: later research shows that people can choose restrictions that turn out to be poorly matched to their actual needs. Strong barriers therefore make most sense when the goal is stable and the person genuinely wants less future flexibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isps.yale.edu/resource/tying-odysseus-to-the-mast-evidence-from-a-commitment-savings-product-in-the-philippines" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yale.edu">[isps.yale.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tying Odysseus to the Mast: Evidence from a Commitment Savings Product in the Philippines &#124; Institution for Social and Policy Studies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tying Odysseus to the Mast: Evidence from a Commitment Savings Product in the Philippines &#124; Institution for Social and Policy Studies</span></span></span>

## Design for repeated moments, not perfect control

The strongest use of environmental design is usually not a dramatic lockdown. It is the quiet correction of a repeated imbalance. If a temptation appears once a month, rebuilding your environment around it may be unnecessary. If the same distracting, costly or unwanted option appears twenty times a day, changing the setting can eliminate hundreds of future acts of resistance.

A useful way to diagnose the problem is to observe where behaviour repeatedly diverges from intention. What appears immediately before the unwanted action? Is the cue visual, digital or physical? Is the tempting behaviour easier than the intended alternative? Is it already selected by default? Can the cue be removed, access delayed or the preferred action made ready in advance?

The intervention should remain proportional. Research on choice architecture increasingly stresses that effects vary sharply between contexts, and a 2025 review in *Nature Reviews Psychology* concluded that [average effects]({{ 'average-effects/' | relative_url }}) are smaller and less reliably generalisable than early enthusiasm for nudging sometimes implied. An intervention that works in one setting may do little — or occasionally backfire — in another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-025-00471-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nature.com.</span></span></span> That argues for treating personal environmental design as an experiment: change one recurring feature, observe whether behaviour actually shifts, and keep the change only if it helps.

The deeper principle is that self-improvement does not have to be measured by how often you defeat temptation in a fair fight. Often the more effective strategy is to stop arranging fair fights. Put the useful behaviour closer, make the distracting behaviour less automatic, remove needless cues and let the environment carry part of the self-control burden.

    });

      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }

  }

      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;

    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');

    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();

        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
