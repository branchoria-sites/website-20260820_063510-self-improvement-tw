## Introduction

A routine performed only twice a week can take much longer to become automatic than one performed every day for a simple reason: **habit learning happens through repetitions, not merely through the passage of calendar time**. Each performance in a recurring context gives the brain another opportunity to strengthen the association between a cue and an action. A twice-weekly routine supplies roughly eight such opportunities in a month; a daily routine supplies about 30. Research consistently links greater behavioural frequency with stronger [automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }}), although frequency is not the only factor that matters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6667662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Habits, Quick and Easy: Perceived Complexity Moderates the Associations of Contextual Stability and Rewards With Beha...</span></span></span>

That distinction is especially important for complex self-improvement routines such as gym sessions, [meal preparation]({{ 'meal-prep/' | relative_url }}) or structured study. The familiar habit-formation estimates measured in days mostly come from studies of behaviours attempted **daily**. They should not simply be transferred to routines performed two or three times a week. For low-frequency behaviours, counting meaningful cue-action repetitions is often more informative than counting days on the calendar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

## Habit learning depends on repeated cue-action pairings

A habit is more than something a person does regularly. In contemporary habit research, the crucial feature is automaticity: encountering a familiar context begins to activate a learnt response with decreasing need for conscious deliberation. That relationship develops through repeated experience in which the relevant cue and behaviour occur together. Reviews of habit formation therefore describe the core process as repetition in a sufficiently consistent context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17437199.2011.603640" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlinePromoting habit formation: Health Psychology Review: Vol 7, No sup1 - Get AccessOctober 11, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 11, 2011</span></span></span>

Experimental evidence supports the underlying learning mechanism. In two experiments involving a sequential sushi-making task, researchers found that greater practice produced stronger cue-response associations in memory. Those associations, in turn, predicted better performance when participants later carried out the task without instructions while under cognitive load. The researchers concluded that repetition helps recurring contextual cues automatically activate the responses practised in their presence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38047612/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Measuring context-response associations that drive habits</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Measuring context-response associations that drive habits - PubMedDecember 4, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 4, 2023</span></span></span>

This makes frequency important in a way that a calendar cannot capture. Suppose someone begins two new routines on 1 January. They take a vitamin after breakfast every day but go to the gym every Tuesday and Saturday. After six weeks, the vitamin behaviour has had as many as 42 opportunities to become associated with its cue; the gym routine has had about 12. Both routines are “six weeks old”, but their learning histories are radically different.

The comparison is simplified: repetitions are not interchangeable units, and habit strength does not rise by a fixed amount every time. Context stability, reward, behavioural difficulty and individual differences all matter. Even so, a study covering 25 behaviours in 459 participants found that frequency and contextual stability were each independently positively associated with automaticity, while perceived complexity was negatively associated with it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6667662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Habits, Quick and Easy: Perceived Complexity Moderates the Associations of Contextual Stability and Rewards With Beha...</span></span></span>

For complex low-frequency routines, the disadvantage can therefore be compounded. There are fewer occasions on which to learn the routine, and each occasion may involve more decisions or sub-actions than a simple behaviour does.

## Calendar time can hide the real amount of practice

The best-known real-world habit study illustrates why “days to form a habit” can be misleading when applied to infrequent routines. Phillippa Lally and colleagues asked 96 participants to choose an eating, drinking or activity behaviour and perform it **daily in the same context** for 12 weeks. Among participants whose automaticity trajectories could be modelled, the estimated time to reach 95% of their eventual automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days. Automaticity followed a curve: gains were initially larger and then diminished as participants approached a plateau.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

Those numbers are frequently treated as general-purpose habit deadlines, but the study design matters. Participants had a daily opportunity for cue-behaviour learning. A routine scheduled twice weekly cannot accumulate comparable numbers of repetitions at the same speed.

The arithmetic shows the scale of the difference. At perfect adherence, 30 repetitions require about one month for a daily behaviour, around ten weeks for a three-times-weekly routine, and roughly fifteen weeks for a twice-weekly routine. Sixty repetitions would require about two months, twenty weeks and thirty weeks respectively. These are **illustrations of exposure counts, not scientifically established thresholds at which habits suddenly form**. Research does not support a universal number of repetitions that guarantees automaticity.

Indeed, researchers have specifically warned that habit-formation studies often analyse change against elapsed time even though repetition is the proposed learning mechanism. A 2022 methodological review noted that the real-world formation studies it examined analysed habit development over time rather than directly over repetitions. The authors also highlighted a case in which participants sometimes performed a behaviour more than once per day, demonstrating why days themselves can be an imperfect proxy for learning opportunities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23311908.2022.2041277" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on tandfonline.com.</span></span></span>

This distinction also explains why an occasional missed performance is different from a routinely sparse schedule. In Lally's daily study, missing one opportunity did not materially derail the formation process; automaticity continued developing afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span> A twice-weekly schedule, by contrast, is not a daily schedule with occasional lapses. Long gaps are built into it. Missing one Tuesday gym session could mean going a full week between two performances, sharply reducing the number of cue-action pairings accumulated that month.

## What six-month habit data suggest about frequency

Newer longitudinal evidence gives frequency a particularly important role. A 2026 preregistered reanalysis by Sebastian Bürgler and colleagues followed 254 people for six months, measuring behavioural automaticity monthly alongside ten possible determinants of habit development. The researchers identified trajectories of habit formation and habit degradation, and membership of those trajectories differed according to behavioural complexity, frequency and context stability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42053513/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What Makes a Habit? Investigating Potential Determinants of Habit Formation - PubMed...</span></span></span>

In the multilevel analyses, behavioural frequency was positively associated with habit strength, as were context stability, autonomous motivation and positive feelings such as feeling pleased and energised; perceived difficulty was negatively associated with habit strength. Crucially for low-frequency routines, **frequency was the determinant that showed a statistically significant interaction with time after correction for multiple testing**. The finding strengthens the case that how often a behaviour is actually performed matters to how its automaticity changes across months.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/01461672261440446" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsWhat Makes a Habit? Investigating Potential Determinants of Habit Formation - Sebastian Bürgler, Marina Milyavskaya, Gail Mc...</span></span></span>

It does not establish a formula such as “twice weekly takes three times as many months as six times weekly”. The study was observational and reanalysed data collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, when people's environments and routines were unusually disrupted. Nor does frequency act independently of everything else. What the evidence supports is the more modest and useful conclusion that habit development reflects accumulated behavioural experience, and people who perform an intended routine infrequently accumulate that experience more slowly.

[Exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) research makes the same issue concrete. A longitudinal study of 111 new gym members over 12 weeks reported that exercising at least four times per week for six weeks was the minimum pattern associated with establishing an exercise habit in that sample. [Consistency]({{ 'consistency/' | relative_url }}), lower behavioural complexity, environmental factors and positive affective judgements also predicted changes in habit formation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Exercise habit formation in new gym members: a longitudinal study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Exercise habit formation in new gym members: a longitudinal study</span></span></span>

That “four bouts” result should not be converted into a universal prescription. Another 13-week longitudinal study following 226 university sports and gym participants found a more complicated picture: cumulative prior class attendance did **not** significantly predict subsequent gains in automaticity after preceding automaticity was included in the model, although frequency was associated with subsequent automaticity in a simpler model. Positive average feelings about the exercise were also associated with greater automaticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33192892/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med(How) Does Affect Influence the Formation of Habits in Exercise?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med(How) Does Affect Influence the Formation of Habits in Exercise?</span></span></span> The contrast is useful because it prevents an overly mechanical conclusion that repetition alone creates a habit.

## Why twice-a-week habits may take much longer

For a low-frequency routine, three effects can converge. First, fewer scheduled performances mean fewer chances to reinforce the cue-action association. Second, missing a single scheduled performance removes a much larger proportion of the month's practice than missing one performance of a daily behaviour. Third, complex routines may contain several decisions and actions, so repeated exposure may be needed not only to automate starting but also to make the sequence itself easier to execute.

Context can either soften or intensify this problem. Research manipulating the stability of where and when new behaviours were performed found greater automaticity when repetition occurred in more stable contexts. A separate study of vitamin-taking found that greater context stability was associated with more frequent repetition and with larger gains in habit strength per repetition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9226889/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span> In practical terms, “strength training every Tuesday and Saturday immediately after leaving work” supplies a more repeatable learning situation than “exercise twice sometime this week”, even though both plans have the same nominal weekly frequency.

There is therefore an important distinction between **low frequency and inconsistent frequency**. Some worthwhile behaviours genuinely should not be performed every day. A twice-weekly routine can still become habitual if its cue and setting recur reliably. The relevant lesson from habit science is not to inflate frequency merely to make the behaviour habitual, but to recognise that fewer repetitions normally imply a longer calendar runway for learning.

This also changes how progress should be judged. After eight weeks, someone completing a twice-weekly routine may have performed it only 16 times. If it still requires deliberate planning, that does not contradict research showing that some daily behaviours become substantially more automatic over a similar number of weeks: the two schedules have supplied very different amounts of practice. Daily nutrition interventions, for example, have found repeated enactment of the planned behaviour to be a key predictor of increasing automaticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33405284/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## The better question is how many consistent repetitions have occurred

For self-improvement routines that happen only a few times each week, “How many days have I been doing this?” can give a false impression of how much habit learning has actually taken place. A more informative set of questions is whether the planned behaviour has been repeatedly completed, whether substantially the same cue precedes it, and whether initiating it is becoming less dependent on conscious reminders.

The evidence does not justify replacing the popular “66 days” claim with a new universal target such as 30, 50 or 100 repetitions. People, behaviours and contexts vary too much, and automaticity develops gradually rather than appearing at a fixed threshold. The classic daily study found enormous individual variation, while newer longitudinal work identifies frequency alongside context stability, complexity, difficulty, motivation and affect as relevant determinants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

What can be said with considerably greater confidence is that **calendar time and learning opportunity are not the same thing**. A twice-weekly complex routine may need many months to accumulate the cue-action repetitions that a daily behaviour receives in a few weeks. For low-frequency habits, a long formation period is therefore not surprising. It is a predictable consequence of learning from fewer repetitions—and a reason to measure progress by consistent practice and growing automaticity rather than by an arbitrary countdown of days.
