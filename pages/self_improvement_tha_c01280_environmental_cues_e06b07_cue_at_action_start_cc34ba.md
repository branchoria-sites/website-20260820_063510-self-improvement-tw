## Introduction

The best place for a behaviour cue is usually the point where the desired action can begin. Put the book on the chair where you will read it, the walking shoes beside the door you will leave through, the instrument at the place where you can pick it up and play, or the planning sheet on the desk where tomorrow’s plan will be made. The aim is not merely to remember the behaviour earlier in the day; it is to encounter the cue when the next useful response is immediately available.

Research does not establish a universal rule that every cue must sit literally beside the first object used. But several evidence streams converge on the same practical principle. Point-of-decision prompts work when people meet them at a behavioural choice, [prospective-memory]({{ 'remembering/' | relative_url }}) research shows advantages when cues are closely tied to the activity in progress, and habit research finds that repeating actions in stable contexts strengthens [automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20117614/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Point-of-decision prompts to increase stair use. A systematic review update - PubMed...</span></span></span>

For self-improvement, that means thinking less about where a reminder is easiest to display and more about **where seeing it can turn directly into doing**.

## Why the action-start location matters

A reminder can succeed at attracting attention while still fail at producing behaviour. Imagine seeing a note saying “walk tonight” on the bathroom mirror in the morning. The intention has been recalled, but walking cannot conveniently begin. Between the reminder and the walk lie hours of other activity, another transition, and another requirement to remember what was intended.

A cue at the front door works differently. If the shoes, coat or walking prompt is encountered as you reach the point where leaving is possible, remembering and beginning are brought together. This is the distinction that matters: a **memory cue** can make an intention come to mind, whereas an **action-start cue** is encountered when the response can actually be initiated.

Prospective-memory research helps explain why reducing this separation is useful. Prospective memory is the ability to remember to carry out an intended action later. Doing that involves maintaining an intention, detecting the relevant opportunity and initiating the response. Research shows that monitoring for future-action cues can itself consume attention, while cues that fit what a person is already attending to can support less effortful retrieval. In experiments, strategic monitoring has been particularly effective when ongoing activity naturally directs attention towards the relevant contextual information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6070398/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Context Cue Focality Influences Strategic Prospective Memory Monitoring - PMC...</span></span></span>

This does not prove that a book on an armchair will outperform a book elsewhere by a particular percentage. Laboratory prospective-memory tasks are not identical to home habit design. They do, however, support a useful design principle: **make the intended action easier to retrieve from the situation in which it belongs rather than requiring continuous mental monitoring for it**.

Implementation-intention research reaches a related conclusion from another direction. An implementation intention specifies the situation in which a response should occur — effectively linking an “if” cue to a “then” action. A major meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found a medium-to-large overall effect on goal attainment, and later work indicated that the mechanism involves both increased accessibility of the specified cue and a stronger cue-response association. A much larger 2024 review, covering 642 independent tests, continued to find beneficial effects across cognitive, emotional and behavioural outcomes and found larger effects for contingent if-then plans.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0065260106380021" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta‐analysis of Effects and Processes - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Physical cue placement can be understood as an environmental version of the same logic. Instead of repeatedly thinking, “When I reach the desk, I should practise”, the practice materials waiting on that desk can help make the situation itself carry part of the instruction.

## The clearest real-world model: prompt at the decision

Stair-use research provides an unusually concrete demonstration because researchers can place a prompt at a particular location and then observe what people actually do.

Point-of-decision prompts are signs positioned near stairs, lifts or escalators where people choose how to move between floors. A Community Guide systematic-review update covering 16 qualifying peer-reviewed studies concluded that there was strong evidence that such prompts increased stair use. The intervention is explicitly defined by its placement at or near the behavioural decision: people encounter the message when taking the stairs is an available response.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20117614/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Point-of-decision prompts to increase stair use. A systematic review update - PubMed...</span></span></span>

That timing is important. A poster about [physical activity]({{ 'physical-capacity/' | relative_url }}) in a distant corridor might improve awareness, but it leaves the reader to remember the message at the later choice. A sign beside the lift can convert the same broad intention — “I ought to move more” — into a present opportunity: **the stairs are here; I can take them now**. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention describes these signs as reminders about an immediate opportunity to be physically active.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity/php/strategies/encouraging-physical-activity.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">encouraging physical activity</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Strategies for Prompts to Encourage Physical Activity &#124; Physical Activity &#124; CDCJanuary 27, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 27, 2025</span></span></span>

Message specificity can sharpen that effect. In a university worksite study, the specific-message condition produced about twice the odds of stair use compared with baseline (OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.46–2.84), and significantly greater odds than a general-message condition (OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.13–2.20). Both signs were presented in the decision-point area near the stairs and lift; only the specific message significantly increased stair use over baseline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24670074/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The results indicate that a specific message may be more effective at promoting stair use.Read more...</span></span></span>

There are limits to what this evidence can establish. Stair studies compare prompts with no prompts or different messages much more often than they experimentally compare the *same* cue at several distances from the action. Indeed, the Community Guide review identified the possible “critical distance” between stairs and lifts as a question still requiring research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/168569/cdc_168569_DS4.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stacks.cdc.gov">[CDC Stacks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stacks.cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stacks Point-of-Decision Prompts to Increase Stair Use</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stacks Point-of-Decision Prompts to Increase Stair Use</span></span></span> It would therefore be too strong to claim that behavioural science has identified an exact optimal number of centimetres or seconds between cue and action.

The better-supported conclusion is narrower: prompts can influence behaviour when they are encountered at the relevant choice, and this provides a strong model for personal cue design.

## What this looks like in everyday self-improvement

The practical test is simple: **after encountering the cue, what is the first physical action required, and can it happen here?** A well-placed cue makes the answer obvious.

* **Reading:** Instead of putting “read more” on a general to-do list, leave the chosen book at the place where reading normally starts — for example, on the armchair, bedside table or commuting bag. An open book or visible bookmark can make the next response even more specific: sit down and read the next page.
* **Walking:** Put walking shoes, coat or another distinctive walking cue at the exit you actually use. The important location is not simply “somewhere visible in the house”; it is the transition point at which putting on the shoes and leaving can follow immediately. This mirrors point-of-decision prompting: the cue arrives as the behavioural choice becomes available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20117614/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Point-of-decision prompts to increase stair use. A systematic review update - PubMed...</span></span></span>
* **Practice:** Place the instrument, sketchbook, language cards or other practice materials at the intended practice position, prepared sufficiently that the first repetition can start. A guitar stored safely in its case may still need protection, for example, but putting the case beside the practice chair is more action-linked than relying on a reminder displayed elsewhere.
* **Planning:** If the desired behaviour is planning tomorrow, put the notebook, planner or planning card where that process will actually happen. A notebook beside the keyboard at the end of the working day creates a more direct cue-response relationship than a planning reminder encountered during breakfast if no planning will occur until evening.

These examples deliberately focus on the **beginning**, not on redesigning the whole activity. A walking cue does not need to solve the entire [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) routine. Its job is to make the first relevant response — putting on the shoes or going through the door — more likely to occur.

## Reduce the gap between seeing and starting

A useful way to audit cue placement is to count the intervening steps. Suppose you see a reminder to practise a language while sitting on the sofa. To act, you must finish what you are doing, remember the intention, get up, go to another room, find the materials and begin. Each transition creates another opportunity for attention to move elsewhere.

Now move the cue to the desk where the language materials are already waiting. Seeing it can be followed by opening the exercise and answering the first question. The intended behaviour has not become intrinsically easier, but the **cue-to-start gap** has shrunk.

This principle should not be exaggerated into a claim that human behaviour is mechanically controlled by object placement. Motivation, competing activities, physical effort and the attractiveness of alternatives still matter. Point-of-decision stair prompts generally produce modest rather than transformative behavioural changes, and reviews have found substantial variation by setting. One systematic review, for example, found significant increases in 28 of 32 results involving escalators but only three of ten involving lifts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/1403494810375865" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sagepub.com.</span></span></span> A cue changes the probability of starting; it does not guarantee the response.

There is also evidence that prompts can lose effectiveness with repeated exposure. An eight-week field experiment in Paris Métro stations found immediate increases in stair use but no lasting post-intervention effect; the researchers suggested that people may eventually habituate to the prompts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0277953617305403" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span> That is a reason to favour cues that are functionally embedded in the action — the shoes you must pick up, the notebook occupying the working space — rather than assuming that another permanent motivational poster will remain noticeable indefinitely.

A practical cue-placement sequence is therefore:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Define the first observable action.** “Read more” becomes “open the book and read the next page”; “exercise” becomes “put on walking shoes and leave”.
2. **Identify the location where that action can occur.** Not where you think about the goal, but where the behaviour becomes physically available.
3. **Put the cue there or immediately before it.** The book goes at the reading place; the shoes at the exit; the practice material at the practice station.
4. **Remove unnecessary intermediate steps.** If seeing the cue still requires searching, fetching, setting up or remembering again much later, move it closer to the start.
5. **Keep the context reasonably stable.** Repeatedly encountering the same cue in the same action setting gives the situation a chance to acquire a stronger association with the behaviour.

</div>

## Repetition can turn the location itself into a cue

Placement is especially useful when it is consistent. In the influential everyday habit-formation study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues, 96 volunteers selected an eating, drinking or activity behaviour and attempted it daily in the same context for 12 weeks. Automaticity generally increased with repetition in that consistent context, although the rate varied greatly between individuals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wiley.com.</span></span></span>

Later longitudinal research tested context stability more directly. In one study, 95 university students building study habits over six weeks were assigned either to keep the performance context stable or deliberately vary rooms and times. A second dataset examined 308 habits reported by 218 app users. Across both datasets, greater context stability predicted greater automaticity and better attainment of repetition goals. The authors caution that their samples and habit types limit generalisation, but the convergence of the two studies supports deliberately specifying a stable time and place when building a habit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.883795/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on frontiersin.org.</span></span></span>

That gives cue placement a longer-term purpose. Initially, the conspicuous book, shoes or practice materials may function as an explicit reminder. With repeated behaviour in the same setting, more of the surrounding context can become associated with the response. The reading chair, arriving home, sitting at the practice desk or reaching the end of the working day may increasingly participate in triggering what comes next.

This is why “put reminders everywhere” is a weaker rule than **put the cue where the behaviour starts, then keep using that relationship**. A scattered reminder asks for attention. A cue anchored to the action-start location can do something more useful: identify the opportunity, specify the next response and shorten the distance between intention and behaviour.
