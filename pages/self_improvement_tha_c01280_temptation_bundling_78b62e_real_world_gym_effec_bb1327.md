## Introduction

The strongest real-world test of temptation bundling suggests that it helps [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}), but by much less than the dramatic headline from the original university experiment. In a field experiment involving 6,792 members of the commercial chain 24 Hour Fitness, researchers tested a lightweight version: participants could receive a free audiobook and, in one condition, were explicitly encouraged to save tempting entertainment for workouts. The intervention raised measures of gym attendance by roughly 10–14%, with effects detectable for as long as 17 weeks after the four-week programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

That is important for self-improvement because the trial traded experimental control for realism. Nobody locked participants' entertainment inside a gym. People had ordinary memberships, could access their audiobook elsewhere and had to enforce the rule themselves. The resulting gains were smaller than those in the pioneering university study, but they survived in a setting much closer to everyday life. The evidence therefore points towards temptation bundling as a modest behavioural aid rather than a transformation: useful precisely because a relatively light intervention can shift repeated behaviour at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## The commercial-gym test

Erika Kirgios and colleagues embedded their temptation-bundling experiment in StepUp, an exercise-promoting programme run with 24 Hour Fitness. Participants were existing adult gym members rather than university students recruited into an unusually controlled study. The researchers could measure behaviour objectively through gym check-ins and had as much as 52 weeks of pre-intervention attendance data for participants, giving them a substantial baseline against which to assess changes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346246007_Teaching_temptation_bundling_to_boost_exercise_A_field_experiment" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The crucial comparison involved three approaches. A control group participated without receiving an audiobook. A second group received a code for a free audiobook but no explicit lesson in temptation bundling. A third received the audiobook and was encouraged to use entertainment strategically with exercise — including reserving enjoyable media for workouts. This was a deliberately low-touch intervention: unlike the original experiment, the researchers did not physically prevent participants from listening elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

That distinction makes the experiment unusually informative. A behavioural technique can work impressively when researchers create the perfect conditions for it yet prove impractical when people must administer it themselves. Here, participants had to remember the rule, resist breaking it and decide when and how to combine entertainment with exercise. The study was therefore testing not merely whether an enforced bundle can alter behaviour, but whether the basic idea can survive contact with ordinary consumer choice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346246007_Teaching_temptation_bundling_to_boost_exercise_A_field_experiment" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The behavioural outcomes were actual gym visits rather than participants' intentions or recollections. Across the study, pairing an audiobook with encouragement to [temptation bundle]({{ 'routine-breaks/' | relative_url }}) increased the probability of making at least one weekly gym visit by about 10–14% and average weekly workouts by about 10–12%, with benefits observed during the programme and in follow-up analyses extending as far as 17 weeks afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

There is an important qualification hidden behind those averages. Only about 14% of participants in the explicit temptation-bundling condition actually downloaded their free audiobook, almost identical to the 13% download rate among those simply offered one. Meanwhile, 78.6% of participants in the explicit condition signed a pledge to temptation bundle, and fewer than a third said audiobooks were their preferred entertainment for pairing with exercise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346246007_Teaching_temptation_bundling_to_boost_exercise_A_field_experiment" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

This means the experiment should not be read as showing that thousands of people faithfully listened to novels only on treadmills and consequently exercised more. The treatment was an offer, an idea and a set of prompts delivered at scale, with substantial variation in how people engaged with it. That messiness is a limitation if the question is the pure physiological effect of listening to an audiobook while exercising. It is a strength if the question is what happens when a behavioural strategy is offered to real people who remain free to ignore, adapt or imperfectly follow it.

## Why the gains were smaller than the university result

The contrast with the original temptation-bundling experiment is striking. Katherine Milkman, Julia Minson and Kevin Volpp's earlier university study randomly assigned 226 participants, of whom 215 attended the intake session. In its strongest condition, participants selected tempting audio novels that were loaded onto an iPod stored in a monitored locker at the university gym. If they wanted the next chapter, they had to return.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

At the beginning of that experiment, the researchers estimated that the enforced-bundle group made 0.48 additional gym visits per week relative to a regression-estimated 0.94 visits in the control condition — a 51% increase. Participants merely encouraged to impose the restriction themselves initially showed a smaller 29% increase. The effects then weakened as the weeks passed, with a particularly conspicuous disruption around the Thanksgiving break.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

The later commercial trial produced no comparable 51% surge. Instead, its roughly 10–14% improvements are more plausible as an estimate of what a scalable, lightly administered programme might achieve. The two experiments did not test identical interventions or populations, so their percentages should not be treated as a clean replication in which one simply supersedes the other. But the difference is informative: enforcing access to the temptation can produce a powerful initial incentive, whereas asking people to create and maintain their own rule yields a smaller behavioural change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

There is another difference that matters just as much: durability. The original study's strongest treatment weakened by about 0.07 gym visits per week as its nine-week intervention progressed, and the Thanksgiving interruption substantially eroded the advantage. By contrast, the commercial-gym study found detectable treatment effects in follow-up analyses extending 17 weeks beyond its four-week intervention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

The large trial therefore changes what the evidence means. The most eye-catching number associated with temptation bundling — a 51% initial increase — came from a small study with unusually strong enforcement. The more scalable version produced a smaller lift but evidence of persistence over months. For someone interested in [self-improvement that works]({{ 'self-improvement-that-works/' | relative_url }}) outside a laboratory-like arrangement, the second pattern is arguably more useful.

## The audiobook itself carried part of the message

One of the most revealing findings was that explicit instruction was not doing all the work. Simply giving members a free audiobook within an exercise programme also increased gym attendance on some measures. During the intervention, the audiobook-only group made an estimated 0.23 additional gym visits per week relative to control, a 17.6% increase on that measure. The researchers also reported benefits after the intervention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346246007_Teaching_temptation_bundling_to_boost_exercise_A_field_experiment" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

Why would an audiobook help when nobody had explicitly told recipients to reserve it for exercise? Follow-up experiments supported what the researchers called an information-leakage explanation. When an exercise programme gives someone an audiobook, the context itself communicates a possible use: *perhaps this is something to enjoy while exercising*. Participants were more likely to infer that intended pairing when the audiobook came from an exercise programme than when the same kind of gift appeared in an unrelated context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/TemptationBundling.pdf?_bhlid=0f6d7e825737d086f8f902008fda30b0ec1b770b" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faculty.wharton.upenn.edu">[Wharton Faculty Platform]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faculty.wharton.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on upenn.edu.</span></span></span>

That helps explain an initially puzzling result. Explicit encouragement to temptation bundle produced only a modest advantage over simply providing an audiobook. In the commercial trial, encouragement had a clearer effect on the **extensive margin** — whether someone went to the gym at all during a week — than on the **intensive margin**, meaning how many total visits they made. At the ten-week follow-up, for example, encouraged participants were estimated to be 3.2 percentage points more likely to make at least one weekly visit than audiobook-only participants, while the additional effect on total weekly visits was less robust.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346246007_Teaching_temptation_bundling_to_boost_exercise_A_field_experiment" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

For practical purposes, that suggests temptation bundling may work more as a nudge across the threshold than as an engine for ever-increasing exercise. Wanting to continue an audiobook may help turn a zero-workout week into a one-workout week. The evidence is weaker for the proposition that explaining the technique makes an already-active person accumulate substantially more sessions.

## What smaller but longer-lasting gains mean

The large commercial trial supports a restrained interpretation of temptation bundling. A 10–14% relative improvement is not the sort of effect that turns an inconsistent exerciser into a devoted athlete overnight. Yet behavioural interventions aimed at repeated everyday actions do not need enormous effects to be useful, particularly when they are cheap, voluntary and easy to distribute. The Behavior Change for Good Initiative summarises the experiment as showing roughly 10–12% increases in weekly workout frequency lasting for as long as 17 weeks after the intervention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bcfg.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Six-Years-of-Impact.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bcfg.wharton.upenn.edu">[Behavior Change for Good Initiative]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bcfg.wharton.upenn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Behavior Change for Good Initiative Six Years of Impact</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Behavior Change for Good Initiative Six Years of Impact</span></span></span>

The results also put the original 51% figure into perspective. That early effect showed what a strong, externally enforced bundle *could* do under favourable conditions. The commercial trial tested something closer to what people can actually implement: unrestricted digital entertainment, ordinary gym access and a self-imposed rule. Its smaller effect is not simply disappointing replication evidence; it is evidence about the cost of moving from a controlled commitment device to a scalable personal strategy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381662/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling - PMCFebruary 1, 2014...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2014</span></span></span>

There are still limits to what can be inferred. Participants voluntarily enrolled in an exercise-boosting programme and were already commercial-gym members, so the results do not establish that the same intervention would persuade sedentary non-members to start exercising. Gym check-ins are an excellent objective measure of attendance, but they do not establish workout intensity, fitness improvements or long-term health outcomes. And because the audiobook-only condition itself appears to have suggested temptation bundling, the experiment makes it difficult to isolate a completely uncontaminated comparison between learning the technique and receiving tempting content.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

The study nevertheless resolves an important uncertainty raised by the original experiment. Temptation bundling does not appear to require researchers literally holding entertainment hostage in order to influence behaviour. People can apparently infer or adopt the pairing themselves, and a lightweight intervention can leave a measurable trace in real gym attendance months later. At the same time, removing external enforcement reduces the spectacular early effect.

For self-improvement, that is a useful calibration. The evidence supports thinking in terms of **small improvements in the probability of doing the beneficial activity**, not dramatic transformations. A well-chosen temptation can make a difficult behaviour easier to initiate, and repeated small shifts can matter. But the commercial-gym evidence gives little reason to expect temptation bundling by itself to overcome every barrier to exercise. Its real-world strength is more modest: changing the immediate attractiveness of the next workout enough that, somewhat more often, the workout actually happens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959782030385X" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>
