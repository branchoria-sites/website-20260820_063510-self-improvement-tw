## Introduction

There is no scientifically supported day on which a repeated behaviour suddenly becomes a habit. The best-known real-world dataset found something more useful: **[automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }}) tends to rise rapidly at first, then improve by progressively smaller amounts as it approaches a plateau**. In Phillippa Lally and colleagues’ study of daily eating, drinking and activity behaviours, the modelled time to reach 95% of each person’s eventual automaticity level ranged from **18 to 254 days**, with a central estimate of about **66 days**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

That 66-day figure is therefore not a deadline or universal “habit formation time”. It describes the middle of a remarkably wide distribution in one influential dataset. Later research has reinforced the larger lesson: different behaviours and different people follow very different timelines, and some continue gaining automaticity for several months.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33405284/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine-based versus time-based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - PubMedJanuary 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2021</span></span></span>

## The automaticity curve rises fast, then flattens

The landmark Lally study followed 96 volunteers who chose a new eating, drinking or [physical-activity]({{ 'physical-capacity/' | relative_url }}) behaviour and attempted it once each day in response to the same contextual cue, such as after breakfast. Participants recorded whether they performed the behaviour and rated how automatic it felt over 12 weeks. Researchers then fitted mathematical learning curves to individual participants rather than assuming everybody progressed at the same rate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

The resulting pattern was **asymptotic**. Imagine a curve that climbs steeply before gradually bending towards horizontal. Early repetitions can produce relatively large increases in automaticity. As learning accumulates, each additional repetition tends to add less. Eventually, further practice may still reinforce the behaviour, but measured automaticity changes comparatively little.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

That shape matters because it changes what “forming a habit” means. There is no obvious psychological switch between “not habitual” and “habitual”. Automaticity is strengthening along a continuum. To make the learning curve measurable, Lally and colleagues operationalised the plateau as the point at which a participant reached **95% of their modelled maximum automaticity**. It was this threshold — rather than some universal state in which a habit becomes complete — that produced the famous figure of roughly 66 days.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

Lally herself has subsequently stressed this distinction. In a 2026 University of Surrey interview, she cautioned against turning the original result into the claim that everybody forms a habit in 66 days: it was an average from that particular study, not a rule for individuals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: surrey.ac.uk">[University of Surrey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">surrey.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of SurreyDoes it really take 66 days to form a habit? We asked the expert, Dr Pippa Lally &#124; University of SurreyJanuary 21, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2026</span></span></span>

The curve also explains an experience that can otherwise be discouraging. Progress may feel obvious during the first few weeks and much less dramatic later. That slowing does not necessarily mean habit formation has stalled. It is exactly what an asymptotic learning process predicts: **large early gains followed by smaller gains as automaticity approaches its ceiling**.

## Why estimates run from weeks to many months

The striking result in the original dataset is arguably not 66 days but the **18-to-254-day range**. Even among people deliberately repeating relatively straightforward behaviours every day, the estimated time to reach 95% of maximum automaticity differed by more than seven months.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

Subsequent studies have made a single fixed number even harder to defend. In a randomised trial of 192 adults building everyday nutrition habits, participants linked their chosen behaviour either to a routine or to a particular time. Among those who successfully formed habits, the median modelled time to peak automaticity was **59 days**. Yet the reported individual range extended from **4 to 335 days**, and only 23% of participants reached the study’s specified habit-formation threshold. Repeatedly carrying out the plan was a key predictor of increasing automaticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33405284/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine-based versus time-based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - PubMedJanuary 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2021</span></span></span>

A different experiment illustrates how the behaviour and circumstances can stretch the curve. Forty-eight students attempted a daily stretching [exercise]({{ 'exercise/' | relative_url }}) for 90 days, either after waking or before bed. Extrapolated learning curves placed automaticity at about **106 days for the morning group and 154 days for the evening group**. The researchers investigated [time-of-day]({{ 'time-of-day/' | relative_url }}) physiology as a possible explanation, although a small experiment of this kind should not be treated as establishing a general rule that morning habits always form faster.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28650196/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Effects of circadian cortisol on the development of a health habit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of circadian cortisol on the development of a health habit - PubMedNovember 1, 2017...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2017</span></span></span>

The broader evidence remains limited. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis covered 20 habit-formation intervention studies involving 2,601 participants, but only **four studies directly reported a time to habit formation**. Their median estimates included 59 and 66 days, while mean estimates in other studies reached 106–154 days; individual estimates across the reviewed research ranged from 4 to 335 days. The authors also judged most included studies to have a high risk of bias.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39685110/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Time to Form a Habit: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Health Behaviour Habit Formation and Its Determinants - PubMed...</span></span></span>

So even “two months” should be understood as orientation, not prediction. The available datasets indicate why several variables can move the plateau:

* **Behavioural demands matter.** In the original work, simpler behaviours such as drinking water tended to reach peak automaticity faster than more elaborate actions such as doing 50 sit-ups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3505409/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Making health habitual: the psychology of ‘habit-formation’ and general practice - PMCDecember 1, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2012</span></span></span>
* **Repetition matters more than calendar time alone.** A day only provides an opportunity for learning; the cue–response association is strengthened when the intended response is actually performed. In the nutrition trial, repeated plan enactment predicted automaticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33405284/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine-based versus time-based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - PubMedJanuary 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 6, 2021</span></span></span>
* **Stable contexts can strengthen learning.** Research comparing habit repetitions across stable and variable contexts found greater automaticity when performance contexts were more stable, consistent with the idea that recurring situations become retrieval cues for the response.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9226889/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>
* **People differ substantially.** The extremely wide individual ranges within the same studies show that behaviour type alone cannot explain the timing differences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

This is why translating population estimates into a personal countdown is misleading. Two people can start nominally similar behaviours on the same Monday and have very different learning curves.

## Why 21 days and 66 days are both poor deadlines

The familiar claim that a habit takes 21 days to form did not emerge from a controlled habit-formation experiment. It is generally traced to plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz's observations about patients adjusting psychologically to changes in appearance, which were later popularised far beyond their original context. A modern systematic review concludes that empirical habit research does not support the 21-day rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The irony is that **66 days can become the same kind of myth if stripped of its context**. The original research was specifically estimating when self-reported automaticity approached an individually modelled ceiling. Participants did not all reach that point on day 66; estimates ranged from 18 to 254 days. Moreover, the asymptotic model fitted 62 participants, with a good fit for 39, rather than perfectly describing everyone in the original sample.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

Nor does the research imply that nothing useful happens until the plateau. Because the learning curve rises fastest near the beginning, substantial automaticity can develop well before its eventual maximum. The plateau is better understood as the mature, slow-changing end of a gradual learning process than as the first day on which the behaviour counts as a habit.

This distinction resolves an apparent contradiction in popular advice. A behaviour can **already be noticeably easier and more automatically prompted while still being months away from its modelled maximum automaticity**. Habit strength is a matter of degree.

## Better signs of progress than counting days

For someone deliberately using cue–response learning for self-improvement, elapsed time is therefore a weak progress measure by itself. The more informative question is whether initiating the behaviour is changing.

One useful sign is **reduced deliberation**. Early on, a cue such as finishing breakfast may be followed by [remembering]({{ 'remembering/' | relative_url }}) the plan, persuading yourself to act and consciously initiating the behaviour. With learning, the intended response should increasingly come to mind readily in that situation. Habit research characterises [strong habits]({{ 'strong-habits/' | relative_url }}) precisely in terms of responses being activated by contexts associated with previous performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1057740809001065" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect The habitual consumer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirect The habitual consumer</span></span></span>

A second sign is that the behaviour feels **progressively easier to initiate in its usual context**. Qualitative research reported by Lally and colleagues found that people initially experienced behaviour change as cognitively effortful but found enactment easier as automaticity developed. Changes of context could temporarily disrupt performance: weekends and holidays sometimes removed familiar work-based cues, while returning to the old context reinstated the behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.surrey.ac.uk/people/phillippa-lally" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: surrey.ac.uk">[University of Surrey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">surrey.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University of Surrey Dr Phillippa Lally &#124; University of Surrey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Surrey Dr Phillippa Lally &#124; University of Surrey</span></span></span>

A third sign is **[consistency]({{ 'consistency/' | relative_url }}) around the cue rather than perfection on a streak counter**. In Lally's original dataset, missing a single opportunity did not materially derail the subsequent automaticity curve. Learning resumed with later repetitions. That does not make repetition unimportant — consistent performance was associated with better-fitting habit-development curves — but it does mean one missed day should not be interpreted as returning to day one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

The most realistic expectation, then, is a curve rather than a countdown. Early repetitions often produce the most noticeable change; improvement then slows as automaticity matures. For some simple behaviours that process may largely level off within weeks. For other people or behaviours it can continue for several months, and some attempts may never produce a strong plateau at all. Current evidence is too heterogeneous and too limited to supply a universal finishing date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

For cue–response learning, that uncertainty is not a flaw in the strategy. It is a reason to measure the right thing. **Count repetitions if they help you remain consistent, but judge progress by whether the recurring cue increasingly prompts the response with less remembering, deciding and effort.**
