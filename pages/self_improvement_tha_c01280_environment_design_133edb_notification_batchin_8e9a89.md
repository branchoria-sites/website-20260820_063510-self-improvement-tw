## Introduction

Notification batching is a middle path between letting a phone interrupt you whenever an app chooses and turning alerts off altogether. Instead of delivering each notification immediately, the phone holds ordinary alerts and releases them at predictable times. The strongest direct experiment on this approach found that three scheduled batches a day were associated with better attention, mood, perceived productivity and control over the phone than normal continuous delivery, while hourly batching offered little benefit. Completely suppressing alerts, meanwhile, increased anxiety and fear of missing out (FoMO).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

That makes batching an unusually interesting form of [environment design]({{ 'design-around-temptation/' | relative_url }}). It does not require repeatedly deciding whether to ignore each buzz. It changes when the temptation appears. At the same time, it preserves reassurance that messages will arrive soon enough — potentially avoiding a weakness of the more severe advice to switch everything off.

## What the notification experiment actually tested

The central evidence comes from a two-week randomised field experiment by Nicholas Fitz, Kostadin Kushlev and colleagues, published in *Computers in Human Behavior* in 2019. The researchers recruited 237 participants and used a custom Android application to control when [notifications]({{ 'notifications/' | relative_url }}) became visible. Rather than simply comparing notifications with no notifications, they created four conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Participants either received notifications normally, received accumulated notifications once every hour, received them in three batches at 9am, 3pm and 9pm, or received no notification alerts at all and had to look for notifications themselves. Participants followed their assigned system for two weeks, completed daily surveys, and had aspects of their phone behaviour recorded by the research application.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://communities.springernature.com/posts/batching-notifications-can-improve-attention-well-being-and-productivity" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities.springernature.com">[Springer Nature Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities.springernature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on springernature.com.</span></span></span>

That four-way comparison matters because it tests two different ideas. One is simply **reducing the number of interruptions**. The other is **making interruptions predictable**. If either were sufficient, hourly batching should have delivered much of the benefit. It did not: apart from participants feeling less interrupted by notifications, the hourly condition generally looked similar to ordinary notification delivery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Three batches a day produced a different pattern. Compared with normal delivery, participants in that condition reported greater attentiveness and concentration, a better mood, greater perceived productivity and more control over their phones. They also unlocked their phones less frequently and felt less interrupted. Reported effect sizes included fewer phone unlocks (Cohen's *d* = −0.60), lower inattention (*d* = −0.65), greater concentration (*d* = 0.54), greater perceived control (*d* = 0.58) and greater perceived productivity (*d* = 0.57).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Omnibus-effects-of-condition-and-unadjusted-pairwise-comparisons-of-each-treatment-with_tbl2_334490090" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on researchgate.net.</span></span></span>

The result therefore does not support the loose rule that “grouping notifications is good”. **Batch frequency appears important.** Moving from an unpredictable stream to an interruption every hour may leave the basic rhythm of distraction largely intact. Moving most alerts into a few well-spaced windows changes the structure of the day much more substantially.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## Why batching can protect attention

A notification can impose an attentional cost even when you resist opening it. In a controlled experiment published in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance*, Cary Stothart and colleagues found that receiving a mobile-phone notification significantly disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task despite participants not interacting with the phone. The observed distraction was comparable in magnitude to effects associated with actively using a phone for calls or text messages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification - PubMedJune 29, 2015...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 29, 2015</span></span></span>

This makes the familiar advice to “just ignore the notification” incomplete. The decision not to respond happens **after the alert has already captured some attention**. A vibration may trigger questions about who contacted you or whether the message matters; a banner may reveal enough information to redirect thought even if it disappears seconds later.

Batching intervenes earlier. During the protected interval, there is no new alert to resist. At the scheduled delivery point, several interruptions that would otherwise have occurred independently are consolidated into one episode. The intervention therefore targets the arrival of temptation rather than demanding better resistance after every arrival.

Related workplace research strengthens the case that reducing notification-triggered interruptions can matter, although it does not establish that batching is always the best method. A 2023 field experiment involving 247 participants compared a day with communication notifications disabled against normal conditions. Reducing notification-caused interruptions benefited performance and reduced strain, with effects varying according to FoMO and “telepressure” — the felt pressure to remain responsive to messages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10244611/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Effects of task interruptions caused by notifications from communication applications on strain and performance - PMC...</span></span></span>

The useful distinction is between **phone use** and **phone-initiated interruption**. Batching does not necessarily prevent someone from voluntarily opening an app every five minutes. What it can do is reduce the number of occasions on which the device itself initiates the contest for attention.

## Attention, productivity and perceived phone control

One of the most useful findings in the Fitz study is not simply that participants said they felt better. Three-times-daily batching was also associated with fewer phone unlocks. That suggests the intervention changed at least one observable behaviour rather than producing only a subjective impression of improved focus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://communities.springernature.com/posts/batching-notifications-can-improve-attention-well-being-and-productivity" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: communities.springernature.com">[Springer Nature Communities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">communities.springernature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on springernature.com.</span></span></span>

Perceived control is also worth separating from raw screen time. A person who intentionally spends 20 minutes replying to messages at an expected time is having a different experience from someone whose attention is repeatedly redirected by alerts throughout those same hours. The batching experiment found greater perceived control in the three-times-daily group, which fits the idea that predictable delivery shifts some control over timing from applications back to the user.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

But the evidence should not be stretched into a claim that notifications explain smartphone overuse in general. A more recent preregistered randomised controlled trial of 205 participants tested a week of disabling notifications and collected objectively logged smartphone behaviour. Turning notifications off did **not** significantly reduce screen time or checking frequency, nor did it improve perceived control, productivity, distraction or several other measures of digital well-being. Participants did report weaker perceived checking habits, but they also experienced greater situational FoMO.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15213269.2024.2334025" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineFull article: Beyond the Buzz: Investigating the Effects of a Notification-Disabling Intervention on Smartphone Be...</span></span></span>

That result complicates simplistic digital-detox advice. Notifications clearly can interrupt cognition, but phone checking also arises from habit, boredom, social expectations and deliberate information seeking. Removing the external cue does not automatically remove those internal reasons to check.

The defensible claim for batching is therefore narrower: **it can reduce externally imposed interruptions while retaining periodic information delivery**. The evidence is much less secure for claims that it will dramatically reduce total screen time or solve compulsive smartphone use by itself.

## Why total notification removal can backfire

Turning everything off sounds like the logical endpoint of distraction reduction. The research repeatedly suggests that it can create a different problem: uncertainty.

In the Fitz experiment, participants who received no alerts did not show the broad benefits found with three-times-daily batching. Although they felt less interrupted, they did not gain the same improvements in concentration, inattention or perceived phone control, and they experienced greater anxiety and FoMO.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Earlier work reached a strikingly similar conclusion. In the “Do Not Disturb Challenge”, Martin Pielot and Luz Rello asked 30 volunteers to disable notification alerts across their devices for 24 hours. Participants reported feeling less distracted and more productive, but some also became anxious about missing important information, failing to respond quickly enough and becoming less connected socially.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pielot.org/2017/07/productive-anxious-lonely-24-hours-without-push-notifications/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pielot.org">[pielot.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pielot.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on pielot.org.</span></span></span>

A 2022 study using objective iPhone Screen Time data from 138 users found another potential failure mode. Participants tended to check their phones more frequently when notifications were silenced than when audio or vibration alerts were active, particularly among people high in FoMO or need to belong. The authors argued that removing the cue can leave some users uncertain about what they might be missing, prompting them to check manually.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563222001601" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Sound of silence: Does Muting Notifications Reduce Phone Use?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirect Sound of silence: Does Muting Notifications Reduce Phone Use?</span></span></span>

The larger 2024 notification-disabling trial provides an important replication of the underlying concern without reproducing every earlier result. Its intervention did not increase objective checking frequency, but it did increase situational FoMO and failed to reduce checking or screen time. In other words, switching notifications off did not reliably produce the behavioural payoff that the intuitive “no alerts, no distraction” argument predicts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15213269.2024.2334025" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineFull article: Beyond the Buzz: Investigating the Effects of a Notification-Disabling Intervention on Smartphone Be...</span></span></span>

This is where scheduled batches have a plausible advantage. They replace indefinite uncertainty with a known waiting period. A message that does not appear immediately has not vanished into an information void; it will surface at the next notification window. That mechanism is a reasonable interpretation of the experimental pattern rather than a separately proven universal effect, but it explains why batching may occupy a useful middle ground between continuous interruption and complete disconnection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## A lower-distraction setup without going unreachable

The research does not establish that everyone should copy the experimental schedule of exactly 9am, 3pm and 9pm. That schedule was a treatment condition, not a scientifically determined optimum. Nor does one relatively small two-week experiment establish three batches as the universal ideal. The participants in the principal study were recruited through an online labour market and lived in India, and notification needs differ considerably between occupations, families and social settings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

A more evidence-consistent way to apply the finding is to preserve the intervention's structure rather than treating its clock times as a prescription:

* **Keep genuinely time-critical channels immediate.** Calls from selected contacts, security alerts, transport changes or work systems where minutes genuinely matter need not wait for a batch.
* **Batch ordinary social and informational alerts.** News, social media, shopping, routine email and non-urgent group messages are stronger candidates because their value rarely depends on second-by-second delivery.
* **Use a small number of meaningful windows.** The experiment's hourly batches produced little improvement over normal delivery, while three daily batches did. The goal is not merely to make interruptions punctual; it is to create substantial periods in which the phone does not initiate them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>
* **Watch for compensatory checking.** If suppressing alerts leads to opening the notification centre or messaging apps repeatedly “just in case”, the environmental intervention is not producing the intended protected interval. Research on silent phones and disabled notifications shows that this failure mode is plausible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563222001601" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Sound of silence: Does Muting Notifications Reduce Phone Use?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirect Sound of silence: Does Muting Notifications Reduce Phone Use?</span></span></span>
* **Judge success by interruptions and control, not screen time alone.** A batching setup can be useful even if necessary communication still consumes time. The more relevant question is whether work, reading, conversation or rest is being broken into fewer phone-initiated fragments.

The policy is therefore better understood as **selective scheduling rather than digital abstinence**. It does not ask someone to become unreachable or to summon enough [self-control]({{ 'self-control/' | relative_url }}) to reject 40 separate prompts. It gives routine notifications designated opportunities to compete for attention and leaves the intervals between them quieter.

## Is batching better than turning notifications off?

For someone who wants fewer interruptions but becomes uneasy when completely disconnected, the available evidence makes batching a credible option — and arguably a better-supported compromise than indiscriminately silencing everything. Three-times-daily batching produced a favourable combination of attention, perceived productivity, mood, phone control and fewer unlocks in the main randomised field experiment, whereas complete suppression brought fewer benefits and more anxiety and FoMO.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

The conclusion needs qualification. The direct evidence for notification batching remains fairly small, and later research on notification disabling shows that notifications are only one contributor to smartphone behaviour. Removing alerts does not necessarily reduce screen time or voluntary checking, and people's responses vary with their social expectations and reasons for using their phones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/joh/article/65/1/e12408/7479297" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[Oxford Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on oup.com.</span></span></span>

That limitation is also the practical lesson. Effective environment design need not make temptation impossible. Often it works by changing **when the environment gets to ask for your attention**. Notification batching does precisely that: instead of receiving dozens of small invitations to switch tasks at somebody else's chosen moments, the phone concentrates ordinary alerts into a few predictable periods. For everyday self-improvement, that is a modest intervention — but one aimed at the distraction before the decision to resist it ever has to be made.
