## Introduction

The famous claim that it takes **66 days to form a habit** comes from a real and influential study, but “66 days” was never a universal deadline. Phillippa Lally and colleagues followed people attempting new everyday health behaviours for 12 weeks and modelled how their sense of **[automaticity]({{ 'automaticity/' | relative_url }})** changed with repetition. Among participants whose habit-growth curves could be modelled well, the estimated time to reach 95% of their eventual automaticity ranged from **18 to 254 days**, with a median of **66 days**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

That distinction changes the lesson considerably. The study did not discover a new magic number to replace the [21-day myth]({{ '21-day-myth/' | relative_url }}). It found that habit formation is a **gradual, highly variable learning process**: automaticity generally rises quickly at first, then improves by progressively smaller amounts as it approaches a plateau. The range, the shape of that curve and the study's modelling limitations are at least as important as its much-repeated midpoint.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

## How the 12-week study measured automaticity

Lally and colleagues recruited **96 volunteers** and asked each to choose a new eating, drinking or [physical-activity]({{ 'physical-capacity/' | relative_url }}) behaviour that they wanted to perform once a day in a consistent situation. An example of such a context was doing the behaviour “after breakfast”. Participants then attempted their chosen behaviour for **12 weeks, or 84 days**, recording both whether they had performed it and how automatic it felt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

This matters because the researchers were not simply counting consecutive successful days. Their central outcome was **automaticity**: the extent to which performing the behaviour came to feel less dependent on conscious deliberation or [remembering]({{ 'remembering/' | relative_url }}). They calculated this using selected items from the Self-Report Habit Index (SRHI), a questionnaire designed to measure characteristics associated with habitual behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

The researchers expected automaticity to follow an **asymptotic curve**. In plain English, that means repetition produces substantial gains early on, but each additional repetition tends to add less than the previous one. Eventually the curve approaches a personal plateau. There is therefore no obvious scientific moment when a behaviour flips overnight from “not a habit” to “habit”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

To put a number on the process, the researchers estimated when each person's curve reached **95% of its predicted plateau**, rather than waiting for some categorical “habit formed” event. Later research using the same approach has explicitly distinguished these two quantities: the time required to approach stable automaticity and the eventual level of automaticity reached. Someone can therefore develop automaticity relatively quickly without necessarily ending up with the same habit strength as somebody else.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/bjhp.12504" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[BPS PsycHub]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BPS PsycHubHabit formation following routine‐based versus time‐based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - Keller - 2021 - Britis...</span></span></span>

There is another important detail hidden by the simple “96 people, 66 days” retelling. Of the 96 volunteers, **82 provided sufficient data for analysis**. The nonlinear model could be fitted for 62 of those people, and the researchers judged it a good fit for **39**. More consistent performance was associated with better model fit. In other words, the famous timing estimate emerged from individual trajectories that behaved sufficiently like the hypothesised learning curve; the study did not observe every one of its 96 participants smoothly becoming habitual after roughly two months.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

## Why 66 days was never a universal deadline

For the well-fitting trajectories, the median estimated time to reach 95% of the automaticity plateau was **66 days**. A median is the middle observation in an ordered set, not a prescription for every person. More revealingly, the individual estimates stretched from **18 days at the low end to 254 days at the high end**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

That spread is the central finding obscured when “median of 66 days” becomes “habits take 66 days”. The slow end was more than fourteen times the fast end. Lally herself has subsequently stressed that the real message of the research was the **large variability in how long habit formation took**, rather than a universal 66-day rule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: surrey.ac.uk">[University of Surrey]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">surrey.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on surrey.ac.uk.</span></span></span>

There is also a methodological reason to interpret the longest estimates carefully. The experiment lasted only **84 days**, yet the fitted curves produced estimates extending as far as 254 days. The researchers were fitting mathematical learning curves to the observed automaticity scores and estimating when those curves would reach 95% of their asymptotes. Consequently, values beyond the 84-day observation period were **model-based projections**, not three, six or eight months of directly observed daily data. The study's abstract itself describes the procedure as nonlinear regression fitted to each participant's scores over the 84 days.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

This does not make the 254-day result meaningless. It means the correct interpretation is narrower: some participants' observed trajectories were consistent with habit development taking substantially longer than the study itself lasted. The evidence supports large variation far better than it supports treating either 66 or 254 as a precise personal forecast.

The 66-day figure is also specific to what this experiment studied: **self-selected eating, drinking and physical-activity behaviours performed daily in a repeated context**. It did not establish the formation time for every behaviour people casually call a habit, from studying and saving money to complex workplace routines. A 2024 systematic review found that even within health behaviours, relatively few studies had directly estimated time to habit formation, reinforcing the need for caution about universal numbers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## What the 18-to-254-day range changes

The range changes the practical interpretation from **“persist for 66 days”** to **“expect your own learning curve”**. At day 30, one behaviour might already be close to its eventual automaticity while another may still require substantial conscious effort. Neither outcome contradicts the original study.

The study also produced a useful finding about imperfect [consistency]({{ 'consistency/' | relative_url }}). **Missing one opportunity** to perform the target behaviour did not materially disrupt the habit-formation process. That is different from saying consistency does not matter: participants who were very inconsistent were less successful, and more consistent performance was associated with better-fitting habit-growth curves. The evidence therefore distinguishes an occasional lapse from chronic non-performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674?aff_id=24772" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

That is especially relevant to self-improvement advice built around perfect streaks. Lally's data do not support the idea that one missed day sends habit formation back to zero. The more defensible goal is repeated performance in a stable context over enough opportunities for the behaviour-context association to strengthen. UCL's contemporary explanation of the research similarly advised specifying clearly **what behaviour will be performed and in what situation**, then repeating it consistently until it begins to require less effort.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit%20" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ucl.ac.uk">[University College London]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ucl.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">University College London How long does it take to form a habit? &#124; UCL News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University College LondonHow long does it take to form a habit? &#124; UCL News - UCL – University College London...</span></span></span>

Later evidence has made the original variability look less like an anomaly. A 2021 study that also modelled individual automaticity trajectories found a median of **59 days** among people who successfully formed the targeted nutrition habit, but again found markedly different trajectories, including unsuccessful and discontinuous formation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.12504" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Habit formation following routine‐based versus time‐based cue planning: A randomized controlled trial - Keller - 2021 - British Journa...</span></span></span> A 2024 systematic review identified only four studies that directly reported [formation times]({{ 'formation-times/' | relative_url }}); across the relevant studies, individual estimates extended from **4 to 335 days**. The review concluded that the duration varies substantially between people and behaviours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

Those later findings should not be used to turn 66 into a different universal number. They strengthen the more important point first made visible by Lally's dataset: **between-person and between-behaviour variation is fundamental to the question**.

## What the study really lets you conclude

The safest reading of the 66-day study is both more modest and more useful than the popular slogan. Repeating a behaviour in a consistent context can make it progressively more automatic, but the process is gradual rather than governed by a fixed calendar threshold. In the best-fitting individual trajectories in Lally and colleagues' study, reaching 95% of the predicted automaticity plateau took a median of 66 days but ranged from 18 to 254 days.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

The study therefore supports three conclusions that the “66-day rule” tends to erase: people can progress at very different speeds; automaticity develops along a curve rather than appearing suddenly on a particular day; and an occasional missed opportunity does not automatically destroy the process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHow are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world - Lally - 2010 - European Journal of Social Psycho...</span></span></span>

For [self-improvement that works]({{ 'self-improvement-that-works/' | relative_url }}), the most defensible use of the research is not to circle day 66 on a calendar. It is to judge progress by whether a well-defined behaviour is becoming **easier to initiate and more automatic in its intended context**, while allowing enough time for a trajectory that may be considerably shorter or longer than the famous midpoint. The enduring finding of the study is not that habits take 66 days. It is that **there was no single formation time in the data**.
