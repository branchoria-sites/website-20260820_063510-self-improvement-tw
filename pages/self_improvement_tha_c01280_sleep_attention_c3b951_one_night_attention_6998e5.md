## Introduction

One short night can make attention measurably less reliable the next day, even when the person has slept rather than stayed awake all night. The clearest evidence comes from a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 44 studies in which adults were restricted to roughly two to six hours of sleep opportunity for one night. The next day, sustained-attention reaction times were slower and attentional lapses were more frequent. Subjective sleepiness increased even more strongly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1087079224000443" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of one night of sleep restriction on sleepiness and cognitive function: A systematic review and meta-analysis - Scien...</span></span></span>

The important word is **sustained**. A short night does not necessarily produce an obvious collapse across every mental ability. Instead, one of its most reproducible effects is difficulty maintaining consistently prompt attention over minutes of repetitive or monotonous activity. That makes the cost easy to underestimate: much of the time performance can still look competent, while occasional slow responses or missed signals become more common.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6879580/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Sleep deprivation, vigilant attention, and brain function: a review - PMCJune 8, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 8, 2019</span></span></span>

## How reaction times change after one restricted night

Researchers often measure this effect with the **psychomotor vigilance test (PVT)**. It is deliberately simple: a stimulus appears at unpredictable intervals and the participant responds as quickly as possible. Because there is little reasoning to do, worsening performance is difficult to explain away as failure to understand a complicated task. Researchers can examine overall response speed, the slowest responses and conspicuously delayed responses — commonly those taking more than 500 milliseconds — classified as lapses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6879580/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Sleep deprivation, vigilant attention, and brain function: a review - PMCJune 8, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 8, 2019</span></span></span>

Across the 44 studies included in the 2024 meta-analysis, a single restricted night produced a standardised mean difference of **0.512 for slower reaction times on sustained-attention tasks** and **0.489 for attentional lapses**. Both effects were statistically significant. Subjective sleepiness increased more strongly, with a standardised mean difference of 0.986. These standardised effect sizes describe average differences across studies rather than a prediction that every person will slow by a particular number of milliseconds.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1087079224000443" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of one night of sleep restriction on sleepiness and cognitive function: A systematic review and meta-analysis - Scien...</span></span></span>

A controlled experiment provides a more concrete illustration. In one study, 24 participants averaged about **7.8 hours of sleep in the normal-sleep condition and 5.0 hours after [sleep restriction]({{ 'mental-habits/' | relative_url }})**. Following the shorter night, overall PVT performance slowed, the slowest 10% of responses deteriorated, and lapses increased significantly. Yet another measure of brief, transient attention in the same experiment — the “attentional blink” — showed no significant effect of sleep restriction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7204874/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)The impact of sleep loss on sustained and transient attention: an EEG study - PMC...</span></span></span>

A different experiment involving 61 healthy adults aged 20–29 compared normal sleep with either four hours in bed or a night containing four deliberately prolonged awakenings. Both disrupted conditions increased omission and commission errors on a sustained-attention task. The finding is useful because it shows that next-day attention can be vulnerable not only when the sleep window is drastically shortened, but also when a night's sleep is substantially interrupted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389945714001579" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of one night of induced night-wakings versus sleep restriction on sustained attention and mood: a pilot study - Scie...</span></span></span>

The evidence nevertheless does **not** support a precise one-night dose formula. In the 2024 synthesis, sustained-attention impairment was not significantly associated with sleep duration across the two-to-six-hour restriction range. That does not mean that two hours and six hours of sleep are equivalent. It means these studies did not establish a sufficiently clean relationship to justify predicting next-day impairment from sleep duration alone. Differences in protocols, testing times and individuals make the response more complicated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38759474/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of one night of sleep restriction on sleepiness and cognitive function: A systematic review and meta-analysis - PubMed...</span></span></span>

## Why lapses matter more than average speed


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

An average reaction time can hide the characteristic problem created by insufficient sleep. Sleep-loss research has long described **state instability**: instead of every response becoming uniformly slower, normal-looking responses are increasingly mixed with unusually slow responses and failures to respond promptly. In other words, performance becomes less dependable from one moment to the next.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6879580/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Sleep deprivation, vigilant attention, and brain function: a review - PMCJune 8, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 8, 2019</span></span></span>

</div>

That distinction changes how a short night's consequences should be interpreted. Imagine that most responses during a monotonous task remain reasonably fast but a handful become dramatically delayed. The average may move only modestly, yet those rare failures could be the responses that matter most if the task involves spotting a road hazard, noticing an alarm, monitoring equipment or catching an error before acting.

This is one reason vigilance tests examine the tail of the reaction-time distribution rather than reporting only a mean. In the five-hour-sleep experiment, for example, researchers found deterioration not just in overall PVT speed but specifically in the **slowest 10% of responses** and in the number of lapses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7204874/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)The impact of sleep loss on sustained and transient attention: an EEG study - PMC...</span></span></span>

The broader sleep-loss literature reinforces this interpretation. Reviews describe vigilant-attention impairment as increased moment-to-moment variability, with exceptionally slow responses interspersed among faster ones. Experiments involving more severe sleep deprivation likewise find that both average reaction time and within-person variability rise as sleep pressure increases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6879580/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Sleep deprivation, vigilant attention, and brain function: a review - PMCJune 8, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 8, 2019</span></span></span>

For self-improvement, this makes a short night particularly relevant to tasks that demand **[consistency]({{ 'consistency/' | relative_url }}) rather than occasional peak performance**. Being capable of a fast response does not demonstrate that attention is fully restored; the problem is that the fast response may not arrive reliably on every occasion. A person may therefore have a productive-looking morning while still being more vulnerable to intermittent attention failures.

The same distinction helps explain why monotonous activities deserve particular caution. Driving research has used sustained-attention measures precisely because sleepiness and prolonged, repetitive performance can interact. In a counterbalanced study of 47 healthy adults, researchers compared normal sleep with a four-hour sleep opportunity and assessed participants using two sustained-attention tasks alongside a computer-based fitness-to-drive battery and subjective and physiological measures of sleepiness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26115651/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The effect of partial sleep deprivation on computer-based measures of fitness to drive - PubMed...</span></span></span>

Not every driving-related measure necessarily deteriorates after one restricted night. Another simulator experiment found clear increases in subjective sleepiness after four hours of sleep but more limited effects attributable specifically to sleep restriction across the driving-performance measures examined. Such findings are a useful restraint on overstatement: laboratory evidence strongly supports reduced vigilance, but it should not be translated into the claim that every complex real-world performance measure deteriorates by the same amount.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15885247/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## Which mental abilities may remain relatively intact

Perhaps the most useful finding from the one-night literature is its **selectivity**. The 2024 meta-analysis examined sustained attention alongside choice reaction time, cognitive throughput, working memory and inhibitory control. It detected significant impairment in sustained-attention speed and lapses but did **not** find statistically significant overall effects for those other cognitive domains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1087079224000443" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of one night of sleep restriction on sleepiness and cognitive function: A systematic review and meta-analysis - Scien...</span></span></span>

This does not prove that working memory or inhibitory control are immune to one short night. Individual experiments can produce different results, tasks vary in sensitivity, and other meta-analyses covering broader or repeated sleep-restriction protocols have found effects extending beyond vigilance. A previous meta-analysis of 61 studies, for example, found adverse effects of short-term sleep restriction across several areas, with sustained attention among the domains showing a significant deficit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763417301641" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sciencedirect.com.</span></span></span>

But for the specific question of **one curtailed night**, the newer synthesis gives a more tightly bounded answer. Vigilance is one of the clearest places to expect a detectable next-day cost. Someone can therefore perform adequately on a brief reasoning, memory or choice task and still have impaired ability to maintain attention reliably over time. The apparent contradiction — “I can still think, so the short night did not affect me” — disappears once different cognitive abilities are separated.

Individual studies also show why sweeping claims are inappropriate. A review of sleep restriction in highly trained or occupationally skilled populations found studies in which vigilance worsened after a restricted night alongside studies in which particular occupation-specific or cognitive measures did not show significant changes. Task demands, testing time and experimental design all influence whether an impairment becomes visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8271199/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## What the next day actually looks like

The evidence supports a narrower and more useful expectation than “sleep deprivation makes you stupid”. After one substantially shortened night, the next day is better characterised as a period in which **alertness is lower and sustained attention is more fragile**. Responses tend to slow, unusually delayed responses become more frequent, and subjective sleepiness rises. Meanwhile, some other tested abilities may remain sufficiently intact that the person still feels capable of normal work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38759474/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of one night of sleep restriction on sleepiness and cognitive function: A systematic review and meta-analysis - PubMed...</span></span></span>

That combination is precisely why a single short night can be deceptive. A person does not need to feel incapable of thinking in order to be less dependable at maintaining attention. Nor does one successful burst of concentrated work demonstrate that vigilance has returned to baseline. The laboratory signature is probabilistic: the risk of a slow or missed response has increased.

For self-improvement, the practical lesson is correspondingly specific. After an unusually short night, it is sensible to distinguish tasks where occasional attention failures have little consequence from those where **one lapse matters disproportionately**. Routine planning or a brief conversation is not cognitively equivalent to prolonged driving, safety monitoring or another repetitive task in which a single missed event can carry a high cost. The strongest one-night evidence concerns that ability to stay continuously responsive, not a universal loss of intelligence or competence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6879580/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Sleep deprivation, vigilant attention, and brain function: a review - PMCJune 8, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 8, 2019</span></span></span>

A short night, then, should not be judged solely by whether you can still perform well for a few minutes. The more revealing question is whether you can keep doing so reliably. Controlled studies indicate that after only one curtailed night, that reliability is already one of the first cognitive qualities to weaken.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1087079224000443" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of one night of sleep restriction on sleepiness and cognitive function: A systematic review and meta-analysis - Scien...</span></span></span>
