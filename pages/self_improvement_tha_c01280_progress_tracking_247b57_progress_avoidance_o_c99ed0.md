## Introduction

Progress tracking has an awkward weakness: the moment [feedback]({{ 'feedback/' | relative_url }}) becomes most useful can also be the moment people least want to see it. When spending is running over budget, workouts have been missed or a project is slipping, checking the numbers can turn a suspected shortfall into an undeniable one. Avoiding the tracker offers immediate emotional relief, but it also removes information that could trigger corrective action.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-illustration"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_progress_tracking_247b57_progress_avoidance_o_c99ed0-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Progress Avoidance illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_progress_tracking_247b57_progress_avoidance_o_c99ed0-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/self_improvement_tha_c01280_progress_tracking_247b57_progress_avoidance_o_c99ed0-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Explanatory illustration 1</span></figcaption></figure>

Researchers call this the **“ostrich problem”**: deliberately avoiding, delaying or discounting available information about progress towards a goal. It is not simply forgetting to track. The distinctive problem is motivated avoidance — not looking because the answer may be uncomfortable, inconvenient or demanding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12071" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOI‘The Ostrich Problem’: Motivated Avoidance or Rejection of Information About Goal Progress - Webb - 2013 - Social and Personality Psyc...</span></span></span>

This creates an important qualification to the advice to “track your progress”. Tracking only improves self-regulation when the feedback loop remains open during bad weeks as well as good ones. The practical challenge is therefore not merely making progress measurable, but making disappointing measurements tolerable and useful enough that people continue to look.

## The ostrich problem in goal monitoring

Imagine someone who has set a monthly spending limit. Halfway through the month, they suspect they have overspent. Opening the banking app could clarify the situation while there is still time to cancel purchases or reduce discretionary spending. Yet precisely because the balance might confirm a problem, postponing the check can feel attractive.

Thomas Webb, Betty Chang and Yael Benn used the “ostrich problem” to describe this broader failure of goal monitoring. Their review distinguishes active monitoring — deliberately seeking information such as checking an account — from information that becomes apparent without deliberate effort. Avoidance can occur at either stage: a person can decline to obtain the information or receive it and then discount, ignore or reinterpret what it means.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12071" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOI‘The Ostrich Problem’: Motivated Avoidance or Rejection of Information About Goal Progress - Webb - 2013 - Social and Personality Psyc...</span></span></span>

That distinction separates progress avoidance from ordinary measurement problems. Someone who cannot obtain reliable information is not necessarily avoiding it. Nor is every imperfect metric evidence of avoidance. But if a student counts pages read because testing their actual understanding would expose weak progress, an apparently respectable measure can become a substitute that protects them from the more diagnostic one. Webb and colleagues explicitly identify this sort of motivated substitution as a possible form of the ostrich problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/spc3.12071" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Webb - 2013 - Social and Personality Psychology Compass - Wiley Online Library...</span></span></span>

The risk matters because progress monitoring is not just record-keeping. A large meta-analysis covering 138 randomised studies and 19,951 participants found that interventions designed to increase monitoring substantially increased monitoring frequency and produced a smaller but meaningful improvement in goal attainment; increases in monitoring statistically mediated improvements in attainment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span> Avoiding feedback therefore interrupts a mechanism that can otherwise help translate intentions into action.

## Why uncomfortable feedback is easy to avoid

The simplest explanation is also an important one: **not knowing can feel better right now**. Information avoidance has been defined more broadly as behaviour intended to prevent or delay acquiring available but potentially unwanted information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1037/a0021288" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sage Journals Information Avoidance: Who, What, When, and Why</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsInformation Avoidance: Who, What, When, and Why - Kate Sweeny, Darya Melnyk, Wendi Miller, James A. Shepperd, 2010...</span></span></span> Goal tracking creates a particularly clear version of that conflict because the information often carries an evaluation: actual behaviour is being compared with something the person wanted, promised or expected themselves to do.

The ostrich-problem framework describes competing motives. Accurate feedback serves self-assessment and self-improvement: knowing that you are behind can help you change course. But the same information may threaten self-enhancement or conflict with an existing view of yourself. Someone can therefore simultaneously want an accurate answer for tomorrow's benefit and want to avoid that answer for today's comfort.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/spc3.12071" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Webb - 2013 - Social and Personality Psychology Compass - Wiley Online Library...</span></span></span>

Later empirical work made those motives more concrete. In one study, participants recalled occasions when they had avoided monitoring personal goals. The most strongly endorsed explanations included believing that the information would require an unwanted action or change of beliefs, knowing that progress was poor, and experiencing negative emotions when thinking about the goal or working towards it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00152/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; Why Do People Act Like the Proverbial Ostrich? Investigating the Reasons That People Provide for Not Monitoring Thei...</span></span></span>

This helps explain why avoidance can intensify when a goal is going badly. A progress check does not merely say, “you are behind”. It can imply, “your current approach is not working”, “you need to do something difficult”, or “your earlier assumptions were wrong”. Ignorance temporarily postpones all three conclusions.

There is another wrinkle. People can rationalise avoidance in apparently practical terms. In the prospective second study by Webb and colleagues, declining an opportunity to monitor progress was associated with doubts about the accuracy and usefulness of the available information; perceived control over goal attainment also predicted avoidance. The reasons people retrospectively *said* were important were therefore not identical to the factors that best predicted subsequent monitoring behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00152/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; Why Do People Act Like the Proverbial Ostrich? Investigating the Reasons That People Provide for Not Monitoring Thei...</span></span></span>

That makes the ostrich problem difficult to recognise in oneself. “I don't want to know because I'm behind” may instead become “that number doesn't tell me much anyway”. Sometimes that criticism will be legitimate. The useful question is whether the metric really has stopped informing decisions or whether its apparent uselessness increases precisely when it begins delivering unwelcome news.

## Avoidance protects mood by sacrificing feedback

Progress avoidance is not irrational in the narrow sense that it has no benefit. Its benefit is simply concentrated in the present. Not checking can postpone disappointment, embarrassment, anxiety or the obligation to make a difficult decision. The cost arrives later.

This is why information avoidance is best understood as a trade-off rather than as simple laziness. Research on the ostrich problem proposes that people can face a [self-control]({{ 'self-control/' | relative_url }}) conflict between proximal concerns — avoiding the unpleasant implications of negative feedback — and the more distant objective of improving performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/spc3.12071" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Webb - 2013 - Social and Personality Psychology Compass - Wiley Online Library...</span></span></span> Recent research in health-information avoidance also supports a broader defensive interpretation: across two studies involving five samples and 1,850 participants, avoidance and derogation of unwanted information behaved as related self-protective strategies. If uncomfortable information was not avoided, people could instead defend themselves by discounting it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39013284/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Information avoidance: An interchangeable strategy of self-protection</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Information avoidance: An interchangeable strategy of self-protection - PubMedJune 28, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 28, 2024</span></span></span>

For goal tracking, that suggests two ways the feedback loop can fail. The obvious one is **not checking**. The less obvious one is **checking without accepting**: repeatedly finding reasons why every disappointing result should not count. A useful monitoring system has to survive both.

There is nevertheless an important caution against treating all discomfort as something to push through. Monitoring itself can sometimes have psychological costs. Research on self-weighing illustrates the point particularly well. A meta-analysis of 29 independent tests involving 10,775 participants found little overall association between self-weighing and affect, body-related attitudes or disordered eating, but a small negative association with psychological functioning; effects also differed between people and circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26742706/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med What is the psychological impact of self-weighing? A meta-analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med What is the psychological impact of self-weighing? A meta-analysis</span></span></span>

The lesson is not that unpleasant feedback should always be confronted more aggressively. It is that a tracking system should produce **decision-relevant information without turning every measurement into a verdict on the person**. When a metric is genuinely harmful, misleading or inappropriate, changing it can be sensible. When the metric is useful but merely unwelcome because performance has slipped, abandoning it removes the very signal needed for recovery.

## Keeping missed targets visible enough to act on

The central design problem is therefore to reduce the opportunity for a bad result to end the monitoring habit. A tracker that works beautifully during successful weeks but disappears during unsuccessful ones gives a distorted picture of progress.

One useful principle is to make the **checking rule independent of the result**. Rather than deciding whether to review progress according to how things feel, decide in advance: check spending every Friday; review completed study sessions each Sunday; record planned versus completed workouts at the end of each week. The point is not constant surveillance. It is to prevent anticipated bad news from determining whether the measurement happens at all.

A second principle is to track information that leads naturally to a response. The ostrich-problem research suggests that people avoid information partly when it seems inaccurate or useless.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00152/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersFrontiers &#124; Why Do People Act Like the Proverbial Ostrich? Investigating the Reasons That People Provide for Not Monitoring Thei...</span></span></span> A metric should therefore make the next decision easier. “I am failing at fitness” is an emotionally loaded judgement. “One of three planned runs happened this week” is narrower, observable and capable of prompting a specific adjustment.

A practical review can consequently separate three things that are easily collapsed into one:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **What happened?** Record the result without explanation: two planned sessions were missed.
* **What made the plan fail?** Identify the obstacle rather than converting the shortfall into a judgement of character.
* **What changes next?** Reduce the target, alter the timing, remove friction or make another concrete adjustment.

</div>

This structure matters because negative feedback is valuable principally for what it enables next. Webb and colleagues argue that avoiding progress information makes it harder both to recognise the need for action and to identify an appropriate response.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/spc3.12071" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Webb - 2013 - Social and Personality Psychology Compass - Wiley Online Library...</span></span></span> A tracker that displays failure but offers no route towards adaptation preserves the unpleasant part of feedback while weakening its practical benefit.

Keeping a physical or persistent record can also make selective memory harder. The broader experimental evidence on progress monitoring found larger effects on goal attainment when information was physically recorded and when outcomes were reported or made public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence - PubMedFebruary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 1, 2016</span></span></span> That does not mean every private goal needs public accountability. It does suggest that a durable record has advantages over repeatedly asking oneself, “How do I think I'm doing?”

## The tracker must work on bad weeks

The most revealing test of a progress system is not whether someone enjoys using it while ahead. It is whether the system still produces usable information after several missed targets.

That changes what “good tracking” means. A sophisticated dashboard that someone stops opening when results deteriorate is less useful than a simple record they continue to review. Likewise, increasing the number of metrics can backfire if it creates more opportunities for judgement, rationalisation or avoidance. The aim is not maximum measurement; it is a sufficiently reliable feedback channel between behaviour and the next decision.

Research on monitoring across different kinds of personal goals reinforces the importance of making progress legible. Studies involving interviews and two online samples found that people reported monitoring more readily when goals were represented in quantifiable terms and when they felt confident about attaining them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28392775/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Which Factors Are Associated with Monitoring Goal Progress?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Which Factors Are Associated with Monitoring Goal Progress?</span></span></span> The second finding is particularly revealing: monitoring is easier when the expected news is good, which is exactly why a useful system needs protection against disappearing when confidence falls.

There is also a limit to what the evidence currently establishes. The ostrich-problem literature provides a persuasive framework, observational patterns and experimental work on reasons for avoidance, but it does not establish one universally effective anti-avoidance technique for every kind of personal goal. Even the European Commission's summary of the research programme describes the work as identifying why monitoring fails and potential directions for interventions rather than supplying a single settled solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/280515/reporting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cordis.europa.eu">[CORDIS]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cordis.europa.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">nge interventions &#124; FP7 &#124; CORDIS &#124; European Commission...</span></span></span>

The safest practical inference is narrower: **do not let anticipated performance determine whether you obtain performance information**. Make checks regular rather than mood-dependent, keep the metric closely connected to controllable behaviour, distinguish the result from self-judgement, and make every shortfall point towards a decision. Progress tracking earns its place in self-improvement not by making failure pleasant, but by keeping failure visible early enough to do something about it.
